Passage

Acts 21

Verses 1–16 · WEB

Paul’s company sails from Miletus to Tyre, where disciples, speaking through the Spirit, urge him not to go to Jerusalem; after a week they kneel and pray on the beach and part. At Caesarea they stay with Philip the evangelist, whose four daughters prophesy. The prophet Agabus binds his own hands and feet with Paul’s belt and foretells that the Jews will bind Paul and hand him over to the Gentiles. Everyone begs Paul not to go, but he declares he is ready to be bound and even to die for the name of the Lord Jesus, and they fall silent, saying, “The Lord’s will be done.”

Acts 21:1

1When we had departed from them and had set sail, we came with a straight course to Cos, and the next day to Rhodes, and from there to Patara.

Arminian

The “we” of the narrative returns: Luke is aboard as the ship runs from Miletus past Cos and Rhodes to Patara. Tearing away from the Ephesian elders, whom Paul had just told they would not see him again, is described as a wrench.

Reformed

Each port is named in order, the kind of log a fellow traveler keeps. The steady itinerary carries the reader toward Jerusalem, the city Paul said in 20:22 he was going to “bound by the Spirit.”

Acts 21:2

2Having found a ship crossing over to Phoenicia, we went aboard and set sail.

Arminian

At Patara they switch to a cargo ship crossing open water to Phoenicia, a faster route that suited Paul’s hope of reaching Jerusalem by Pentecost (20:16).

Reformed

Nothing supernatural happens here; Paul simply books passage. Luke lets ordinary travel arrangements carry the plan forward.

Acts 21:3

3When we had come in sight of Cyprus, leaving it on the left hand, we sailed to Syria and landed at Tyre, for the ship was there to unload her cargo.

Arminian

Cyprus passes on the left, the island where Paul and Barnabas began their first journey (13:4). The ship unloads its cargo at Tyre, giving Paul an unplanned week ashore.

Reformed

Tyre had a church Paul did not found, likely planted by believers scattered after Stephen’s death (11:19). The mission has already outrun its best-known missionary.

Acts 21:4

4Having found disciples, we stayed there seven days. These said to Paul through the Spirit that he should not go up to Jerusalem.

Arminian

The Tyrian disciples, “through the Spirit,” urge Paul not to go up to Jerusalem. Many Arminian interpreters take this to mean the Spirit revealed the danger and the disciples drew the conclusion that he should avoid it, leaving Paul responsible to weigh their counsel and choose.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters usually distinguish what the Spirit revealed, that suffering awaited Paul, from the human inference that he should stay away. The Spirit does not contradict himself: in 20:22–23 he had already bound Paul to go.

Acts 21:5

5When those days were over, we departed and went on our journey. They all, with wives and children, brought us on our way until we were out of the city. Kneeling down on the beach, we prayed.

Arminian

Whole families walk Paul to the shore, and the farewell ends with men, women, and children kneeling on the beach to pray.

Reformed

Luke records children in the scene, a small detail that shows households, not only individual adults, making up these early congregations.

Acts 21:6

6After saying goodbye to each other, we went on board the ship, and they returned home again.

Arminian

After goodbyes they board, and the Tyrian believers go home. A week was enough to form a bond that ends in tears and prayer.

Reformed

The scene mirrors the parting at Miletus in 20:36–38. Luke repeats the pattern so the reader feels the weight of each farewell on the way to Jerusalem.

Acts 21:7

7When we had finished the voyage from Tyre, we arrived at Ptolemais. We greeted the brothers and stayed with them one day.

Arminian

A one-day stop at Ptolemais, modern Acre, includes a visit with believers there. Another coastal town already has a Christian community.

Reformed

Luke mentions the brothers at Ptolemais only in passing, which shows how ordinary such churches had become along the coast.

Acts 21:8

8On the next day, we who were Paul’s companions departed and came to Caesarea. We entered into the house of Philip the evangelist, who was one of the seven, and stayed with him.

Arminian

At Caesarea they stay with Philip, “one of the seven” from 6:5 and the evangelist of Samaria and the Ethiopian official in chapter 8. Paul, who once drove Philip’s friends out of Jerusalem, now lodges in his home.

Reformed

The title “evangelist” appears here and in Ephesians 4:11 and 2 Timothy 4:5. Reformed writers have often treated it as a distinct gift of gospel proclamation within the church.

Acts 21:9

9Now this man had four virgin daughters who prophesied.

Arminian

Philip has four unmarried daughters who prophesy. Wesleyan and Holiness churches have long cited them, along with Joel 2:28, in support of women’s public ministry.

Reformed

Reformed churches agree that the Spirit gave these women a genuine prophetic gift. Many distinguish this gift from the governing office of elder, while some Reformed writers read the verse more broadly.

Acts 21:10

10As we stayed there some days, a certain prophet named Agabus came down from Judea.

Arminian

Agabus, who earlier foretold the famine under Claudius (11:28), arrives from Judea. His track record gives his next word weight.

Reformed

Luke introduces Agabus as if new, though he has appeared before, perhaps because this section comes from Luke’s own travel notes.

Acts 21:11

11Coming to us and taking Paul’s belt, he bound his own feet and hands, and said, “The Holy Spirit says: ‘So the Jews at Jerusalem will bind the man who owns this belt, and will deliver him into the hands of the Gentiles.’”

Arminian

Agabus ties his own hands and feet with Paul’s belt, an acted parable in the tradition of Jeremiah and Ezekiel, and says the Jews in Jerusalem will bind Paul and hand him to the Gentiles.

Reformed

His words echo Jesus’ prediction that he would be handed over to the Gentiles (Luke 18:32). Luke is shaping Paul’s road to Jerusalem to resemble his Lord’s.

Acts 21:12

12When we heard these things, both we and the people of that place begged him not to go up to Jerusalem.

Arminian

Even Luke and the traveling companions join the local believers in begging Paul not to go. Love for him blinds them, for a moment, to what the Spirit had said.

Reformed

Their plea recalls Peter’s rebuke of Jesus when he foretold his suffering (Matthew 16:22). The people closest to God’s servant can be the ones who try hardest to turn him aside.

Acts 21:13

13Then Paul answered, “What are you doing, weeping and breaking my heart? For I am ready not only to be bound, but also to die at Jerusalem for the name of the Lord Jesus.”

Arminian

Paul asks why they are breaking his heart: he is ready not only to be bound but to die in Jerusalem for the name of the Lord Jesus. Arminian writers find here a man who has counted the cost and freely embraces it.

Reformed

Reformed writers stress that Paul’s readiness rests on the Spirit’s prior binding (20:22). His resolve is the response of a servant who knows his course has been set by his Lord.

Acts 21:14

14When he would not be persuaded, we ceased, saying, “The Lord’s will be done.”

Arminian

When Paul will not be persuaded, they stop arguing and say, “The Lord’s will be done.” Their surrender is a choice to trust God with someone they love.

Reformed

The words echo Jesus in Gethsemane (Luke 22:42). The church yields to God’s will even when it means suffering, which Reformed theology treats as a central mark of trust in providence.

Acts 21:15

15After these days we took up our baggage and went up to Jerusalem.

Arminian

They pack and set out on the roughly sixty-mile journey up to Jerusalem.

Reformed

Luke’s plain sentence marks the end of a long approach. The city that killed Jesus and Stephen now receives Paul.

Acts 21:16

16Some of the disciples from Caesarea also went with us, bringing one Mnason of Cyprus, an early disciple, with whom we would stay.

Arminian

Caesarean disciples escort them and arrange lodging with Mnason of Cyprus, a disciple from the earliest days. Hosting a man rumored to oppose the law took courage.

Reformed

Mnason is likely a Hellenistic Jewish believer, a natural host for a mixed group that included Gentiles such as Trophimus (21:29).

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
