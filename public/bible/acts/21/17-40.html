Passage

Acts 21

Verses 17–40 · WEB

The Jerusalem believers welcome Paul, and James and the elders glorify God for his report about the Gentiles. They warn him that thousands of law-observant Jewish believers have heard he teaches Jews to abandon Moses, and they advise him to join four men completing a vow to show he keeps the law. Near the end of the seven days, Jews from Asia see him in the temple, stir up the crowd, and falsely claim he brought Greeks inside. The mob drags Paul out and tries to kill him until the Roman commander intervenes, arrests him, and, after learning he is a citizen of Tarsus, permits him to address the people from the barracks steps.

Acts 21:17

17When we had come to Jerusalem, the brothers received us gladly.

Arminian

The brothers in Jerusalem receive them gladly. The first welcome is warm, whatever tensions surface the next day.

Reformed

Luke records the welcome before the trouble, perhaps to show that the Jerusalem church as a whole stood with Paul.

Acts 21:18

18The day following, Paul went in with us to James; and all the elders were present.

Arminian

Paul visits James, the Lord’s brother, with all the elders present. Peter is no longer mentioned; James now leads the Jerusalem church.

Reformed

The scene shows the church governed by James and a body of elders, a pattern of plural leadership that Reformed church order has drawn on.

Acts 21:19

19When he had greeted them, he reported one by one the things which God had worked among the Gentiles through his ministry.

Arminian

Paul reports “one by one” what God had done among the Gentiles through his ministry. He presents his work as God’s doing, not his own achievement.

Reformed

Luke consistently names God as the actor in the Gentile mission (14:27; 15:4, 12), and Paul does the same here.

Acts 21:20

20They, when they heard it, glorified God. They said to him, “You see, brother, how many thousands there are among the Jews of those who have believed, and they are all zealous for the law.

Arminian

The elders glorify God, then mention the “many thousands” of Jewish believers who are all zealous for the law. The church in Jerusalem had grown enormously and remained thoroughly Jewish in practice.

Reformed

Zeal for the law and faith in Jesus sit side by side here. The leaders do not condemn this; they look for a way to keep Jewish and Gentile believers in one fellowship.

Acts 21:21

21They have been informed about you, that you teach all the Jews who are among the Gentiles to forsake Moses, telling them not to circumcise their children and not to walk after the customs.

Arminian

The rumor is that Paul teaches diaspora Jews to abandon Moses, stop circumcising their children, and leave the customs. Paul’s letters show he taught that circumcision does not justify, but he did not forbid Jewish believers to keep it.

Reformed

The rumor confuses justification with practice. Paul denied that circumcision contributes to a person’s standing before God (Galatians 5:2–6), while allowing Jewish customs as matters of conscience (1 Corinthians 7:18).

Acts 21:22

22What then? The assembly must certainly meet, for they will hear that you have come.

Arminian

“What then?” The elders foresee that word of Paul’s arrival will spread quickly and they want to act before it does.

Reformed

Their concern is pastoral rather than doctrinal. The issue is how a crowd of believers will receive a man they have heard misrepresented.

Acts 21:23

23Therefore do what we tell you. We have four men who have taken a vow.

Arminian

They propose that Paul join four men completing a Nazirite vow, a public act of temple devotion that would contradict the rumor.

Reformed

Numbers 6 describes the vow and its closing offerings. Sponsoring others in it was a recognized act of piety among Jews.

Acts 21:24

24Take them and purify yourself with them, and pay their expenses for them, that they may shave their heads. Then all will know that there is no truth in the things that they have been informed about you, but that you yourself also walk keeping the law.

Arminian

Paul is to purify himself with them and pay for their sacrifices, so that everyone will know he still lives in observance of the law.

Reformed

This fits Paul’s stated principle of becoming “as a Jew” to win Jews (1 Corinthians 9:20). Reformed writers see Christian liberty at work: he can keep the custom without treating it as necessary for salvation.

Acts 21:25

25But concerning the Gentiles who believe, we have written our decision that they should observe no such thing, except that they should keep themselves from food offered to idols, from blood, from strangled things, and from sexual immorality.”

Arminian

The elders reaffirm the council’s letter to Gentile believers (15:29). Nothing in this plan puts the law back on Gentiles.

Reformed

The reminder guards the gospel. What Jewish believers may choose to keep is never made a requirement for Gentiles.

Acts 21:26

26Then Paul took the men, and the next day purified himself and went with them into the temple, declaring the fulfillment of the days of purification, until the offering was offered for every one of them.

Arminian

Paul agrees, purifies himself the next day, and announces in the temple when the days of purification will end. He submits to the elders’ counsel for the sake of peace.

Reformed

Some commentators question whether Paul compromised. Most Reformed writers answer that he did nothing against conscience or the gospel, since the vow was not offered as a ground of justification.

Acts 21:27

27When the seven days were almost completed, the Jews from Asia, when they saw him in the temple, stirred up all the multitude and laid hands on him,

Arminian

Near the end of the seven days, Jews from Asia, likely from Ephesus where Paul had spent three years, recognize him in the temple and stir up the crowd.

Reformed

The very act meant to calm the rumors becomes the setting for Paul’s arrest. Agabus’s word in verse 11 begins to come true through a plan made in good faith.

Acts 21:28

28crying out, “Men of Israel, help! This is the man who teaches all men everywhere against the people, and the law, and this place. Moreover, he also brought Greeks into the temple and has defiled this holy place!”

Arminian

They shout that Paul teaches everyone everywhere against the people, the law, and the temple, and has now defiled the temple by bringing Greeks inside. Gentiles who passed the inner barrier faced death under Jewish law, which Rome allowed.

Reformed

The charges echo those against Stephen (6:13). Luke shows the same accusations following the same message from one witness to the next.

Acts 21:29

29For they had seen Trophimus the Ephesian with him in the city, and they supposed that Paul had brought him into the temple.

Arminian

Luke explains that they had seen Trophimus the Ephesian with Paul in the city and assumed Paul had taken him into the temple. A guess became an accusation, and the accusation became a riot.

Reformed

Trophimus was one of the delegates carrying the Gentile churches’ gift (20:4). The collection meant to unite Jew and Gentile becomes the occasion of a false charge.

Acts 21:30

30All the city was moved and the people ran together. They seized Paul and dragged him out of the temple. Immediately the doors were shut.

Arminian

The whole city is roused, people run together, and they drag Paul out of the temple. The doors are shut at once.

Reformed

Luke’s mention of the shut doors is pointed: the temple closes on the apostle as he is cast out, much as Jesus was taken outside the city.

Acts 21:31

31As they were trying to kill him, news came up to the commanding officer of the regiment that all Jerusalem was in an uproar.

Arminian

While they are trying to kill him, word reaches the commander of the Roman cohort stationed in the Antonia Fortress overlooking the temple courts.

Reformed

Rome’s garrison was positioned to watch for exactly this kind of disturbance at festival time. Paul’s rescue comes through an ordinary military routine.

Acts 21:32

32Immediately he took soldiers and centurions and ran down to them. They, when they saw the chief captain and the soldiers, stopped beating Paul.

Arminian

The commander runs down with soldiers and centurions, at least two hundred men, and the beating stops when the crowd sees them.

Reformed

The pagan army protects the apostle from the religious crowd. Throughout Acts, Roman officials more often shield Paul than threaten him.

Acts 21:33

33Then the commanding officer came near, arrested him, commanded him to be bound with two chains, and inquired who he was and what he had done.

Arminian

The commander arrests Paul and orders him bound with two chains, then asks who he is and what he has done. Agabus’s prophecy is fulfilled, though Gentile hands, not Jewish ones, actually bind him.

Reformed

Luke records the fulfillment without comment. The Spirit’s word through Agabus proves true in substance even if the details are loosely matched.

Acts 21:34

34Some shouted one thing and some another, among the crowd. When he couldn’t find out the truth because of the noise, he commanded him to be brought into the barracks.

Arminian

The crowd shouts contradictory things, so the commander cannot learn the facts and orders Paul taken into the barracks.

Reformed

The confusion recalls the Ephesian riot (19:32). Mobs rarely know their own reasons, and truth gets lost in the noise.

Acts 21:35

35When he came to the stairs, he was carried by the soldiers because of the violence of the crowd;

Arminian

At the stairs the soldiers have to carry Paul because the crowd is so violent.

Reformed

Luke gives a vivid eyewitness picture: the apostle lifted above the mob on soldiers’ shoulders.

Acts 21:36

36for the multitude of the people followed after, crying out, “Away with him!”

Arminian

The crowd keeps following and shouting, “Away with him!” The same cry was raised against Jesus (Luke 23:18).

Reformed

The echo of the passion narrative is deliberate. Paul is following his Lord’s path, as Jesus had warned his disciples they would.

Acts 21:37

37As Paul was about to be brought into the barracks, he asked the commanding officer, “May I speak to you?” He said, “Do you know Greek?

Arminian

At the barracks entrance Paul asks in Greek whether he may speak. The commander is surprised that he knows Greek.

Reformed

Paul’s calm request contrasts with the chaos around him. He is ready to use any opening for testimony.

Acts 21:38

38Aren’t you then the Egyptian who before these days stirred up to sedition and led out into the wilderness the four thousand men of the Assassins?”

Arminian

The commander had assumed Paul was the Egyptian who recently led four thousand Sicarii, the “dagger men,” into the wilderness, an event Josephus also mentions.

Reformed

The mistake shows how Rome viewed unrest in Judea. Luke wants readers to see that Paul is no revolutionary.

Acts 21:39

39But Paul said, “I am a Jew from Tarsus in Cilicia, a citizen of no insignificant city. I beg you, allow me to speak to the people.”

Arminian

Paul identifies himself as a Jew from Tarsus in Cilicia, “a citizen of no ordinary city,” and asks to address the people.

Reformed

Paul presents his credentials precisely: Jewish by birth, from a respected Greek city. He has not yet mentioned his Roman citizenship, which he saves for 22:25.

Acts 21:40

40When he had given him permission, Paul, standing on the stairs, beckoned with his hand to the people. When there was a great silence, he spoke to them in the Hebrew language, saying,

Arminian

Granted permission, Paul stands on the stairs, motions for silence, and speaks in Hebrew, probably Aramaic. A great hush falls.

Reformed

A prisoner in chains, standing between a mob and a garrison, receives a platform to tell his story to the very people who just tried to kill him.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
