Passage

Acts 10

Verses 1–23 · WEB

Cornelius, a God-fearing Roman centurion in Caesarea who prays and gives generously, sees an angel who tells him to send for Peter in Joppa. The next day, while his messengers are on the way, Peter falls into a trance on a rooftop and sees a sheet full of unclean animals, with a voice telling him to kill and eat; when he refuses, the voice says not to call unclean what God has cleansed. As Peter ponders the vision, the Spirit tells him to go with the men without hesitation, and he hosts them and sets out the following day.

Acts 10:1

1Now there was a certain man in Caesarea, Cornelius by name, a centurion of what was called the Italian Regiment,

Arminian

Cornelius is a centurion of the Italian cohort in Caesarea. Arminian readers see a Gentile soldier, an unlikely candidate for the next great step of the mission.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God choosing a Gentile household to open the door to the nations.

Acts 10:2

2a devout man, and one who feared God with all his house, who gave gifts for the needy generously to the people, and always prayed to God.

Arminian

He is devout, fears God with his household, gives alms, and prays continually. Arminian commentators often see prevenient grace already at work, drawing him toward God before he heard the gospel.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see God’s preparatory work in a man he intends to save, while noting that he still needed to hear of Christ (11:14).

Acts 10:3

3At about the ninth hour of the day, he clearly saw in a vision an angel of God coming to him and saying to him, “Cornelius!”

Arminian

At three in the afternoon he sees an angel in a vision. Arminian readers see God responding to a seeker’s prayers.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God initiating contact at the hour of prayer.

Acts 10:4

4He, fastening his eyes on him and being frightened, said, “What is it, Lord?” He said to him, “Your prayers and your gifts to the needy have gone up for a memorial before God.

Arminian

His prayers and alms have gone up as a memorial before God. Arminian expositors see God noticing sincere devotion.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see God remembering what he himself prompted, without treating it as earning salvation.

Acts 10:5

5Now send men to Joppa, and get Simon, who is also called Peter.

Arminian

He is to send to Joppa for Simon Peter. Arminian readers see God using a human messenger even when an angel is present.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the angel directing him to the preached word, the ordinary means of salvation.

Acts 10:6

6He is staying with a tanner named Simon, whose house is by the seaside.

Arminian

Peter is staying with Simon the tanner by the sea. Arminian commentators see precise directions given.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see providence in every detail.

Acts 10:7

7When the angel who spoke to him had departed, Cornelius called two of his household servants and a devout soldier of those who waited on him continually.

Arminian

Cornelius calls two servants and a devout soldier. Arminian readers see immediate obedience.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a household already shaped by his faith.

Acts 10:8

8Having explained everything to them, he sent them to Joppa.

Arminian

He explains everything and sends them to Joppa. Arminian expositors see his openness to God’s direction.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see God moving events toward the meeting.

Acts 10:9

9Now on the next day as they were on their journey and got close to the city, Peter went up on the housetop to pray at about noon.

Arminian

At noon the next day Peter goes up on the roof to pray. Arminian readers see prayer as the place where God redirects his servants.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God timing Peter’s vision with the messengers’ approach.

Acts 10:10

10He became hungry and desired to eat, but while they were preparing, he fell into a trance.

Arminian

He becomes hungry and falls into a trance. Arminian commentators see God speaking through ordinary needs.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see God preparing his servant’s heart for a new step.

Acts 10:11

11He saw heaven opened and a certain container descending to him, like a great sheet let down by four corners on the earth,

Arminian

He sees heaven opened and a sheet let down. Arminian readers see a vision that comes from God, not from Peter’s imagination.

Reformed

Reformed readers see revelation from heaven overturning settled assumptions.

Acts 10:12

12in which were all kinds of four-footed animals of the earth, wild animals, reptiles, and birds of the sky.

Arminian

It contains all kinds of animals, clean and unclean. Arminian expositors see a picture of all nations.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see the food laws, which separated Israel from the nations, about to be set aside.

Acts 10:13

13A voice came to him, “Rise, Peter, kill and eat!”

Arminian

“Rise, Peter, kill and eat!” Arminian readers see a command that challenges Peter’s habits.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s authority to redefine what is clean.

Acts 10:14

14But Peter said, “Not so, Lord; for I have never eaten anything that is common or unclean.”

Arminian

Peter refuses because he has never eaten anything unclean. Arminian commentators see sincere scruples that God must correct.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see that even an apostle needed his thinking reformed by revelation.

Acts 10:15

15A voice came to him again the second time, “What God has cleansed, you must not call unclean.”

Arminian

“What God has cleansed, you must not call unclean.” Arminian readers see God declaring Gentiles welcome in his people.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s cleansing as his sovereign act, which no human may contradict.

Acts 10:16

16This was done three times, and immediately the thing was received up into heaven.

Arminian

This happens three times. Arminian expositors recall Peter’s threefold denial and threefold restoration.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see emphasis on certainty.

Acts 10:17

17Now while Peter was very perplexed in himself what the vision which he had seen might mean, behold, the men who were sent by Cornelius, having made inquiry for Simon’s house, stood before the gate,

Arminian

While Peter is perplexed, Cornelius’s men arrive. Arminian readers see God answering Peter’s question through events.

Reformed

Reformed readers see providence and revelation meeting at the same moment.

Acts 10:18

18and called and asked whether Simon, who was also called Peter, was lodging there.

Arminian

They ask whether Simon Peter is staying there. Arminian commentators see the moment of decision approaching.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see God’s plan unfolding step by step.

Acts 10:19

19While Peter was pondering the vision, the Spirit said to him, “Behold, three men seek you.

Arminian

The Spirit tells Peter that three men are looking for him. Arminian readers see the Spirit guiding personally.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Spirit directing the mission to the Gentiles.

Acts 10:20

20But arise, get down, and go with them, doubting nothing; for I have sent them.”

Arminian

He must go with them without doubting, for the Spirit sent them. Arminian expositors see a call to trust that overrides old barriers.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see the Spirit as the true sender of the mission.

Acts 10:21

21Peter went down to the men, and said, “Behold, I am he whom you seek. Why have you come?”

Arminian

Peter goes down and asks why they have come. Arminian readers see willingness to engage.

Reformed

Reformed readers see obedience following revelation.

Acts 10:22

22They said, “Cornelius, a centurion, a righteous man and one who fears God, and well spoken of by all the nation of the Jews, was directed by a holy angel to invite you to his house, and to listen to what you say.”

Arminian

They describe Cornelius as righteous, God-fearing, and well spoken of by the Jews. Arminian commentators see a good reputation preparing the way.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see God’s instruction that Cornelius should hear Peter’s words.

Acts 10:23

23So he called them in and provided a place to stay. On the next day Peter arose and went out with them, and some of the brothers from Joppa accompanied him.

Arminian

Peter invites them in as guests, and the next day goes with some brothers from Joppa. Arminian readers see hospitality to Gentiles as the first sign of change.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Peter bringing witnesses, which will matter in chapter 11.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
