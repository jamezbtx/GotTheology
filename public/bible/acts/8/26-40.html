Passage

Acts 8

Verses 26–40 · WEB

An angel sends Philip to the desert road toward Gaza, where an Ethiopian official, treasurer to the queen, is returning from worship in Jerusalem and reading Isaiah. Prompted by the Spirit, Philip asks whether he understands, and the man invites him to explain the passage about the sheep led to slaughter. Philip tells him the good news about Jesus. When they come to water, the eunuch is baptized, and the Spirit carries Philip away. The official goes on his way rejoicing, and Philip preaches in the towns up to Caesarea.

Acts 8:26

26Then an angel of the Lord spoke to Philip, saying, “Arise, and go toward the south to the way that goes down from Jerusalem to Gaza. This is a desert.”

Arminian

An angel tells Philip to go to the desert road. Arminian readers see Philip leaving a successful ministry to obey a strange command.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God directing his servant to meet the one he has prepared.

Acts 8:27

27He arose and went; and behold, there was a man of Ethiopia, a eunuch of great authority under Candace, queen of the Ethiopians, who was over all her treasure, who had come to Jerusalem to worship.

Arminian

Philip meets an Ethiopian eunuch, a high official who had come to worship. Arminian commentators see a seeker already drawn toward God.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see God reaching a man excluded from full temple worship by Deuteronomy 23:1, fulfilling Isaiah 56:3–5.

Acts 8:28

28He was returning and sitting in his chariot, and was reading the prophet Isaiah.

Arminian

He is reading Isaiah in his chariot. Arminian readers see someone searching Scripture for answers.

Reformed

Reformed readers see providence arranging the very text he is reading.

Acts 8:29

29The Spirit said to Philip, “Go near, and join yourself to this chariot.”

Arminian

The Spirit tells Philip to go to the chariot. Arminian expositors see the Spirit guiding the evangelist.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see the Spirit orchestrating both preacher and hearer.

Acts 8:30

30Philip ran to him, and heard him reading Isaiah the prophet, and said, “Do you understand what you are reading?”

Arminian

Philip asks if he understands what he is reading. Arminian readers see the gospel offered through conversation.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the need for Scripture to be explained.

Acts 8:31

31He said, “How can I, unless someone explains it to me?” He begged Philip to come up and sit with him.

Arminian

“How can I, unless someone explains it to me?” Arminian commentators see humility and openness.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see the ordinary means of grace: God uses preachers to open the word.

Acts 8:32

32Now the passage of the Scripture which he was reading was this, “He was led as a sheep to the slaughter. As a lamb before his shearer is silent, so he doesn’t open his mouth.

Arminian

The passage is Isaiah 53: like a sheep led to slaughter. Arminian readers see Christ’s silent suffering for the world.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the suffering Servant bearing the sins of many as a substitute.

Acts 8:33

33In his humiliation, his judgment was taken away. Who will declare His generation? For his life is taken from the earth.”

Arminian

In his humiliation justice was denied him. Arminian expositors see the injustice of Jesus’ trial foretold.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see the Servant’s humiliation as the path to his exaltation.

Acts 8:34

34The eunuch answered Philip, “Who is the prophet talking about? About himself, or about someone else?”

Arminian

He asks whether the prophet speaks of himself or someone else. Arminian readers see a question that opens the door to the gospel.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a central interpretive question answered only in Christ.

Acts 8:35

35Philip opened his mouth, and beginning from this Scripture, preached to him about Jesus.

Arminian

Starting from this Scripture, Philip tells him the good news of Jesus. Arminian commentators see Christ preached from the Old Testament.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see all Scripture leading to Christ.

Acts 8:36

36As they went on the way, they came to some water; and the eunuch said, “Behold, here is water. What is keeping me from being baptized?”

Arminian

They come to water, and he asks what prevents him from being baptized. Arminian readers see eager response to the message.

Reformed

Reformed readers see baptism as the sign that marks one who has received the gospel.

Acts 8:38

38He commanded the chariot to stand still, and they both went down into the water, both Philip and the eunuch, and he baptized him.

Arminian

They both go down into the water, and Philip baptizes him. Credobaptists in both traditions point to the believer’s confession before baptism; Arminian readers here see a free personal response sealed in obedience. The WEB omits verse 37, which appears in later manuscripts.

Reformed

Paedobaptist Reformed readers note that this is an adult convert, where profession naturally comes first. Reformed Baptists see a model of believer’s baptism. The WEB omits verse 37, a later addition.

Acts 8:39

39When they came up out of the water, the Spirit of the Lord caught Philip away, and the eunuch didn’t see him any more, for he went on his way rejoicing.

Arminian

The Spirit carries Philip away, and the eunuch goes on rejoicing. Arminian expositors see joy as the fruit of salvation.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see the new believer sustained by God even without his teacher.

Acts 8:40

40But Philip was found at Azotus. Passing through, he preached the Good News to all the cities until he came to Caesarea.

Arminian

Philip appears at Azotus and preaches to all the towns up to Caesarea. Arminian readers see a tireless evangelist.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the word continuing to spread through faithful messengers.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
