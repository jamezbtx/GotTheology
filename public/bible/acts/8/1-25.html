Passage

Acts 8

Verses 1–25 · WEB

Saul approves of Stephen’s death, and a severe persecution scatters the believers through Judea and Samaria, while Saul ravages the church. The scattered believers preach the word, and Philip proclaims Christ in Samaria, where signs and healings bring great joy. Simon, a sorcerer the Samaritans revered, also believes and is baptized. When Peter and John come and pray, the Samaritans receive the Holy Spirit through the laying on of hands. Simon offers money for this power, and Peter rebukes him sharply and calls him to repent.

Acts 8:1

1Saul was consenting to his death. A great persecution arose against the assembly which was in Jerusalem in that day. They were all scattered abroad throughout the regions of Judea and Samaria, except for the apostles.

Arminian

Saul approves of the killing, and persecution scatters everyone except the apostles. Arminian readers see suffering pushing the church outward, as Jesus said in 1:8.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God using persecution to spread the gospel exactly where he had promised it would go.

Acts 8:2

2Devout men buried Stephen and lamented greatly over him.

Arminian

Devout men bury Stephen and mourn deeply. Arminian commentators see grief that is fitting even for a martyr.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see that faith in the resurrection does not forbid sorrow.

Acts 8:3

3But Saul ravaged the assembly, entering into every house and dragged both men and women off to prison.

Arminian

Saul ravages the church, dragging men and women to prison. Arminian readers see the depth of hostility that grace will later overcome.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the persecutor whom God has already set apart, as Galatians 1:15 says.

Acts 8:4

4Therefore those who were scattered abroad went around preaching the word.

Arminian

Those who were scattered preach the word wherever they go. Arminian expositors see ordinary believers taking up the mission.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see the word spreading through the scattering God permitted.

Acts 8:5

5Philip went down to the city of Samaria and proclaimed to them the Christ.

Arminian

Philip proclaims Christ in Samaria. Arminian readers see the gospel crossing an old ethnic and religious divide.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the second stage of 1:8 fulfilled.

Acts 8:6

6The multitudes listened with one accord to the things that were spoken by Philip when they heard and saw the signs which he did.

Arminian

The crowds listen with one accord as they see the signs. Arminian commentators see hearts opened through hearing and seeing.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see God giving attention to his word.

Acts 8:7

7For unclean spirits came out of many of those who had them. They came out, crying with a loud voice. Many who had been paralyzed and lame were healed.

Arminian

Unclean spirits come out, and many paralyzed and lame are healed. Arminian readers see Christ’s power over evil continuing through his servants.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the kingdom advancing against the powers of darkness.

Acts 8:8

8There was great joy in that city.

Arminian

There is great joy in the city. Arminian expositors see joy as the fruit of receiving the gospel.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see salvation bringing gladness to a people once estranged.

Acts 8:9

9But there was a certain man, Simon by name, who used to practice sorcery in the city and amazed the people of Samaria, making himself out to be some great one,

Arminian

Simon had practiced sorcery and amazed the Samaritans. Arminian readers see a man with influence built on deception.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the spiritual bondage the gospel confronts.

Acts 8:10

10to whom they all listened, from the least to the greatest, saying, “This man is that great power of God.”

Arminian

They all called him the great power of God. Arminian commentators see people drawn to counterfeit power.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see the human tendency to honor whatever seems mighty.

Acts 8:11

11They listened to him because for a long time he had amazed them with his sorceries.

Arminian

They followed him because he had astonished them for a long time. Arminian readers see how long habits of belief can hold people.

Reformed

Reformed readers see darkness that only the gospel can break.

Acts 8:12

12But when they believed Philip preaching good news concerning God’s Kingdom and the name of Jesus Christ, they were baptized, both men and women.

Arminian

When they believed Philip’s preaching, men and women were baptized. Arminian expositors see belief followed by baptism.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see the kingdom and the name of Jesus at the center of the message that brought faith.

Acts 8:13

13Simon himself also believed. Being baptized, he continued with Philip. Seeing signs and great miracles occurring, he was amazed.

Arminian

Simon himself believed and was baptized, and he stayed close to Philip, amazed by the signs. Arminian readers often take Simon’s faith as genuine but immature, endangered by his later sin.

Reformed

Reformed readers often see Simon’s faith as a belief in the signs rather than saving trust, since verse 21 says his heart was not right.

Acts 8:14

14Now when the apostles who were at Jerusalem heard that Samaria had received the word of God, they sent Peter and John to them,

Arminian

The apostles send Peter and John to Samaria. Arminian commentators see the Jerusalem church welcoming the new believers.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see apostolic oversight joining Samaria to the one church.

Acts 8:15

15who, when they had come down, prayed for them, that they might receive the Holy Spirit;

Arminian

They pray for the Samaritans to receive the Holy Spirit. Arminian readers see prayer as the means by which the gift comes.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God marking this new stage of the mission with a visible confirmation.

Acts 8:16

16for as yet he had fallen on none of them. They had only been baptized in the name of Christ Jesus.

Arminian

The Spirit had not yet fallen on any of them; they had only been baptized. Wesleyan and Pentecostal interpreters often see a second experience of the Spirit after conversion here.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters usually see an unusual delay meant to unite Samaritan believers with the apostolic church, not a pattern for every believer.

Acts 8:17

17Then they laid their hands on them, and they received the Holy Spirit.

Arminian

The apostles lay hands on them, and they receive the Spirit. Arminian expositors see God answering prayer through the apostles’ ministry.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see the gift confirming that Samaritans belong fully to God’s people.

Acts 8:18

18Now when Simon saw that the Holy Spirit was given through the laying on of the apostles’ hands, he offered them money,

Arminian

Simon sees the Spirit given through the apostles’ hands and offers money. Arminian readers see an old way of thinking resurfacing.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a heart that wanted power without submission to God.

Acts 8:19

19saying, “Give me also this power, that whomever I lay my hands on may receive the Holy Spirit.”

Arminian

He asks for the power to give the Spirit by laying on hands. Arminian commentators see ambition mistaking God’s gift for a skill.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see the error later named simony, the attempt to buy spiritual things.

Acts 8:20

20But Peter said to him, “May your silver perish with you, because you thought you could obtain the gift of God with money!

Arminian

Peter says, “May your silver perish with you,” since he thought God’s gift could be bought. Arminian readers see a solemn warning about where Simon is heading.

Reformed

Reformed readers see grace as a free gift that cannot be purchased.

Acts 8:21

21You have neither part nor lot in this matter, for your heart isn’t right before God.

Arminian

Simon has no part in this matter, for his heart is not right before God. Arminian expositors see a believer whose heart has gone wrong and who now stands in danger.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see an outward profession without inward change.

Acts 8:22

22Repent therefore of this, your wickedness, and ask God if perhaps the thought of your heart may be forgiven you.

Arminian

“Repent of this wickedness, and ask God if perhaps the thought of your heart may be forgiven.” Arminian readers see the door of repentance still open.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the call to repent addressed to Simon whatever his true state.

Acts 8:23

23For I see that you are in the poison of bitterness and in the bondage of iniquity.”

Arminian

Peter sees him in the gall of bitterness and the bond of iniquity. Arminian commentators see sin that has taken hold through his choices.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see language that describes an unregenerate condition.

Acts 8:24

24Simon answered, “Pray for me to the Lord, that none of the things which you have spoken happen to me.”

Arminian

Simon asks Peter to pray that nothing he said would happen. Arminian readers see a response that falls short of repentance but leaves the outcome open.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Simon fears the consequences rather than grieving over sin.

Acts 8:25

25They therefore, when they had testified and spoken the word of the Lord, returned to Jerusalem, and preached the Good News to many villages of the Samaritans.

Arminian

Peter and John preach in many Samaritan villages on their way home. Arminian expositors see the apostles embracing the mission to Samaria.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see the gospel advancing village by village.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
