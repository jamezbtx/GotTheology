Passage

Acts 23

Verses 1–11 · WEB

Before the council Paul declares that he has lived in all good conscience before God, and the high priest Ananias orders him struck. Paul rebukes him as a whitewashed wall, then apologizes when told he is the high priest. Seeing the council divided between Sadducees and Pharisees, Paul declares he is on trial for the hope of the resurrection, and the dispute grows so violent that the commander removes him. That night the Lord stands by Paul and tells him to take courage, for as he has testified in Jerusalem, he must also testify in Rome.

Acts 23:1

1Paul, looking steadfastly at the council, said, “Brothers, I have lived before God in all good conscience until today.”

Arminian

Paul looks straight at the council and says he has lived before God in all good conscience to this day. Even his persecuting years were sincere, though he later calls them ignorance (1 Timothy 1:13).

Reformed

A clear conscience is not the same as innocence before God. Paul elsewhere says, “I know nothing against myself, yet I am not justified by this” (1 Corinthians 4:4).

Acts 23:2

2The high priest, Ananias, commanded those who stood by him to strike him on the mouth.

Arminian

The high priest Ananias, son of Nedebaeus, known from Josephus for greed and violence, orders those near Paul to strike him on the mouth.

Reformed

The blow recalls Jesus being struck before Annas (John 18:22). The court breaks its own law before any testimony is heard.

Acts 23:3

3Then Paul said to him, “God will strike you, you whitewashed wall! Do you sit to judge me according to the law, and command me to be struck contrary to the law?”

Arminian

Paul fires back: “God will strike you, you whitewashed wall!” The judge sits to judge by the law yet orders a blow against the law. Ananias was assassinated about a decade later.

Reformed

“Whitewashed wall” echoes Ezekiel 13:10–15 and Jesus’ “whitewashed tombs” (Matthew 23:27), an image of a sound surface hiding decay.

Acts 23:4

4Those who stood by said, “Do you malign God’s high priest?”

Arminian

Bystanders object: “Do you insult God’s high priest?” They defend the office, not the man.

Reformed

The challenge raises a fair question about respect for office even when its holder acts unjustly.

Acts 23:5

5Paul said, “I didn’t know, brothers, that he was high priest. For it is written, ‘You shall not speak evil of a ruler of your people.’”

Arminian

Paul says he did not know it was the high priest, and quotes Exodus 22:28, “You shall not speak evil of a ruler of your people.” Some think poor eyesight or an informal session explains it; others hear irony.

Reformed

Whatever the reason, Paul submits his words to Scripture at once. The law binds even a provoked apostle.

Acts 23:6

6But when Paul perceived that the one part were Sadducees and the other Pharisees, he cried out in the council, “Men and brothers, I am a Pharisee, a son of Pharisees. Concerning the hope and resurrection of the dead I am being judged!”

Arminian

Seeing Sadducees and Pharisees in the room, Paul cries out that he is a Pharisee, a son of Pharisees, on trial for the hope of the resurrection of the dead.

Reformed

This is not a mere tactic. The resurrection of Jesus is exactly the hope Paul preaches, and it is the point where Pharisees and Sadducees divided.

Acts 23:7

7When he had said this, an argument arose between the Pharisees and Sadducees, and the crowd was divided.

Arminian

The statement splits the council, and the assembly divides along party lines.

Reformed

God uses the council’s own doctrinal quarrel to protect his servant.

Acts 23:8

8For the Sadducees say that there is no resurrection, nor angel, nor spirit; but the Pharisees confess all of these.

Arminian

Luke explains: Sadducees deny resurrection, angels, and spirits; Pharisees affirm all three.

Reformed

The note helps Gentile readers follow the scene and shows how central the resurrection was to the dispute.

Acts 23:9

9A great clamor arose, and some of the scribes of the Pharisees’ part stood up, and contended, saying, “We find no evil in this man. But if a spirit or angel has spoken to him, let’s not fight against God!”

Arminian

Some Pharisee scribes declare they find nothing wrong with Paul and suggest a spirit or angel may have spoken to him.

Reformed

The concession echoes Gamaliel’s caution in 5:39. Pharisees again become unlikely defenders.

Acts 23:10

10When a great argument arose, the commanding officer, fearing that Paul would be torn in pieces by them, commanded the soldiers to go down and take him by force from among them and bring him into the barracks.

Arminian

The dispute turns violent, and the commander, fearing Paul will be torn apart, has soldiers pull him out and take him back to the barracks.

Reformed

For the second time in two days, Roman troops rescue Paul from his own people.

Acts 23:11

11The following night, the Lord stood by him and said, “Cheer up, Paul, for as you have testified about me at Jerusalem, so you must testify also at Rome.”

Arminian

The next night the Lord stands by Paul: “Cheer up, Paul, for as you have testified about me at Jerusalem, so you must testify also at Rome.” Discouragement is met with personal presence.

Reformed

The word “must” (dei) marks God’s fixed purpose. From this point everything, from plots to shipwreck, will be read in light of this promise that Paul will reach Rome.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
