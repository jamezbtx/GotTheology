Passage

Acts 23

Verses 12–35 · WEB

More than forty men vow not to eat or drink until they have killed Paul, planning an ambush with the chief priests’ help. Paul’s nephew hears of the plot and reports it to Paul and then to the commander. The commander sends Paul by night under heavy guard to Governor Felix in Caesarea, with a letter explaining that Paul is accused only over questions of Jewish law and has done nothing deserving death or chains. Felix agrees to hear the case when the accusers arrive and keeps Paul in Herod’s palace.

Acts 23:12

12When it was day, some of the Jews banded together and bound themselves under a curse, saying that they would neither eat nor drink until they had killed Paul.

Arminian

At daybreak some Jews band together and bind themselves under a curse not to eat or drink until they have killed Paul.

Reformed

The oath is a self-curse invoking God against themselves if they fail. Their religious intensity is aimed at murder.

Acts 23:13

13There were more than forty people who had made this conspiracy.

Arminian

More than forty men join the conspiracy, a serious threat for a man in custody.

Reformed

The size of the plot sets up how God will overturn it through a single young man.

Acts 23:14

14They came to the chief priests and the elders, and said, “We have bound ourselves under a great curse to taste nothing until we have killed Paul.

Arminian

They inform the chief priests and elders of their vow, seeking help. Religious leaders are drawn into a murder plot.

Reformed

The leaders’ willingness to cooperate exposes how far the hostility has gone.

Acts 23:15

15Now therefore, you with the council inform the commanding officer that he should bring him down to you tomorrow, as though you were going to judge his case more exactly. We are ready to kill him before he comes near.”

Arminian

The council is to ask the commander to bring Paul down again on a pretext of further inquiry, and the assassins will kill him on the way.

Reformed

The plan depends on deceiving the Roman officer, the same one who rescued Paul twice.

Acts 23:16

16But Paul’s sister’s son heard they were lying in wait, and he came and entered into the barracks and told Paul.

Arminian

Paul’s nephew, his sister’s son, hears of the ambush and enters the barracks to tell him. This is the only glimpse of Paul’s family in the New Testament.

Reformed

God’s protection comes through an unnamed relative who happens to hear. The promise of verse 11 is kept by very ordinary means.

Acts 23:17

17Paul summoned one of the centurions and said, “Bring this young man to the commanding officer, for he has something to tell him.”

Arminian

Paul calls a centurion and asks him to take the young man to the commander. Paul trusts God’s promise and still acts sensibly.

Reformed

Reformed writers point to this as an example of providence working through human prudence rather than replacing it.

Acts 23:18

18So he took him and brought him to the commanding officer and said, “Paul, the prisoner, summoned me and asked me to bring this young man to you. He has something to tell you.”

Arminian

The centurion brings him, explaining that the prisoner Paul asked for this.

Reformed

Paul’s good standing with his guards makes the message travel quickly.

Acts 23:19

19The commanding officer took him by the hand, and going aside, asked him privately, “What is it that you have to tell me?”

Arminian

The commander takes the young man by the hand and leads him aside privately, a touch of kindness from a senior officer.

Reformed

The commander’s care for confidentiality allows the plan to be thwarted without tipping off the conspirators.

Acts 23:20

20He said, “The Jews have agreed to ask you to bring Paul down to the council tomorrow, as though intending to inquire somewhat more accurately concerning him.

Arminian

The nephew relays the plot: the council will ask for Paul tomorrow as if to inquire more closely.

Reformed

His account matches verses 14–15 exactly, giving the commander reason to believe him.

Acts 23:21

21Therefore don’t yield to them, for more than forty men lie in wait for him, who have bound themselves under a curse to neither eat nor drink until they have killed him. Now they are ready, looking for the promise from you.”

Arminian

“Don’t yield to them,” he urges; more than forty men are waiting and have sworn an oath. A young man speaks boldly to a Roman tribune.

Reformed

The detail that they are “now ready” adds urgency. The commander must act that night.

Acts 23:22

22So the commanding officer let the young man go, charging him, “Tell no one that you have revealed these things to me.”

Arminian

The commander dismisses him with orders to tell no one.

Reformed

Secrecy protects both the young man and the plan to move Paul.

Acts 23:23

23He called to himself two of the centurions, and said, “Prepare two hundred soldiers to go as far as Caesarea, with seventy horsemen and two hundred men armed with spears, at the third hour of the night.”

Arminian

He orders two centurions to prepare two hundred soldiers, seventy horsemen, and two hundred spearmen to leave at nine that night for Caesarea.

Reformed

Nearly five hundred troops escort one prisoner. The scale reflects both the danger and the commander’s concern to avoid blame.

Acts 23:24

24He asked them to provide mounts, that they might set Paul on one, and bring him safely to Felix the governor.

Arminian

Mounts are provided so Paul can be brought safely to Felix the governor.

Reformed

Felix, a former slave who rose to govern Judea, was known to Tacitus for cruelty and lust. Paul is heading into another uncertain court.

Acts 23:25

25He wrote a letter like this:

Arminian

The commander writes a letter, which Luke reproduces in summary form.

Reformed

The letter is the first official Roman document about Paul’s case in Acts.

Acts 23:26

26“Claudius Lysias to the most excellent governor Felix: Greetings.

Arminian

“Claudius Lysias, to the most excellent governor Felix: greetings.” This is the first time the commander’s name appears.

Reformed

The formal greeting follows the standard pattern of Greek letters of the period.

Acts 23:27

27“This man was seized by the Jews, and was about to be killed by them when I came with the soldiers and rescued him, having learned that he was a Roman.

Arminian

Lysias claims he rescued Paul after learning he was a Roman, quietly reversing the order of events and omitting the near-scourging.

Reformed

The letter is a small study in self-interest: the official edits the record to look good.

Acts 23:28

28Desiring to know the cause why they accused him, I brought him down to their council.

Arminian

He says he brought Paul before the council to learn the charge.

Reformed

This part of the report is accurate and shows Lysias trying to follow procedure.

Acts 23:29

29I found him to be accused about questions of their law, but not to be charged with anything worthy of death or of imprisonment.

Arminian

He found Paul accused about questions of Jewish law, with nothing deserving death or chains. This is the first Roman declaration of Paul’s innocence.

Reformed

Luke records several such verdicts (25:25; 26:31). Like Jesus, Paul is repeatedly found innocent by those who hold him.

Acts 23:30

30When I was told that the Jews lay in wait for the man, I sent him to you immediately, charging his accusers also to bring their accusations against him before you. Farewell.”

Arminian

Informed of a plot, he sent Paul at once and told the accusers to state their case before Felix.

Reformed

Transferring the case removes it from Jerusalem’s pressure and places it in Roman hands.

Acts 23:31

31So the soldiers, carrying out their orders, took Paul and brought him by night to Antipatris.

Arminian

The soldiers take Paul by night as far as Antipatris, about forty miles, through hill country where ambush was likely.

Reformed

Luke records the route precisely, as if from someone who knew the road.

Acts 23:32

32But on the next day they left the horsemen to go with him, and returned to the barracks.

Arminian

Once the danger zone is past, the foot soldiers return and the cavalry continue with Paul.

Reformed

The escort is scaled down once the threat recedes, a sensible military detail.

Acts 23:33

33When they came to Caesarea and delivered the letter to the governor, they also presented Paul to him.

Arminian

At Caesarea they deliver the letter and present Paul to the governor.

Reformed

Caesarea, Rome’s administrative capital in Judea, becomes Paul’s home for the next two years.

Acts 23:34

34When the governor had read it, he asked what province he was from. When he understood that he was from Cilicia, he said,

Arminian

Felix reads the letter and asks Paul’s province; learning it is Cilicia, he accepts jurisdiction.

Reformed

Cilicia was then administered with Syria, so Felix could hear the case without sending Paul home.

Acts 23:35

35“I will hear you fully when your accusers also arrive.” He commanded that he be kept in Herod’s palace.

Arminian

He promises a full hearing when the accusers arrive and orders Paul kept in Herod’s praetorium, the palace built by Herod the Great.

Reformed

Paul is confined in a former royal palace, safe from ambush, while God’s promise about Rome waits for its time.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
