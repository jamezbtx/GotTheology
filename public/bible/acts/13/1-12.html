Passage

Acts 13

Verses 1–12 · WEB

In the church at Antioch, while prophets and teachers worship and fast, the Holy Spirit tells them to set apart Barnabas and Saul for the work to which he has called them. After fasting, prayer, and the laying on of hands, they sail to Cyprus with John Mark and preach in the synagogues. At Paphos the proconsul Sergius Paulus wants to hear the word, but the sorcerer Elymas opposes them. Saul, also called Paul, filled with the Spirit, rebukes him and pronounces temporary blindness, and the proconsul believes, astonished at the teaching about the Lord.

Acts 13:1

1Now in the assembly that was at Antioch there were some prophets and teachers: Barnabas, Simeon who was called Niger, Lucius of Cyrene, Manaen the foster brother of Herod the tetrarch, and Saul.

Arminian

The Antioch church has prophets and teachers from diverse backgrounds. Arminian readers see a church reflecting the gospel’s reach.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God raising gifted leaders for his church.

Acts 13:2

2As they served the Lord and fasted, the Holy Spirit said, “Separate Barnabas and Saul for me, for the work to which I have called them.”

Arminian

While they worship and fast, the Spirit says, “Separate Barnabas and Saul for the work to which I have called them.” Arminian commentators see God calling particular people to a task, with the church responding.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see the Spirit as sovereign over the mission, choosing and sending.

Acts 13:3

3Then, when they had fasted and prayed and laid their hands on them, they sent them away.

Arminian

After fasting and prayer they lay hands on them and send them off. Arminian readers see the church affirming God’s call.

Reformed

Reformed readers see an orderly commissioning by the church.

Acts 13:4

4So, being sent out by the Holy Spirit, they went down to Seleucia. From there they sailed to Cyprus.

Arminian

Sent by the Holy Spirit, they go to Seleucia and Cyprus. Arminian expositors see the Spirit as the true sender.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see mission directed from heaven.

Acts 13:5

5When they were at Salamis, they proclaimed God’s word in the Jewish synagogues. They also had John as their attendant.

Arminian

They preach in synagogues at Salamis, with John as helper. Arminian readers see the pattern of going to the Jews first.

Reformed

Reformed readers see continuity with God’s covenant people.

Acts 13:6

6When they had gone through the island to Paphos, they found a certain sorcerer, a false prophet, a Jew whose name was Bar Jesus,

Arminian

At Paphos they meet a Jewish sorcerer and false prophet, Bar-Jesus. Arminian commentators see opposition from a spiritual counterfeit.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see the powers of darkness resisting the gospel.

Acts 13:7

7who was with the proconsul, Sergius Paulus, a man of understanding. This man summoned Barnabas and Saul, and sought to hear the word of God.

Arminian

The proconsul Sergius Paulus, an intelligent man, wants to hear the word. Arminian readers see an open seeker.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God drawing a person of influence.

Acts 13:8

8But Elymas the sorcerer (for so is his name by interpretation) withstood them, seeking to turn the proconsul away from the faith.

Arminian

Elymas opposes them, trying to turn the proconsul from the faith. Arminian expositors see someone actively hindering another’s response.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see opposition that cannot overturn God’s purpose.

Acts 13:9

9But Saul, who is also called Paul, filled with the Holy Spirit, fastened his eyes on him

Arminian

Saul, also called Paul, filled with the Spirit, stares at him. Arminian readers note the change of name as Paul’s Gentile mission begins.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Spirit empowering his servant for confrontation.

Acts 13:10

10and said, “You son of the devil, full of all deceit and all cunning, you enemy of all righteousness, will you not cease to pervert the right ways of the Lord?

Arminian

Paul calls him a son of the devil and enemy of righteousness. Arminian commentators see sharp words for deliberate deceit.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see the spiritual conflict exposed.

Acts 13:11

11Now, behold, the hand of the Lord is on you, and you will be blind, not seeing the sun for a season!” Immediately a mist and darkness fell on him. He went around seeking someone to lead him by the hand.

Arminian

The Lord’s hand will make him blind for a time. Arminian readers note the judgment is temporary, leaving room for repentance.

Reformed

Reformed readers see an echo of Paul’s own blindness and God’s power over opponents.

Acts 13:12

12Then the proconsul, when he saw what was done, believed, being astonished at the teaching of the Lord.

Arminian

The proconsul believes, astonished at the teaching of the Lord. Arminian expositors see a seeker persuaded by word and sign.

Reformed

Reformed expositors note that he is amazed at the teaching, not only the miracle.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
