Passage

Acts 12

Verses 1–25 · WEB

King Herod kills James the brother of John and, seeing that this pleases the Jewish leaders, arrests Peter during the feast, while the church prays earnestly for him. On the night before his trial an angel wakes Peter, his chains fall off, and he walks out past the guards into the city. He goes to the house of Mary, mother of John Mark, where the servant girl Rhoda is so overjoyed that she leaves him knocking at the gate. Herod executes the guards; later, accepting praise as a god in Caesarea, he is struck by an angel and dies. The word of God continues to grow, and Barnabas and Saul return with John Mark.

Acts 12:1

1Now about that time, King Herod stretched out his hands to oppress some of the assembly.

Arminian

Herod lays hands on some of the church to harm them. Arminian readers see political power turning against believers.

Reformed

Reformed readers see rulers opposing Christ as Psalm 2 foretold.

Acts 12:2

2He killed James, the brother of John, with the sword.

Arminian

He kills James, the brother of John, with the sword. Arminian commentators see that God does not always rescue his servants from death.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see God’s sovereign purposes in both James’s death and Peter’s rescue.

Acts 12:3

3When he saw that it pleased the Jews, he proceeded to seize Peter also. This was during the days of unleavened bread.

Arminian

Seeing it pleases the Jews, he arrests Peter during Unleavened Bread. Arminian readers see a ruler seeking popularity.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the timing echoing Jesus’ arrest at Passover.

Acts 12:4

4When he had arrested him, he put him in prison and delivered him to four squads of four soldiers each to guard him, intending to bring him out to the people after the Passover.

Arminian

He puts Peter under four squads of soldiers, planning a public trial after Passover. Arminian expositors see heavy security that only God could overcome.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see human precautions that cannot stop God.

Acts 12:5

5Peter therefore was kept in the prison, but constant prayer was made by the assembly to God for him.

Arminian

Peter is kept in prison, but the church prays earnestly for him. Arminian readers see prayer as something God genuinely responds to.

Reformed

Reformed readers see prayer as the means God ordained to accomplish his purpose.

Acts 12:6

6The same night when Herod was about to bring him out, Peter was sleeping between two soldiers, bound with two chains. Guards in front of the door kept the prison.

Arminian

The night before his trial Peter sleeps between two soldiers. Arminian commentators see peace in danger.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see rest that comes from trusting God’s care.

Acts 12:7

7And behold, an angel of the Lord stood by him, and a light shone in the cell. He struck Peter on the side and woke him up, saying, “Stand up quickly!” His chains fell off his hands.

Arminian

An angel strikes Peter’s side, wakes him, and the chains fall off. Arminian readers see God intervening dramatically.

Reformed

Reformed readers see deliverance that is entirely God’s work.

Acts 12:8

8The angel said to him, “Get dressed and put on your sandals.” He did so. He said to him, “Put on your cloak and follow me.”

Arminian

The angel tells him to dress and follow. Arminian expositors note that Peter must still get up and act.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see God directing every step.

Acts 12:9

9And he went out and followed him. He didn’t know that what was being done by the angel was real, but thought he saw a vision.

Arminian

Peter follows, thinking he is seeing a vision. Arminian readers see that he does not grasp what is happening.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God working beyond his servant’s understanding.

Acts 12:10

10When they were past the first and the second guard, they came to the iron gate that leads into the city, which opened to them by itself. They went out and went down one street, and immediately the angel departed from him.

Arminian

They pass the guards, the iron gate opens by itself, and the angel leaves. Arminian commentators see obstacles removed one by one.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see every barrier yielding to God’s will.

Acts 12:11

11When Peter had come to himself, he said, “Now I truly know that the Lord has sent out his angel and delivered me out of the hand of Herod, and from everything the Jewish people were expecting.”

Arminian

Peter realizes the Lord sent his angel to rescue him. Arminian readers see understanding dawning after the event.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Peter acknowledging God’s providence.

Acts 12:12

12Thinking about that, he came to the house of Mary, the mother of John who was called Mark, where many were gathered together and were praying.

Arminian

He goes to the house of Mary, where many are praying. Arminian expositors see the church still praying.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see the answer arriving while the prayer continues.

Acts 12:13

13When Peter knocked at the door of the gate, a servant girl named Rhoda came to answer.

Arminian

Rhoda comes to answer the knock. Arminian readers note the vivid detail of an eyewitness account.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s care touching ordinary moments.

Acts 12:14

14When she recognized Peter’s voice, she didn’t open the gate for joy, but ran in and reported that Peter was standing in front of the gate.

Arminian

Overjoyed, she runs back without opening. Arminian commentators see the humor of joy outrunning sense.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see the church slow to believe its own answered prayer.

Acts 12:15

15They said to her, “You are crazy!” But she insisted that it was so. They said, “It is his angel.”

Arminian

They tell her she is out of her mind; then they say it is his angel. Arminian readers see weak faith even in praying believers.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God answering prayers offered with imperfect faith.

Acts 12:16

16But Peter continued knocking. When they had opened, they saw him and were amazed.

Arminian

Peter keeps knocking, and they are amazed. Arminian expositors see persistence finally rewarded.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see God exceeding expectations.

Acts 12:17

17But he, beckoning to them with his hand to be silent, declared to them how the Lord had brought him out of the prison. He said, “Tell these things to James and to the brothers.” Then he departed and went to another place.

Arminian

He tells how the Lord brought him out, sends word to James, and leaves. Arminian readers note James, the Lord’s brother, now leading.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Peter giving God the credit.

Acts 12:18

18Now as soon as it was day, there was no small stir among the soldiers about what had become of Peter.

Arminian

At daybreak the soldiers are in turmoil. Arminian commentators see human plans thrown into confusion.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see God’s action confounding the powerful.

Acts 12:19

19When Herod had sought for him and didn’t find him, he examined the guards, then commanded that they should be put to death. He went down from Judea to Caesarea, and stayed there.

Arminian

Herod examines the guards and executes them. Arminian readers see cruelty from a frustrated ruler.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the contrast between the tyrant and the God who rescues.

Acts 12:20

20Now Herod was very angry with the people of Tyre and Sidon. They came with one accord to him and, having made Blastus, the king’s personal aide, their friend, they asked for peace, because their country depended on the king’s country for food.

Arminian

Herod is angry with Tyre and Sidon, who seek peace because they depend on his land for food. Arminian expositors see political maneuvering.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see the setting for Herod’s downfall.

Acts 12:21

21On an appointed day, Herod dressed himself in royal clothing, sat on the throne, and gave a speech to them.

Arminian

Herod sits on his throne in royal robes and gives a speech. Arminian readers see pride on display.

Reformed

Reformed readers see human glory set against God’s glory.

Acts 12:22

22The people shouted, “The voice of a god, and not of a man!”

Arminian

The people shout, “The voice of a god, and not of a man!” Arminian commentators see flattery Herod accepts.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see a creature accepting worship due to God alone.

Acts 12:23

23Immediately an angel of the Lord struck him, because he didn’t give God the glory. Then he was eaten by worms and died.

Arminian

An angel strikes him because he did not give God the glory, and he dies. Arminian readers see judgment on pride.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God defending his glory.

Acts 12:24

24But the word of God grew and multiplied.

Arminian

But the word of God grows and multiplies. Arminian expositors see the gospel outliving its persecutor.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see the contrast between the dying king and the living word.

Acts 12:25

25Barnabas and Saul returned to Jerusalem when they had fulfilled their service, also taking with them John who was called Mark.

Arminian

Barnabas and Saul return with John Mark. Arminian readers see the stage set for the first mission.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God gathering the team he will send.
