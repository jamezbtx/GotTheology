Passage

Daniel 1

Verses 8–16 · ESV · Voices

Daniel resolves not to defile himself with the king’s food and wine, and asks the chief of the eunuchs. God gives him favor and compassion; the chief still fears the king. Daniel proposes a ten-day test of vegetables and water. At the end they look better than those who ate the king’s food, and the steward keeps them on that diet.

Daniel 1:8

8But Daniel resolved that he would not defile himself with the king's food, or with the wine that he drank. Therefore he asked the chief of the eunuchs to allow him not to defile himself.

Arminian

Daniel resolved that he would not defile himself, then asked. Wesley: meats forbidden by the law; delicacies that would gratify the flesh; things consecrated to idols; fare unsuited to the people’s affliction. A rare pattern of avoiding occasions of evil—not a diet campaign, and not a culture fight about empire’s kitchens.

Arminian resolve: a real purpose in the heart under the Most High. Grace assists; it does not empty the asking. He could have taken the table; he does not. The fork of sovereignty waits on v. 9’s “God gave”; here the human yes is named without Pelagian leftover. Prevenient and assisting grace meet a will that actually purposes.

Reformed

Calvin: he endured what he could neither cast off nor escape, yet took care not to depart from the fear of God nor become a stranger to his race. Not superstition about meats—“to the pure all things are pure”—but he abstained at first from court luxuries to escape being tampered with. God enlightened his mind by His Spirit as soon as he prayed. He was also adviser to his companions.

Reformed resolve: the Spirit governs the purpose. Shared with Wesley: he will not defile himself, and he asks. The food is not a modern cause. The fork is how the next verse’s favor arrives.

Daniel 1:9

9And God gave Daniel favor and compassion in the sight of the chief of the eunuchs,

Arminian

And God gave Daniel favor and compassion in the chief’s sight. Wesley will mark the blessing on the later test; here the giving is already named. The Most High rules a courtier’s pity without canceling the fear of v. 10.

Arminian “gave”: real human agency under that rule. Ashpenaz’s compassion is a creaturely affection God governs, not a puppet-string. Daniel asked; God gave favor; the chief still has a head to fear. Match the hub: a King who governs, provides, and judges, leaving creaturely choice as a real second cause. Favor is gift; it is not irresistible determination of every courtier’s next word.

Reformed

Calvin: God placed him in favor and pity before that prefect. He ascribes the benefit to God, who bent the heart of a man otherwise unsoftened to clemency and humanity. Minds and hearts are so governed by God’s secret instinct that He changes their affections just as He pleases. On both sides the will is in God’s power—to bend to humanity or to harden what was tender.

Reformed “gave”: effectual rule, not a bare permission after the fact. The chief’s kindness is God’s actual hand, including the means. Second causes remain; they are established, not erased. Match the hub: nothing happens but what He has knowingly and willingly decreed, yet He is not the author of the court’s idolatry.

Key contrast Both confess the Most High rules: “God gave” favor. Arminians typically hear real asking and real pity under that rule—the eunuch’s heart is governed without irresistible determination of every choice. Reformed readers typically hear effectual providence, not a bare permission: God bent an otherwise unsoftened heart (Calvin).

Daniel 1:10

10and the chief of the eunuchs said to Daniel, “I fear my lord the king, who assigned your food and your drink; for why should he see that you were in worse condition than the youths who are of your own age? So you would endanger my head with the king.”

Arminian

The chief fears the king who assigned the food: worse faces would endanger his head. Wesley’s pattern of avoiding evil meets a man who will not risk that head. The refusal is honest, not a foil for politics.

Arminian court: fear is a real second cause. Favor in v. 9 did not erase the chief’s calculation. Agency stands on both sides of the table—Daniel asked; Ashpenaz declines. God still rules; the head is still at stake.

Reformed

Calvin: his humanity is praised not for granting the prayer but for burying in silence what might have brought death. He feared the king who had ordered the food. He is not to be blamed as if he feared man more than God, for he could not have any knowledge of God. He clung to Babylon’s religion while treating the youths civilly.

Reformed court: even the refusal sits inside providence. Shared: he will not grant it, and he does not accuse. The secret instinct of v. 9 has not yet opened the door; another servant will.

Daniel 1:11

11Then Daniel said to the steward whom the chief of the eunuchs had assigned over Daniel, Hananiah, Mishael, and Azariah,

Arminian

Daniel turns to the steward set over the four. Wesley’s pattern continues: he does not drop the purpose when the first door closes. A second ask is still an ask, not a slogan.

Arminian persistence: after a no, he purposes still. Grace that assists a first resolve assists a second approach. No Pelagian leftover—he is already a man God has favored—and no fatalism that treats the chief’s fear as the last word.

Reformed

Calvin: singular constancy. After a vain first try he did not cease. If we do not retrace our steps but try all ways, we show the root of piety. It might have seemed excusable after the first repulse; he goes to the servant. Voluntarily to incur risk was no common prudence; he was directed by God’s Spirit to this act of prudence.

Reformed persistence: the Spirit is the author of the next step. Shared: he speaks to the steward. The test of v. 12 is coming as means, not as magic.

Daniel 1:12

12“Test your servants for ten days; let us be given vegetables to eat and water to drink.

Arminian

“Test your servants ten days; vegetables and water.” Wesley: pulse and water against the king’s meat—not a diet as identity, but a rare pattern of avoiding occasions of evil. Ten days is a finite prove, not a culture-war on tables.

Arminian test: they propose means a steward can actually run. Human prudence under the Most High. The outcome is not bargained as a prosperity deal; they ask to be seen and dealt with according to what appears (v. 13).

Reformed

Calvin: without doubt, when Daniel brought this forward, he was directed by the Spirit and impelled to make this request. It would not have been duty but rashness had he been the author of the plan without assurance of the issue. Pulse and water: not because wine or flesh were unlawful (save to Nazarites), but he was unwilling to accustom himself to palace delicacies and forget Abraham’s family.

Reformed test: the plan is gift, not cleverness alone. Shared: vegetables and water for ten days. No diet-war as a modern cause.

Daniel 1:13

13Then let our appearance and the appearance of the youths who eat the king's food be observed by you, and deal with your servants according to what you see.”

Arminian

Compare our appearance with those who eat the king’s food, and deal with us as you see. Wesley’s later note will call the result the blessing of God upon homely fare. Here they submit the proof to the steward’s eyes.

Arminian sight: they do not demand a miracle on the calendar. They risk looking worse. Faith that will not defile itself still leaves the steward free to judge. Agency on his side remains.

Reformed

Calvin: the scheme is cautious enough for the servant—no crime if faces are fresh. Daniel did not speak without due consideration, but was assured of the event by the Spirit of God.

Reformed sight: God can make pulse serve as manna. Shared: the steward is asked to look. The contrast is not invented as prosperity versus fatalism; it is a prove in the open.

Daniel 1:14

14So he listened to them in this matter, and tested them for ten days.

Arminian

He listened and tested them ten days. Wesley’s reasons for the purpose now meet a steward’s consent. Listening is a human act under the giving of v. 9.

Arminian consent: Melzar hears and runs the test. The Most High rules; the steward still says yes. Second causes are not theater.

Reformed

Calvin: the servant could not make the same objection as the prefect. God rendered this man tractable and governed the whole event. Do not ascribe it entirely to human kindness: he never could have obtained it until the man perceived it could be granted safely.

Reformed consent: providence includes the steward’s interest and the ten days. Shared: he listened. The result of v. 15 will show whose blessing it was.

Daniel 1:15

15At the end of ten days it was seen that they were better in appearance and fatter in flesh than all the youths who ate the king's food.

Arminian

At ten days they looked better and fatter than those who ate the king’s food. Wesley: the blessing of God upon homely fare affords often more health and strength than costly fare to them that eat the fat and drink the sweet.

Arminian blessing: God answers the prove without turning vegetables into a law for every table. The youths remain under the Most High; the other youths are not a political foil. Health here is gift meeting resolve, not a slogan about diet.

Reformed

Calvin: this surprising event—neither leanness nor debility from mean food. God testified by the result that He had advised them. Man lives not by bread alone: we are nourished by God’s secret blessing; bread has no life in it of itself. Pulse can become as manna when the Lord turns it so.

Reformed blessing: the fatter flesh is God’s hand, not the virtue of legumes. Shared with Wesley: homely fare, and the blessing is the Lord’s. No culture-war on food.

Daniel 1:16

16So the steward took away their food and the wine they were to drink, and gave them vegetables.

Arminian

The steward takes away their portion and wine and gives them vegetables. Wesley’s pattern holds for the rest of the training years implied here. The table is settled without a manifesto.

Arminian continuance: a steward’s ongoing yes. Daniel’s purpose stands; Melzar’s practice stands. God gave favor; humans keep keeping the diet. The story is holiness in exile, not a campaign.

Reformed

Calvin: when Melsar saw he could gratify them without danger and even profit, he was humane and easily dealt with. Daniel’s intention: we ought not to ascribe it to the kindness of man that they remained pure. God openly shows how all the praise was due to Himself, to exercise their gratitude.

Reformed continuance: the standing diet is providence, including a steward’s self-interest. Shared: vegetables instead of the king’s food. The four remain unspotted worshipers under names the palace gave.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on a resolve not to defile, favor God gave in a fearful court, and homely fare that proved fairer—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Daniel 1:8–16.

Arminian Notable voices

“But Daniel purposed—there may be several weighty reasons assigned why Daniel did this. Because many of those meats provided for the king’s table were forbidden by the Jewish law. Daniel knew these delicates would too much gratify the flesh. He did not dare to eat and drink things consecrated to idols. He was sensible how unsuitable delicate fare would be to the afflicted state of God’s people. Therefore he was herein a rare pattern of avoiding all the occasions of evil. Fairer and fatter—the blessing of God upon homely fare affords often more health and strength than more costly fare to them that eat the fat and drink the sweet.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Dan 1:8, 15)

Reformed Notable voices

“Here Daniel shows his endurance of what he could neither cast off nor escape; but meanwhile he took care that he did not depart from the fear of God, nor become a stranger to his race, but he always retains the remembrance of his origin, and remains a pure, and unspotted, and sincere worshipper of God. God placed him in favor and pity before that prefect. Daniel obtained favor, since God bent the heart of a man, otherwise unsoftened, to clemency and humanity. The minds and hearts of men are so governed by God’s secret instinct that he changes their affections just as he pleases.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Daniel (on Dan 1:8–9)
