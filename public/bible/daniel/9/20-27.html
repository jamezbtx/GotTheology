Passage

Daniel 9

Verses 20–27 · ESV · Voices

While Daniel is still confessing, Gabriel arrives at the hour of the evening sacrifice with insight and a decreed span of seventy weeks. The weeks are history with a terminus: transgression finished, iniquity atoned, an anointed one cut off, and a city left desolate—read here as both restoration’s timetable and Messiah’s work.

Daniel 9:20

20While I was speaking and praying, confessing my sin and the sin of my people Israel, and presenting my plea before the Lord my God for the holy hill of my God,

Arminian

While he was still speaking, confessing his sin and his people’s, presenting a plea for the holy hill, the answer is already on the way. Wesley’s silence here is not disagreement. The prayer of 9:1–19 is still in the prophet’s mouth.

Arminian note: Daniel does not stop confessing in order to be heard. He names his own sin with Israel’s. The holy hill remains the burden of the plea. Mercy for a people and a place is still the subject when heaven begins to speak.

Reformed

Calvin: the prayers were by no means useless. Gabriel is sent to elevate his mind with confidence and to console the whole church with hope of return. Daniel, among the most perfect, still confesses himself a sinner and renounces his own righteousness. Even he places his only hope in mere mercy.

Reformed accent: God extends grace to the wretched who fly to it. Daniel’s own sin is not a courtesy. The man God will call greatly loved is still in the flock of sinners while he prays for the mountain of the sanctuary.

Daniel 9:21

21while I was speaking in prayer, the man Gabriel, whom I had seen in the vision at the first, came to me in swift flight at the time of the evening sacrifice.

Arminian

Wesley: the evening sacrifice was a solemn set time of devotion. Though the altar was in ruins and no oblation was offered, the pious were daily thoughtful of the hour, hoping prayer would be set forth as incense. This was peculiarly a type of that great sacrifice which Christ was to offer; and it was in virtue of that sacrifice that Daniel’s prayer was accepted, when he prayed for the Lord’s sake.

Arminian reading: Gabriel comes at a remembered hour. Wesley will not treat the time as empty custom. The ruined altar still points forward. Acceptance is in Christ’s offering, which Daniel’s “for the Lord’s sake” had already named.

Reformed

Calvin notes that seventy years had passed without Daniel seeing a sacrifice, yet he still speaks of the evening offering as if the temple’s hours remained. The fathers’ signs can still tutor the heart in exile. Gabriel, seen in the former vision, comes in swift flight as a minister of grace.

Reformed note: God answers in the ordinary holy hour the prophet still kept. The messenger is the same Gabriel. Consolation is sent to a man at prayer, not to a speculator of times. The ruined altar has not erased the day’s appointed remembrance.

Key contrast Both keep the evening sacrifice in view while the temple is down. Wesley presses it as a type of Christ’s great offering, and says Daniel’s prayer was accepted in virtue of that sacrifice when he prayed for the Lord’s sake. Calvin treats the named hour as devotion still practiced in exile, the fathers’ rite remembered though the altar was gone.

Daniel 9:22

22He made me understand, speaking with me and saying, “O Daniel, I have now come out to give you insight and understanding.

Arminian

Gabriel comes out to give insight and understanding. The seventy weeks will not be a riddle for its own sake. Daniel is made to understand. Wesley’s later notes will spend the understanding on Messiah’s redemption, not on a later calendar war.

Classical Arminians receive the stated purpose: skill to understand. The angel does not scold the prayer. He answers it with light. What follows is for a beloved man who asked mercy for his people.

Reformed

Calvin: the angel prepares Daniel’s mind—he came from heaven to teach. Such a gift must not be neglected. God opens his heart by the word; teachers are sent so that what would be hidden may be interpreted. The gospel itself is preached for the obedience of faith.

Reformed accent: insight is given, not seized. Daniel will be told to consider and understand because God has sent the messenger. The seventy weeks are instruction for the church, not a puzzle God refused to explain.

Daniel 9:23

23At the beginning of your pleas for mercy a word went out, and I have come to tell it to you, for you are greatly loved. Therefore consider the word and understand the vision.

Arminian

At the beginning of the pleas a word went out. Daniel is greatly loved; therefore he must consider the word and understand the vision. Love does not cancel the duty to attend. It grounds it.

Arminian pastoral: the answer was decreed as the prayer began, yet the prophet must still consider. Grace that loves a man also teaches him. Wesley will read the vision as Messiah’s work for our salvation. The beloved hearer is not excused from understanding.

Reformed

Calvin: the word went forth from the time Daniel began to pray—God heard, then promulgated what he had already decreed. Promises do not make us slothful. The saints plead what God has spoken, and he grants what he had determined, as if obedient to their desires.

Reformed comfort: you are greatly loved is not flattery; it is why the angel has come. Consider the word and understand the vision. The seventy weeks are a published decree, timed to a plea God had already chosen to answer.

Daniel 9:24

24“Seventy weeks are decreed about your people and your holy city, to finish the transgression, to put an end to sin, and to atone for iniquity, to bring in everlasting righteousness, to seal both vision and prophet, and to anoint a most holy place.

Arminian

Wesley: these weeks are weeks of days, and these days are so many years. The angel first names the disease in three words covering all sorts of sin, which the Messiah should free us from by his full redemption; then the cure—to finish transgression, make an end of sin, make reconciliation: to pardon, blot out, destroy. Everlasting righteousness is justification by the free grace of God in Christ, and sanctification by his Spirit—everlasting because Christ is eternal, and so are the acceptance and holiness purchased for us.

He brings this in by his merit, by his gospel declaring it, and by faith applying and sealing it by the Holy Ghost. To seal up is to abrogate the former dispensation of the law and ratify the gospel covenant. To anoint alludes to the name Messiah and Christ: anointed at conception, at baptism, to his three offices. Both historic weeks of years and a messianic aim: Wesley will not let the verse stop at a rebuilt city.

Reformed

Calvin: seventy weeks of years—490—cut off upon the people and holy city, a sevenfold span of grace after Jeremiah’s seventy of judgment, until God sets forth salvation in his Christ. The angel announces remission: to close up wickedness, seal sin, expiate iniquity. Christ poured out his blood, offered himself as an expiatory victim, and satisfied God by his death. Law sacrifices were shadows; had sins then been truly expiated, Christ need not have come.

Eternal righteousness is brought in because his death sufficed for all transgressions, unlike daily offerings. Vision and prophecy are sealed in him—fulfilled, and the obscure age of figures gives way to gospel light. To anoint the Holy of Holies points to the true tabernacle: Christ’s body the temple, the spiritual anointing when the Spirit rested on him. Calvin, like Wesley, reads history that lands on Christ, not a later rapture scheme.

Key contrast Both take the seventy weeks as years aiming at Christ, not as a blank for later systems. Wesley hears “everlasting righteousness” as justification by free grace and sanctification by the Spirit, and “anoint the most Holy” as the Messiah consecrated to his three offices. Calvin hears once-for-all expiation by Christ’s blood over against the law’s repeated shadows, and the anointing as the true Holy of Holies—Christ himself the temple.

Daniel 9:25

25Know therefore and understand that from the going out of the word to restore and build Jerusalem to the coming of an anointed one, a prince, there shall be seven weeks. And for sixty-two weeks it shall be built again with squares and moat, but in a troubled time.

Arminian

Wesley: from the going forth—from the publication of the edict, whether of Cyrus or Darius, to restore and to build. He will not pretend the starting decree is clearer than the text. Seven weeks and sixty-two follow; the street and wall rise in troublous times.

Arminian note: restoration is real history—squares, moat, trouble. The anointed prince is still the aim of the count. Wesley’s “whether” is modesty about which Persian word began the clock, not doubt that Messiah is where the weeks run.

Reformed

Calvin requires the going forth of the edict to be Cyrus’s decree, when a free return was granted. The seven weeks (forty-nine years) belong to the repair of city and temple amid hostility—the narrow, anxious times of Ezra and Nehemiah. He will not start the seventy weeks at a later Darius as if the chain from Jeremiah’s seventy years were broken.

Reformed accent: consolation depends on continuity. After seventy years of exile, seven weeks of troubled rebuilding, then sixty-two toward Messiah the Leader. The street is built with a sword in one hand. God still builds in the anxieties of the times.

Key contrast Both start the weeks from a Persian word to restore Jerusalem and end them at Christ. Wesley leaves the edict as “whether of Cyrus or Darius.” Calvin insists the seventy weeks begin with Cyrus’s monarchy, the seven weeks measuring the troubled rebuilding, so Jeremiah’s seventy years and these weeks remain one chain of mercy.

Daniel 9:26

26And after the sixty-two weeks, an anointed one shall be cut off and shall have nothing. And the people of the prince who is to come shall destroy the city and the sanctuary. Its end shall come with a flood, and to the end there shall be war. Desolations are decreed.

Arminian

Wesley: after the seven and the sixty-two, Messiah is cut off—not for himself, but for our sakes and for our salvation. The people of the prince who comes are the Romans under Titus. God has decreed to destroy that place and people by the miseries of war.

Historic and messianic together: the cut-off Anointed is Christ dying for others; the ruined city is Jerusalem under Rome. Wesley does not turn the verse into a later Antichrist timetable. The salvation for which Messiah is cut off is the same full redemption named in verse 24.

Reformed

Calvin: after the sixty-two weeks, Christ shall be cut off. He refuses Jewish evasions that make Agrippa the “Christ,” or Vespasian the covenant-maker. The death is ignominious: nothing shall remain to him, as if he vanished from men’s sight—Isaiah’s unlovely servant. The people of the coming leader destroy city and sanctuary; the end comes like a flood; desolations are decreed.

Reformed reading: the cutting off belongs with the last week’s work, when the covenant is confirmed and the Messiah is slain. History (Rome, flood of war) sits under a decree. Calvin and Wesley agree the ruined sanctuary is not a denial of the gospel. It is what God said would follow the rejected Christ.

Key contrast Both read an Anointed cut off and a Roman destruction of city and sanctuary. Wesley stresses “not for himself” but for our salvation, and names Titus’s Romans as the coming people. Calvin likewise takes Christ, not Agrippa, as the one cut off, and hears “nothing shall remain to him” as the shame of that death, the flood of war following under God’s decree.

Daniel 9:27

27And he shall make a strong covenant with many for one week, and for half of the week he shall put an end to sacrifice and offering. And on the wing of abominations shall come one who makes desolate, until the decreed end is poured out on the desolator.”

Arminian

Wesley: he shall confirm—Christ confirmed the new covenant by the testimony of angels, of John the Baptist, of the wise men, of living saints, of Moses and Elijah; by his preaching, signs, holy life, resurrection and ascension, and by his death and blood-shedding. He causes sacrifice to cease: all the Jewish rites and Levitical worship. By his death he abrogated that laborious service forever.

“That determined” Wesley reads as the spirit of slumber God would pour on the desolate nation until the time draws near when all Israel shall be saved. The weeks remain Messiah’s week. The desolation is real and long. It is not a third-party Antichrist as the Arminian side of a modern chart.

Reformed

Calvin: Christ confirms the covenant with many for one week when he assumes the Leader’s office and promulgates grace. “Many” reaches beyond the old narrow church to Gentiles joined with Jews. Sacrifice and offering cease for half a week at the resurrection: after he offered himself, the law’s rites closed; continued slaughter of beasts could no longer be legitimate.

The last week is gospel confirmation, then the city’s devastation under a decreed end. Calvin will not give the confirming “he” to Vespasian bargaining for surrender. Christ is the confirmer; Rome is the flood. Historic ruin, messianic covenant—the same two beams Wesley uses, fitted differently in the week’s interior.

Key contrast Both give verse 27 to Christ, not to a future Antichrist or a rapture scheme. Wesley reads the confirmed covenant as Christ’s new covenant, Levitical worship ended by his death, and a determined slumber on the nation until all Israel shall be saved. Calvin reads the last week as the Messiah confirming the covenant with many—Jews and Gentiles—and ending sacrifice when he offered himself, the city’s desolation following under God’s decreed close.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Gabriel’s answer and the seventy weeks that aim at Christ—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Daniel 9:20–27.

Arminian Notable voices

“The time of the evening sacrifice was a solemn and set time of devotion. Though the altar was in ruins, and there was no oblation offered upon it, yet the pious Jews were daily thoughtful of the time when it should have been offered. This was peculiarly a type of that great sacrifice which Christ was to offer: and it was in virtue of that sacrifice that Daniel’s prayer was accepted. Seventy weeks—These weeks are weeks of days, and these days are so many years. The angel discovers first the disease in three several words, which contain all sorts of sin, which the Messiah should free us from by his full redemption. To bring in everlasting righteousness—To bring in justification by the free grace of God in Christ, and sanctification by his spirit: called everlasting, because Christ is eternal, and so are the acceptance and holiness purchased for us. Not for himself—But for our sakes, and for our salvation. He shall confirm—Christ confirmed the new covenant. By his death he abrogated, and put an end to this laborious service, for ever.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Dan 9:21, 24, 26–27)

Reformed Notable voices

“His prayers were by no means useless, nor yet without their fruit, as Gabriel was sent to elevate his mind with confidence. Even he confesses himself a sinner before God, and utterly renounces his own righteousness. The angel foretells what they were to expect from Christ. First of all, he announces remission of sins. He poured forth his blood by which he blotted out our sins; he also offered himself as an expiatory victim, and satisfied God by the sacrifice of his death, so as entirely to absolve us from guilt. Therefore Christ alone brought in eternal righteousness—his death alone sufficed for expiating all transgressions. Christ took upon him the character of a leader when he promulgated the grace of God. This is the confirmation of the covenant of which the angel now speaks. After he had offered himself up as a victim, then all the rites of the law came to a close.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Daniel (on Dan 9:20–21, 24, 27)
