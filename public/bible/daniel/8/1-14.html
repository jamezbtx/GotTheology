Passage

Daniel 8

Verses 1–14 · ESV · Voices

In the third year of Belshazzar a vision appears to Daniel, after that which appeared at the first. He sees in the vision: Susa the citadel, in Elam, at the Ulai canal. A ram with two horns, one higher and later, charging west, north, and south; he became great. A male goat from the west, without touching the ground; a conspicuous horn between his eyes; he struck the ram, broke both horns, trampled him. The goat became exceedingly great; the great horn was broken; four conspicuous horns toward the four winds. Out of one, a little horn, grew toward the south, the east, and the glorious land. It grew to the host of heaven; some of the host and of the stars thrown down. It became great as the Prince of the host; the regular burnt offering taken away; the sanctuary overthrown. A host given over with the regular burnt offering because of transgression; truth thrown to the ground; it acted and prospered. A holy one: how long? Two thousand three hundred evenings and mornings. Then the sanctuary shall be restored to its rightful state.

Daniel 8:1

1In the third year of the reign of King Belshazzar a vision appeared to me, Daniel, after that which appeared to me at the first.

Arminian

In the third year of Belshazzar a vision appears to Daniel, after that which appeared at the first. Wesley: after that—in the other vision he speaks of all the four monarchies; here only of the three first; this vision being a comment upon the first.

Arminian pastoral: a named year, a second vision, not a new newspaper. Stay with the three: Medes and Persians, Greece, then the little horn. Shared: this comments on what was already shown, not a chart of modern flags.

Reformed

Calvin: another vision, differing from the former as a part from the whole. God had shown four monarchies; now only two remain in view, because Babylon is about to fall. The chief object: to prepare the faithful to bear patiently the horrible tyranny of Antiochus.

Reformed pastoral: God shows a part so the church will not faint when the sanctuary is hurt. Shared: after the first vision; not a 21st-century map. Not a dispensational versus amillennial fight.

Daniel 8:2

2And I saw in the vision; and when I saw, I was in Susa the citadel, which is in the province of Elam. And I saw in the vision, and I was at the Ulai canal.

Arminian

He sees in the vision: Susa the citadel, in Elam, at the Ulai canal. Wesley: the river of Ulai—which ran round the city.

Arminian pastoral: a real place in the vision, not a cue to invent a modern capital. Shared: Susa, Elam, Ulai. Daniel is shown, not relocated as a tourist. Not a prophecy-chart war.

Reformed

Calvin: he was in Shushan when he saw—caught up by the prophetic spirit, not dwelling in Persia as if he had left Babylon. By naming Belshazzar he shows he still dwelt under that power. The Ulai is the Eulaeus. The repetition adds certainty: not a vanishing specter.

Reformed pastoral: vision-place, not a private trip. Shared: Susa by the Ulai. God locates the ram where Persia will stand. Not a culture-war on empire.

Daniel 8:3

3I raised my eyes and saw, and behold, a ram standing on the bank of the canal. It had two horns, and both horns were high, but one was higher than the other, and the higher one came up last.

Arminian

A ram on the bank of the canal; two horns, both high; one higher, and the higher came up last. Wesley: two horns—the kingdom of Media and Persia. And the higher—the kingdom of Persia which rose last, in Cyrus, became more eminent than that of the Medes.

Arminian pastoral: two historic horns, not a modern map. Shared with Calvin: Medes and Persians, Persia later and higher. Do not recast the ram as a 21st-century power.

Reformed

Calvin: the empire of Persia and Media under the image of a ram; two horns for two empires, not two persons, united under one sovereignty. The two horns were lofty; the Persian horn higher than the Median; it rose backwards—afterwards—so that Cyrus’s later horn obscured the former glory.

Reformed pastoral: two empires, one ram. Shared: Media and Persia; the higher came last. This is history under God, not a newspaper.

Daniel 8:4

4I saw the ram charging westward and northward and southward. No beast could stand before him, and there was no one who could rescue from his power. He did as he pleased and became great.

Arminian

The ram charges west, north, and south; no beast can stand; none can rescue; he does as he pleases and becomes great. Wesley: west-ward—toward Babylon, Syria, Cappadocia, Asia the less, and Greece, all westward from Media and Persia. North-ward—against the Armenians, Iberians, Lydians, Colchi Caspians. South-ward—against Ethiopia, Arabia, Egypt.

Arminian pastoral: historic directions from Media and Persia. Shared: the ram becomes great, still a creature. Not a cue to name today’s states.

Reformed

Calvin: the ram struck west, north, and south. The Persian and Median territory lay east of Babylon, Egypt, Syria, Asia Minor, and Greece. This extends to Cyrus’s successors, who convulsed the world. No beasts stood before him; he did according to his pleasure and became mighty—formidable, not flawless in every campaign.

Reformed pastoral: greatness is still timed. Shared: west, north, south; none rescue. Not a modern flag-chart.

Daniel 8:5

5As I was considering, behold, a male goat came from the west across the face of the whole earth, without touching the ground. And the goat had a conspicuous horn between his eyes.

Arminian

A male goat from the west across the whole earth, without touching the ground; a conspicuous horn between his eyes. Wesley: an he-goat—the Grecian empire. The whole earth—the whole Persian empire. Touched not the ground—went with incredible swiftness. A horn—this was Alexander the great.

Arminian pastoral: Greece, Alexander, swift. Shared with Calvin. Do not invent a later goat. Not a prophecy-chart war.

Reformed

Calvin: a he-goat from the west—Macedon relative to Persia—over the face of the whole earth, yet he did not touch the ground: speed, not census. A remarkable horn between his eyes: Alexander, general of Greece against the Persians.

Reformed pastoral: swift and conspicuous is still a creature. Shared: goat from the west, Alexander. Not a millennial camp-fight.

Daniel 8:6

6He came to the ram with the two horns, which I had seen standing on the bank of the canal, and he ran at him in his powerful wrath.

Arminian

He comes to the ram with two horns and runs at him in powerful wrath. Wesley: the ram—the king of Media and Persia.

Arminian pastoral: wrath against a named ram, not a modern proxy. Shared: Greece strikes Persia. The Most High still times the charge.

Reformed

Calvin: it came to the ram which had two horns—the king of the Medes and Persians—and ran against it in the ardor of its bravery. Alexander’s perseverance and celerity: Darius sat secure; the goat surrounded him by speed.

Reformed pastoral: fury is still permitted, not final. Shared: the goat runs at the ram. Not a newspaper.

Daniel 8:7

7I saw him come close to the ram, and he was enraged against him and struck the ram and broke his two horns. And the ram had no power to stand before him, but he cast him down to the ground and trampled on him. And there was no one who could rescue the ram from his power.

Arminian

He comes close, is enraged, strikes the ram, breaks both horns, casts him down, tramples him; none can rescue. Wesley writes no note here; his nearest (v. 6): the ram—the king of Media and Persia. His next (v. 8): when Alexander was greatest, then was he broken.

Arminian pastoral: both horns break; the ram is trampled. Shared: Persia falls to Greece. Stay with Wesley’s Alexander, not a 21st-century horn.

Reformed

Calvin: God showed the victory of Alexander. He struck the ram and broke his two horns; the ram had no power to stand; he threw him prostrate and trod him down; none could deliver. Alexander acquired the Median as well as the Persian empire.

Reformed pastoral: trampling is real and timed. Shared: both horns broken. Not a culture-war on empire.

Daniel 8:8

8Then the goat became exceedingly great, but when he was strong, the great horn was broken, and instead of it there came up four conspicuous horns toward the four winds of heaven.

Arminian

The goat becomes exceedingly great; when he is strong, the great horn is broken; four conspicuous horns toward the four winds of heaven. Wesley: was broken—when Alexander was greatest, then was he broken, and that to pieces, for he, his mother, son, brother, and all his kindred were destroyed. The four winds: Antipater got Greece; Asia was possessed by Antigonus; Ptolemy got Egypt; Seleucus had Babylon and Syria. To the east, Babylon and Syria; to the south, Egypt; to the north, Asia the less; to the west, Greece.

Arminian pastoral: broken at the height, then four. Shared: Alexander falls; four succeed. Not a prophecy-chart of later goats.

Reformed

Calvin: when the he-goat was in his strength, the great horn was broken—Alexander at the height of prosperity, then suddenly snatched away. Four conspicuous horns: Cassander obtained Macedon; Seleucus, Syria; Antigonus, Asia; Ptolemy, Egypt. By the four winds of heaven: west, east, north, south.

Reformed pastoral: greatness does not last. Shared: one horn broken, four toward the four winds. Not a millennial camp-fight.

Daniel 8:9

9Out of one of them came a little horn, which grew exceedingly great toward the south, toward the east, and toward the glorious land.

Arminian

Out of one of them a little horn, growing exceedingly great toward the south, the east, and the glorious land. Wesley: this little horn was Antiochus Epiphanes. The south—Egypt where he besieged and took many places. The east—in Syria, Babylon, Armenia. The pleasant land—Judea, so called because of the temple and people of God in it, and the fruitfulness of it.

Arminian pastoral: Wesley names Antiochus, not a license to invent a modern little horn. Shared: south, east, glorious land. Not a dispensational versus amillennial fight, and not a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: out of one of those four horns a little horn arose—Antiochus Epiphanes is most distinctly pointed out. Little, because no one supposed he would be king. Mighty toward the south (Egypt), the east, and the glory—Judea, the sanctuary of God.

Reformed pastoral: a named tyrant in a named land. Shared with Wesley: Antiochus, toward the glorious land. Do not recast this as a 21st-century candidate.

Daniel 8:10

10It grew great, even to the host of heaven. And some of the host and some of the stars it threw down to the ground and trampled on them.

Arminian

It grew great even to the host of heaven; some of the host and of the stars thrown down and trampled. Wesley: the host of heaven—the church of God militant, who worship the God of heaven, who are citizens of heaven, whose names are written in heaven; and among these the priests, and champions, who were as stars shining above the rest; these he profaned and slew cruelly.

Arminian pastoral: the church is thrown down, not abandoned. Shared: host and stars are God’s people. Not a star-chart of modern politics.

Reformed

Calvin: the horn became magnificent even to the army of the heavens—without doubt the elect people of God. The Church, precious before God though trodden on earth. Some of that army and of the stars cast down and stamped: the tyrant loosed to treat the Church with contempt, as if drawing stars from heaven.

Reformed pastoral: trampling is permitted, not final. Shared: the host is the church. God still numbers his own. Not a prophecy-chart war.

Daniel 8:11

11It became great, even as great as the Prince of the host. And the regular burnt offering was taken away from him, and the place of his sanctuary was overthrown.

Arminian

It became great, even as great as the Prince of the host; the regular burnt offering taken away from him; the place of his sanctuary overthrown. Wesley: the prince—not only against the high-priest, but against God himself. Was cast down—he took away the use of the temple as to the holy service and sacrifices.

Arminian pastoral: Wesley will later (v. 16) name Christ’s voice in this vision; here the Prince is God himself, and the high-priest is not excluded. Do not flatten. Shared: offering taken, sanctuary hurt. The time will be limited.

Reformed

Calvin: some take the prince of the army for the high priest; that is too forced. The true sense: such arrogance that Antiochus declared war with God himself. God is the Prince of the army because he defends his Church. The perpetual sacrifice snatched away; the place of his sanctuary cast down.

Reformed pastoral: the Prince is God; do not flatten him into a mere priest. Shared: sanctuary overthrown, offering taken. God still limits what he permits. Not a millennial camp-fight.

Key contrast Both hear the little horn magnifying itself against the Prince of the host, taking away the regular offering, overthrowing the sanctuary. Arminians typically hear Wesley: not only against the high-priest, but against God himself. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin: the high-priest reading is too forced; the Prince is God, who defends his Church. Neither camp is being asked to flatten the Prince into a mere officer, or to fight a modern prophecy-chart war.

Daniel 8:12

12And a host will be given over to it together with the regular burnt offering because of transgression, and it will throw truth to the ground, and it will act and prosper.

Arminian

A host given over with the regular burnt offering because of transgression; truth thrown to the ground; it acted and prospered. Wesley: by reason of transgression—both the transgression of the priests, and of the people.

Arminian pastoral: prospering is permitted because of sin, not because God has left the temple. Shared: truth thrown down, yet the sanctuary will be restored. Not a newspaper, and not a chart.

Reformed

Calvin: time shall be given him—Antiochus can do nothing unless divinely permitted and limited. In sin: the Jews must feel this as the reward of their sins. He casts truth to the ground and prospers. God moderates the evil by bounding the time, and humbles the people by naming their transgression.

Reformed pastoral: given over is still given. Shared: transgression, truth down, it prospers for a time. God limits the trampling.

Daniel 8:13

13Then I heard a holy one speaking, and another holy one said to the one who spoke, “For how long is the vision concerning the regular burnt offering, the transgression that makes desolate, and the giving over of the sanctuary and host to be trampled underfoot?”

Arminian

One holy one speaking; another asks how long: the vision concerning the regular burnt offering, the transgression that makes desolate, the sanctuary and host trampled. Wesley: one saint—that is, one holy angel. How long—how long shall Antiochus continue his vexations against the people and prevent the worship of God? This is the treading down of the sanctuary, and the host.

Arminian pastoral: the question is mercy: how long, not whether. Shared: trampling is timed. Do not turn “how long” into a date-chart.

Reformed

Calvin: Daniel heard one holy one—an angel—and another asked the Wonderful One. Reason requires a superior glory: Christ, chief of angels, the wonderful. How long will the vision of the perpetual sacrifice and the devastating sin last? How long will sanctuary and host be trodden?

Reformed pastoral: heaven asks for the church’s sake. Shared: how long is a real limit. Not a rapture timetable. The sanctuary will be restored.

Key contrast Both hear a holy one ask how long the offering, the desolating transgression, and the trampling last. Arminians typically hear Wesley: one holy angel asking how long Antiochus will vex the people and prevent worship. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin: an angel inquires of Christ, the Wonderful One, chief of angels. Neither camp is being asked to invent a modern how-long chart.

Daniel 8:14

14And he said to me, “For 2,300 evenings and mornings. Then the sanctuary shall be restored to its rightful state.”

Arminian

For 2,300 evenings and mornings; then the sanctuary shall be restored to its rightful state. Wesley: he—that angel. Then—just so long it was, from the defection of the people, procured by Menelaus, the high-priest, to the cleansing of the sanctuary, and the re-establishment of religion among them.

Arminian pastoral: Wesley counts days until cleansing, not a day-year scheme he does not use. Shared: the time is limited; the sanctuary is restored. God limits the trampling. Not a prophecy-chart war.

Reformed

Calvin: unto evening and morning, two thousand three hundred—not years or months; those who receive it of years and months are mistaken. Natural days: about six years and four months, from Antiochus’s first insult of the worship to the Maccabean purifying of the temple. Then the sanctuary shall be justified—its rights restored.

Reformed pastoral: evenings and mornings, then restoration. Shared: God bounds the time; the sanctuary is restored. Do not invent a day-year chart Wesley and Calvin do not use.

Key contrast Both take 2,300 evenings and mornings as a limited span of days ending in the sanctuary’s restoration, not as a day-year scheme they do not use. Arminians typically hear Wesley: from the defection procured by Menelaus to the cleansing and the re-establishment of religion. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin: natural days, about six years and four months, from the first pollution to the temple’s purifying; then the sanctuary is justified. Shared: God limits the trampling; the sanctuary is restored.

Reformed Notable voices

The ram represents the Persian and Median empire; two horns for two empires united under one sovereignty; the Persian horn higher, raised afterwards.
