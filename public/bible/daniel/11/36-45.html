Passage

Daniel 11

Verses 36–45 · ESV · Voices

The vision closes the king who does as he wills: he exalts himself above every god, honors a god of fortresses, overflows countries, enters the glorious land, and pitches palatial tents between the sea and the glorious holy mountain—yet he comes to his end, with none to help him. The angel tells it so the church may wait the appointed indignation, not a newspaper map of later nations.

Daniel 11:36

36And the king shall do as he wills. He shall exalt himself and magnify himself above every god, and shall speak astonishing things against the God of gods. He shall prosper till the indignation is accomplished; for what is decreed shall be done.

Arminian

Wesley: the king—Antiochus was an eminent type of antichrist; to whom many things that follow may be applied by way of accommodation: although they principally refer to Antiochus, and had their primary accomplishment in him. For that that is determined—that which God hath decreed to be done by him shall be done; and that which God hath purposed to be done upon him. “Do as he wills” names a real, blaspheming will; the primary stage is still this king.

Arminian reading of the range: type is not a blank check for later newspapers. Accommodation may apply some lines to antichrist; Wesley will not let that cancel the historic Epiphanes. Exalting himself is responsible pride. The church is not to treat the verse as a key to modern nations. The verse is theology of a self-magnifying king under a decree that both uses him and ends him.

Reformed

Calvin will not apply the verse to Antiochus: he did not long survive the pollution of the Temple, and the following events do not suit that hour. By “king” the angel means an empire—the Romans—whether senate, consuls, or proconsuls. He shall magnify himself against all deities and speak wonderful things against the God of gods. He shall prosper till the end of the indignation: until God should punish the hypocrites, and thus humble his Church.

Reformed accent: the angel passes from Antiochus to Rome so the pious would not think God had gone silent between the Maccabees and Christ. Appointment bounds the blasphemy. The church is not asked to master later maps, but to know that even this pride was told beforehand, and that indignation has a consummation God has already determined.

Key contrast Both refuse to treat vv. 36–45 as a chart of modern nations. Wesley: they principally refer to Antiochus and had their primary accomplishment in him; he is an eminent type of antichrist by accommodation. Calvin: the king is not that Syrian but the Roman Empire as a kingdom, named so the pious would hold out until Christ’s advent. They split on which historical power the self-exalting king is.

Daniel 11:37

37He shall pay no attention to the gods of his fathers, or to the one beloved by women. He shall not pay attention to any other god, for he shall magnify himself above all.

Arminian

Wesley does not pause on this verse, but his map of the chapter still has Antiochus in view: the king who set Jupiter in the sanctuary (v. 31) now magnifies himself above all. Paying no attention is a chosen contempt, not a puppet string. The one beloved by women is left unnamed in Wesley; the point is the self-magnifying will.

Arminian pastoral: a man can refuse the gods of his fathers and still be fully responsible. Magnifying himself above all is sin God reports and bounds (v. 36), not sin God authors. The church is not to invent a later identity for “the one beloved by women,” nor to treat self-exaltation as fate. The same decree that lets him prosper also appoints what shall be done upon him.

Reformed

Calvin: this king should be a gross despiser of all deities, fierce and barbarous against all mortals, because he should magnify himself above them all. The desire of women he reads as a figure for humanity itself: God has appointed that affection as a natural bond; the angel means a ruler utterly devoid of piety and of charity. Pride so blinded the Romans as to cause them to forget both piety and humanity.

Reformed note: contempt of the gods of the fathers is not a private hobby. It is the empire’s manner. The church under such a power is to see that self-magnifying is already in the word, and that the absence of natural affection is part of the scourge God had named—not a riddle for later courts.

Daniel 11:38

38He shall honor the god of fortresses instead of these. A god whom his fathers did not know he shall honor with gold and silver, with precious stones and costly gifts.

Arminian

Wesley: in his estate—in the room of his father’s god. The God of forces—this seems to be Jupiter Olympius, never introduced among the Syrians, till Antiochus did it.

A new god, paid in gold and stones: worship is redirected, not abolished. Arminian reading keeps Wesley’s identification in that generation. Fathers did not know this god because Antiochus introduced him. Costly gifts are a responsible policy. The church is not to treat “god of fortresses” as a cipher for later war-states. The verse is theology of idolatry: when a king honors force as god, the sanctuary already profaned (v. 31) is not an accident.

Reformed

Calvin: at first glance the king despises all deities and yet worships a certain god. This agrees with the Romans, who treated worship as business and were destitute of any perception of the divinity. They claimed a divine power as their own. The god of bulwarks is that perverse confidence by which they were puffed up. They limited Jupiter to his own capitol; nothing was esteemed divine but what pleased themselves. He shall honor him in his own place, with gold and silver and precious stones.

Reformed pastoral: the angel names a worship that is really self-worship. Riches heaped on temples do not prove piety. The church is to see the capitol-god as the word already described, and to refuse both envy of the gold and the habit of relocating this god onto later flags.

Daniel 11:39

39He shall deal with the strongest fortresses with the help of a foreign god. Those who acknowledge him he shall load with honor. He shall make them rulers over many and shall divide the land for a price.

Arminian

Wesley: with a strange god—using all art and authority to propagate his worship. Fortresses fall with a foreign god’s help; acknowledgers are loaded with honor; land is divided for a price.

Art and authority to propagate worship: this is mission for an idol, not mere logistics. Arminian accent: buying men with land and honor is a chosen policy. Those who acknowledge him could have refused. The church learns that strange worship travels with spoil, and that a price on land is still a moral fact. Wesley keeps the strange god as the worship Antiochus pushed. Faith is not required to re-map “divide the land” onto later markets.

Reformed

Calvin: he shall multiply the glory—acquire territories, power, treasures. He shall make them rule far and wide, and divide the soil for a price: not a defined sum only, but avarice that sells what it cannot usefully keep. Allies are enriched from another’s property; kingdoms are bestowed and stripped. The strange god is the divinity that rests on the opinion and will of the sovereign people.

Reformed consolation: even this success is already written, so that when land is sold and rulers made, the pious will not think heaven has changed sides. Price is the empire’s; the limit remains God’s. The church waits the end of v. 45, not a later ledger of provinces.

Daniel 11:40

40At the time of the end, the king of the south shall attack him, but the king of the north shall rush upon him like a whirlwind, with chariots and horsemen, and with many ships. And he shall come into countries and shall overflow and pass through.

Arminian

Wesley is silent here, yet his heading still places vv. 30–43 as the mischief Antiochus would do to the Jews. South attacks; north rushes like a whirlwind; countries overflow. The flood-verb of the earlier wars returns.

Arminian reading: “time of the end” in this chapter is the appointed close of this king’s career (vv. 35–36, 45), not a calendar for later blocs. Chariots, horsemen, ships are creaturely means. Overflow is a campaign the church between the armies must endure. The verse does not invite renaming south and north every generation. Wesley’s “principally Antiochus” still governs: the whirlwind is that hour’s war under the decree of v. 36.

Reformed

Calvin has already said the long series of victories prevents applying this stretch to Antiochus, who died soon after spoiling the Temple. The appointed time now shows Egypt thrusting and the northern power—Rome in his reading—coming like a flood with ships as well as horse. Overflow and pass through is the same flood-language the angel used of earlier armies, now stretched over a monarchy, not a single madman.

Reformed note: “time of the end” is still God’s clock, the consummation of indignation (v. 36), not a newspaper. The church is to recognize providence in the whirlwind. South and north remain the historic pair that sit on either side of the glorious land.

Daniel 11:41

41He shall come into the glorious land. And tens of thousands shall fall, but these shall be delivered out of his hand: Edom and Moab and the main part of the Ammonites.

Arminian

Wesley: the children of Ammon—he will not hurt them; because they helped him against the Jews. The glorious land is still Judea, as in v. 16. Tens of thousands fall there; neighboring peoples who sided against the covenant people escape.

Arminian pastoral: deliverance of Edom, Moab, and Ammon is a historical exemption, not a virtue. Help against the Jews is a chosen alliance, and it is remembered. The land God made glorious is again under a destroyer. Faith lives there without baptizing the escapees, and without stretching “glorious land” onto later maps. Wesley keeps the neighbors on that generation’s border.

Reformed

Calvin: the Romans come to the mountain of the desire of holiness so the novelty of a distant nation trampling the church would not drive the pious to despair. Nothing happens by chance; turbulent motions are governed by a divine power. Tens of thousands fall in the land God chose; some neighboring peoples are not in the same path of the army.

Reformed accent: the epithet “glorious” still rests on God’s choice of dwelling, not on the land’s safety. The church is to read the fall of thousands as a scourge already named, and the exceptions as the angel’s precision—not as a code for later tribes. The same word that lets the land be entered has already said the invader will come to his end (v. 45).

Daniel 11:42

42He shall stretch out his hand against the countries, and the land of Egypt shall not escape.

Arminian

Egypt shall not escape: the south that opened the chapter (v. 5) is still on the page at the end. Stretching out the hand is a real reach of power. Wesley’s silence here does not move the verse off Antiochus; his heading runs the mischief against the Jews through v. 43, with Egypt’s treasures in the next line.

Arminian reading: “shall not escape” is the truth of that campaign. Countries are not ciphers. The church is not to treat Egypt as a standing symbol to be reassigned, nor to deny that a king’s hand really stretched. Escape is refused because the decree of v. 36 still stands. Patience, not speculation, is the work.

Reformed

Calvin’s Roman reading keeps Egypt in its place on the map: the south still thrust (v. 40), and it does not escape the northern power that overflowed. Stretching out the hand is the empire’s reach after the land of desire has been entered.

Reformed pastoral: the angel names Egypt so the pious who remember the earlier Egyptian wars will know the story has not slipped into another book. Appointment, not chance, governs who does not escape. The church waits the treasures of v. 43 and the helpless end of v. 45 without needing a later Egypt to make the sentence true.

Daniel 11:43

43He shall become ruler of the treasures of gold and of silver, and all the precious things of Egypt, and the Libyans and the Cushites shall follow in his train.

Arminian

Wesley: at his steps—he had them at his foot, at his beck. Gold, silver, precious things of Egypt; Libyans and Cushites in the train. Booty and followers are historical.

Arminian note: becoming ruler of treasures is a will that takes, and a people who can be made to follow. Beck is not worship. The church is warned that wealth moving south to north does not prove the taker is the Lord’s anointed. Wesley keeps the train on that king’s road. Faith refuses to turn Libyans and Cushites into later blocs. Precious things test kings; they do not rewrite the covenant.

Reformed

Calvin has already shown how Rome drew gold, silver, and desirable things into its temples and treasury, spoiling provinces to ornament the city. Egypt’s precious things and the peoples in the train fit an empire that sold land for a price (v. 39) and honored the god of ramparts with what it seized.

Reformed accent: the train is still under the word. Following in his steps is not the last word of the chapter. The church is to see providence in the flow of treasure, and to remember that the same angel who grants this rule has already prepared the alarm of v. 44 and the end with none to help.

Daniel 11:44

44But news from the east and the north shall alarm him, and he shall go out with great fury to destroy and devote many to destruction.

Arminian

Wesley’s heading: his fall, vv. 44–45. News alarms; fury goes out to destroy and devote many. The king who did as he willed (v. 36) is now a man who can be frightened.

Arminian theology: alarm is a real passion, fury a real choice. East and north are directions from which reports come in that hour, not a standing code. Devote many to destruction is holy-war language in a pagan mouth: he would put under the ban what is not his to ban. The church is not to hunt later rumors to fulfill the verse. Wesley is about to say God cuts him off. Fury does not outrun that cutting.

Reformed

Calvin: if the Romans were sometimes in difficulties, they soon recovered their usual success—until the indignation was complete. Alarm from east and north, then a going out in fury, belongs to a long monarchy that could be checked and still rage.

Reformed note: the angel already joined alarm to fury so the pious would not think a rumor meant the vision had failed, nor think fury meant God had lost the bridle. Destroying many is still bounded by v. 45. The church hears the news as part of the decreed indignation, not as a chart of later fronts.

Daniel 11:45

45And he shall pitch his palatial tents between the sea and the glorious holy mountain. Yet he shall come to his end, with none to help him.

Arminian

Wesley: none shall help him—God shall cut him off in the midst of his days. And when he destroys, who can help? Palatial tents between the sea and the glorious holy mountain: the king plants himself as if he owned the land of desire, then ends without an ally.

Arminian close: the midst of his days is a historical cutting off of Antiochus, the primary referent Wesley named at v. 36. Help fails because God destroys. The church is not to keep the tents standing by inventing a later pitch. When he destroys, who can help? That is the theology of the verse. Faith learns the end of self-exaltation without needing a modern “north” to make the sentence true.

Reformed

Calvin: they shall fix the tents of the palace between the seas, near the mountain of the desire of holiness—not a night’s camp only, but settled sway in the east without making Asia the seat of empire. The consolation follows: they shall come at length to their end, and no one shall bring them help. This was not fulfilled in a single hour; the monarchy could still flourish, the city be razed, and yet the end come. Nothing happens by chance; all these motions are governed by a divine power.

Reformed pastoral: pitching tents at the holy mountain is permitted; remaining there is not. None shall help him is the angel’s last word on the willful king. The church’s hope is not that empires last, but that the indignation is appointed, and that the God who named the mountain glorious also names the invader’s end.

Key contrast Both end the chapter with a helpless death under God’s hand, not with a key to later states. Arminians typically follow Wesley: God cuts Antiochus off in the midst of his days; when He destroys, who can help? Reformed readers typically follow Calvin: the Roman power pitches palace-tents at the holy mountain and still comes to its end with none to help, so the pious would know the trampling was not chance. Neither camp needs a later millennial scheme to hear that verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the willful king, the god of fortresses, and an end with none to help—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Daniel 11:36–45.

Arminian Notable voices

“The king—Antiochus was an eminent type of antichrist; to whom many things that follow may be applied by way of accommodation: altho’ they principally refer to Antiochus, and had their primary accomplishment in him. For that that is determined—That which God hath decreed to be done by him shall be done; and that which God hath purposed to be done upon him. None shall help him—God shall cut him off in the midst of his days. And when he destroys, who can help?”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Dan 11:36, 45)

Reformed Notable voices

“I apply it entirely to the Roman Empire. By the word “king” I do not think a single person indicated, but an empire, whatever be its government. The angel, then, when saying, a king shall do anything, does not allude to Antiochus, for all history refutes this. God desired to support the spirits of the pious, lest they should be overwhelmed by the massacres which awaited them and the whole Church even to the advent of Christ. It was not sufficient to predict the occurrences under the tyranny of Antiochus; the time which intervened between the Maccabees and the manifestation of Christ ought not to be omitted. He shall prosper until the consumption of the indignation, since the determination has been made.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Daniel (on Dan 11:36)
