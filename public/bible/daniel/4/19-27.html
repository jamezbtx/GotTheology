Passage

Daniel 4

Verses 19–27 · WEB · Voices

Daniel is dismayed, then interprets: the tree is the king; a watcher has decreed he will be driven to the beasts until he knows the Most High rules in the stump means the kingdom will be confirmed when he knows Heaven rules. Counsel: break off sins by righteousness and mercy to the poor. Perhaps there may be a lengthening of prosperity.

Daniel 4:19

19Then Daniel, whose name was Belteshazzar, was stricken mute for a while, and his thoughts troubled him. The king answered, “Belteshazzar, don’t let the dream or the interpretation trouble you.” Belteshazzar answered, “My lord, may the dream be for those who hate you, and its interpretation to your adversaries.

Arminian

Daniel dismayed; the king tells him not to fear; Daniel wishes the dream on enemies. Wesley: he foresaw troubles on a king he reverenced; what address and excellent spirit in this short preface. Speak out, let the event be what it will.

Daniel feels pity for a tyrant. Jeremiah had bid them pray for Babylon’s peace. Loyalty under the Most High is. The interpretation will still be plain.

Reformed

Calvin: sorrow, not ecstasy. He was bound to pray for him; until the seventy years, not lawful to invoke wrath. Faithful to his king though a tyrant. The pious fear God’s judgments even when spared; the impious sleep when threatened. He does not turn aside from unsparing reproof.

The Prophet’s middle course—humanity without flattery. On this both agree: alarmed, then he speaks. The tree is next.

Daniel 4:20

20The tree that you saw, which grew and was strong, whose height reached to the sky and its sight to all the earth;

Arminian

The tree you saw: grown strong, top to heaven, visible to the end of the earth. Daniel recites the dream before naming its meaning. Shared with v. 22: this height is the king’s.

He walks the king back through his own vision. The recital is a means of helping the king hear. The Most High still rules the tree that reached.

Reformed

Calvin: he follows up with perseverance. Calls him lord with cordiality; meanwhile ambassador of the Supreme King, discourse above the king’s command.

The figure is not in doubt. The tree is the same one the king saw.

Daniel 4:21

21whose leaves were beautiful and its fruit plentiful, and in it was food for all; under which the animals of the field lived, and on whose branches the birds of the sky had their habitation—

Arminian

The gifts of 4:12 now sit on the man who must hear “it is you.”

Usefulness under a flawed crown. Shelter does not undo the chop. No slogan about empire as such.

Reformed

Calvin: political order as trees on whose fruit all feed. God’s blessing in princes even when they neglect duty; grace not all extinguished.

Ordinance, not this man’s equity. Food for all.

Daniel 4:22

22it is you, O king, that have grown and become strong; for your greatness has grown, and reaches to the sky, and your dominion to the end of the earth.

Arminian

It is you, O king. Wesley: thou art high and mighty in the majesty which God hath given thee; to the Caspian, Euxine, Aegean, Mediterranean.

“You”—no riddle left. Gifted majesty, growth. The Most High who gives (4:17) named the giver in the height. Agency of conquest under that gift.

Reformed

Calvin: he does not speak with hesitation or obscurity. Thou art the king. Why? Thou hast become great and strong; magnitude extended to the heavens, power to the end of the earth.

The interpretation is the decree already. Shared with Wesley: it is you. The watcher-sentence follows.

Daniel 4:23

23“Whereas the king saw a holy watcher coming down from the sky and saying, ‘Cut down the tree, and destroy it; nevertheless leave the stump of its roots in the earth, even with a band of iron and bronze, in the tender grass of the field, and let it be wet with the dew of the sky. Let his portion be with the animals of the field, until seven times pass over him.’

Arminian

A watcher from heaven: chop down, leave the stump, dew, beasts, seven times. Recital of 4:13–16 as the king’s own sight. Both readings: stump is mercy, not annihilation.

“Watcher”—heaven’s word, already heard. Seven times still to be lived. The king remains a man under the sentence.

Reformed

Calvin: angels called watchers, prompt to fulfill bidding. Seven times—long; he wished to put forth an example for all ages. Cut down, yet root remains.

The same host that subscribed in 4:17. Chop, and leave the stump.

Daniel 4:24

24“This is the interpretation, O king, and it is the decree of the Most High, which has come on my lord the king:

Arminian

This is the interpretation: the decree of the Most High on my lord the king. Wesley’s thundering peal is coming; here the source is named. Not the watchers instead of God—the Most High decrees.

The decree is a sentence on a lord. Daniel still says “my lord.” Authority of office under a higher decree.

Reformed

Calvin: judgment overhanging. The message sorrowful; kings hardly bear being cited before God’s tribunal. He still elevates the Word above the king.

“Decree”—Most High, not fortune. On my lord the king.

Daniel 4:25

25You will be driven from men and your dwelling shall be with the animals of the field. You will be made to eat grass as oxen, and will be wet with the dew of the sky, and seven times shall pass over you, until you know that the Most High rules in the kingdom of men, and gives it to whomever he will.

Arminian

Driven from men; dwell with beasts; eat grass; wet with dew; seven times; till you know the Most High rules in the kingdom of men, and gives it to whomever he will. Wesley: a thundering peal; wonderful the king could hear it without fury, yet the Lord withheld him. Seven years. Till thou know—how hard for lofty princes to learn this lesson.

The goal of the judgment is knowledge: “until you know that the Most High rules.” Arminians read the seven times as discipline with a door in it—humiliation, aimed at a change of mind.

Reformed

Calvin: they shall cast thee out from among men; habitation with beasts; grass like an ox. Liberty of the Prophet: he shuts his eyes to worldly grandeur. Until thou acknowledge how great a Lord in the kingdom of men, who delivers it to whomsoever He will. Difficult to attribute supreme power to God; especially when raised, we snatch His honor. Violent medicine to learn humility.

Effectual teaching by the rod. He actually gives the kingdom. Wesley agrees: seven times, and the Most High rules in the stump’s mercy is v. 26.

Daniel 4:26

26Whereas it was commanded to leave the stump of the roots of the tree, your kingdom will be restored to you after you learn that Heaven rules.

Arminian

The stump: your kingdom will be restored from the time you learn that Heaven rules. Wesley: that God who sits in heaven rules over all. Held in common: the stump is mercy. Restoration is tied to knowing, not to a bargain that skips the seven times.

Return of a kingdom when he knows. Heaven rules—the same as 4:17. Knowing is a human act under that rule, not empty theater.

Reformed

Calvin: God did not treat him so severely as to give no way to clemency. He mitigates extreme rigor to induce him to call upon God and repent, through hope of pardon. Not merely moderate station but former dignity. General doctrine: we are invited to repentance when God puts an end to chastisements. From which thou shalt know there is power in heaven—promise of spiritual grace; he would never have acquired this of his own accord unless touched by the Spirit.

Mercy in the root, and the knowing itself is gift. Both traditions agree: stump, and Heaven rules. Counsel to break off sins is next—not a fake warning.

Daniel 4:27

27Therefore, O king, let my counsel be acceptable to you, and break off your sins by righteousness, and your iniquities by showing mercy to the poor. Perhaps there may be a lengthening of your tranquility.”

Arminian

Perhaps there may be a lengthening of your tranquility. Wesley: if it may be—Daniel was not certain of pardon for him, nor did he altogether despair of it. Wisdom and tenderness, and yet plainness. The call to repent is earnest. Alms here are mercy to the oppressed, not a works-gospel invented against a fake warning.

A counsel a king can take or refuse. Righteousness and mercy are deeds under grace, testimonies of turning, not a purchase of the seven times. Wesley’s “if it may be” keeps the lengthening uncertain: God may pity; Daniel will not promise what he does not know. No Pelagian leftover—the king already lives under the Most High’s decree—and no despair that counsel is pointless.

Reformed

Calvin: may my counsel please thee; break off thy sins by righteousness and pity to the poor. Exhortation to repentance; Prophets touch outward duties of charity as fruits, not as the root. True justice is pity—not papal satisfactions or works of supererogation. We do not redeem sin before God as if beneficence were expiation; we may restore what we took before men. The decree stands, yet the warning treats men kindly; Ahab’s case shows God sometimes tempers the rod when even signs of turning appear. The medicine is to cease from errors and open the eyes.

The call is made in earnest, the lengthening is God’s to give, not alms buying a quieter year. Calvin refuses a merit-reading of the poor. Wesley agrees: repent, show mercy, do not despair. They part on how sure the “perhaps” is, and how far alms reach.

Key contrast Both take the call at face value: the tree is the king, the stump is mercy, break off sins. Neither a works-gospel that purchases the kingdom nor a fake warning. Wesley hears “if it may be” as uncertainty of pardon without despair. Calvin hears righteousness and mercy to the poor as the fruit and medicine of repentance, not satisfaction before God; any lengthening is God’s tempering of the rod.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

What older interpreters in each camp said about a tree that is the king, a stump left in mercy, and counsel to break off sins.

Arminian Notable voices

“Troubled him—because he fore-saw such troubles coming upon the king for whom he had a high reverence. Let not the dream trouble thee—speak out, let the event be what it will. They shall drive thee—this was such a thundering peal, that it was wonderful the king could endure to hear it without fury boiling in his heart, yet the Lord with-held him. Seven times—seven years. Till thou know—how hard is it for lofty princes to learn this lesson. If it may be—Daniel was not certain of pardon for him, nor did he altogether despair of it. With what wisdom and tenderness does he speak: and yet with what plainness.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Dan 4:19, 25, 27)

Reformed Notable voices

“May my counsel please thee, and break off thy sins by righteousness, and thy iniquities by pity to the poor. Daniel wished to exhort the king to repentance; works are the testimonies to real repentance. True justice consists in pity towards the poor—not works of supererogation, not a satisfaction that redeems fault or penalty before God. The call is real: he might obtain some pardon, even if he had paid the penalty; God always warns by threatening for the purpose of treating men more kindly. We ought always to be prepared to suffer God’s chastisements; yet it is no slight alleviation when we are persuaded of his desire to be propitious.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Daniel (on Dan 4:27)
