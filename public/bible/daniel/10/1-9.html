Passage

Daniel 10

Verses 1–9 · ESV · Voices

In the third year of Cyrus a true word about a great conflict is revealed to Daniel, named Belteshazzar. He mourns three weeks, stands by the Tigris, and sees a man clothed in linen whose glory leaves him alone, strengthless, and fallen on his face.

Daniel 10:1

1In the third year of Cyrus king of Persia a word was revealed to Daniel, who was named Belteshazzar. And the word was true, and it was a great conflict. And he understood the word and had understanding of the vision.

Arminian

A word is revealed in Cyrus’s third year, and it is true. Belteshazzar is the court-name; Daniel is still the prophet. Wesley treats chapters 10–12 as one vision: this chapter is introductory humiliation. “Great conflict” is the burden of the word for the people—not a headline to pin on today’s maps.

Arminian reading: God reveals, and Daniel actually understands. The gift is real; so is the hearer. Revelation is not a private cipher. Grace opens the word to a servant who must still receive it.

Reformed

Calvin: the word itself was most true, although the time of fulfillment should be long. Certainty does not mean haste. If a promise tarries, wait for it. The double name is a monument among Medes and Persians that this Belteshazzar still belongs to Israel’s God. Understanding accredits what follows.

Reformed reading: God gives a stable word and a long horizon. “He understood” invites the church to ask for light rather than to despair at first obscurity. The conflict is in His timetable, not ours to chart.

Daniel 10:2

2In those days I, Daniel, was mourning for three weeks.

Arminian

“I, Daniel, was mourning.” Wesley: because he foresaw calamities that would befall the Jews for their sins, especially for destroying the Messiah and rejecting his gospel. Three ordinary weeks of days—not the sevens of chapter 9. Grief is love that will not treat coming sorrow as a rumor.

Arminian pastoral: mourning is a responsible act of a man who sets his heart to understand. God does not mock such grief. Grace enables the seeking it also requires. Sorrow for the people is holy work, not unbelief.

Reformed

Calvin locates the grief in Cyrus’s third year: rebuilding stalled, freedom looking like a taunt, the second temple’s promised glory seeming a mockery. Daniel mourns because the church’s wound is real. God is not absent from the three weeks; He will later say the words were heard from day one.

Reformed pastoral: the prophet’s tears sit inside providence. Mourning does not move God from indifference to care; it is care’s shape in a servant He already loves. The weeks will be explained as hidden help, not deafness.

Daniel 10:3

3I ate no delicacies, no meat or wine entered my mouth, nor did I anoint myself at all, for the full three weeks.

Arminian

No delicacies, no meat, no wine, no oil—for the full three weeks. Wesley’s Daniel is a man in solemn humiliation. The body is enlisted: appetite is set down so the heart can stay with God’s people in their trouble. This is not a diet for display.

Arminian note: fasting is a real offering of the self, enabled by grace and still actually done. Daniel could have kept a courtier’s table; he did not. Hunger does not purchase visions; sincere seeking is still required.

Reformed

Calvin will not let the passage become a rule for distinguishing flesh, eggs, and fish. Real fasting is a bridle on lusts, content with what necessity requires—plain bread, not exquisite viands under a thinner name. Daniel did not trifle with God. The point is grief for the church and intentness in prayer, not a ladder of merit.

Reformed note: God is not paid by skipped meals. Abstinence trains the prophet He intends to speak to. Ordinary temperance is the church’s life; this fast is extra sorrow when the people are in danger.

Daniel 10:4

4On the twenty-fourth day of the first month, as I was standing on the bank of the great river (that is, the Tigris)

Arminian

The twenty-fourth of the first month—Wesley: Nisan, which is March. Hiddekel is the Tigris. The vision has a bank and a calendar. Daniel is standing, not sleeping his way into a private dream. Place and date serve the word that will follow.

Arminian reading: God meets a mourner who has kept the weeks and now stands by the river. The location does not save him; it locates the gift. Revelation comes to a man already humbling himself in history.

Reformed

Calvin names the first month as March and Hiddekel as the Tigris, and he allows that the prophet may have been rapt in spirit rather than physically posted on the bank. Either way the scene is God’s: a great river, a dated day, a man prepared through three weeks of grief.

Reformed accent: the geography is the Lord’s stage. Whether Daniel’s feet or only his sight stood at the Tigris, the vision is not his invention. God sets the prophet where the word will be accredited.

Daniel 10:5

5I lifted up my eyes and looked, and behold, a man clothed in linen, with a belt of fine gold from Uphaz around his waist.

Arminian

A man clothed in linen, a belt of fine gold from Uphaz. Wesley: “Very probably Christ, who appeared to Daniel in royal and priestly robes, and in so great brightness and majesty.” Linen and gold are not costume trivia; they are how glory is shown to a mourner. Wesley calls verses 4–9 a glorious appearance of the Son of God.

Classical Arminians often receive this figure as the Priest-King meeting a seeker. Majesty is real; so is the grace that shows it. The man stands, belted, before Daniel. This is not a mood and not a newspaper omen.

Reformed

Calvin refuses to make the linen Christ’s priesthood or the girdle an emblem of rigor—“mere refinements… destitute of all reality.” Angels are called men when God clothes them for a time. The marks distinguish this messenger from mortals and accredit the prophecy that follows; they do not turn the angel into the incarnate Son.

Reformed reading: God accommodates Himself to our senses. The man is truly seen and truly more than a man, yet Calvin will reserve “Christ” for Michael later in the chapter. Sober Scripture, not distant allegory.

Key contrast Both receive the linen-clad figure as heavenly, not a mere courtier by the river. Arminians following Wesley typically identify him as the Son of God in royal and priestly majesty. Reformed readers following Calvin typically identify him as an angel sent in human form, whose splendor certifies the coming word rather than revealing the incarnate Christ.

Daniel 10:6

6His body was like beryl, his face like the appearance of lightning, his eyes like flaming torches, his arms and legs like the gleam of burnished bronze, and the sound of his words like the sound of a multitude.

Arminian

Beryl, lightning, torches, burnished bronze, a voice like a multitude. If Wesley is right that this is Christ, the lines match other theophanies: glory that sounds like many, eyes that search. The mourner is being shown why later fear is reasonable.

Arminian pastoral: the details are given so we will not shrink the person. Brightness is mercy to a prophet who asked to understand; it is also a check on casual reading. No need to turn the catalogue into a chart of latter-day symbols.

Reformed

Calvin takes the colors and the voice as fixed marks that this is an angel, not an earthly mortal. Sky-colored body, face like lightning, words like a tumult: God separates the messenger from the crowd so Daniel—and we—will treat the next chapters as His.

Reformed note: the catalogue is credential, not a speculative key. God wanted confidence in the prophecies that follow. The sound of a multitude is still one speaker. Majesty serves the word.

Daniel 10:7

7And I, Daniel, alone saw the vision, for the men who were with me did not see the vision, but a great trembling fell upon them, and they fled to hide themselves.

Arminian

Daniel alone saw; the men with him did not, yet trembling fell on them and they fled. Revelation can be particular without being imaginary. Companions may feel dread they cannot interpret. The prophet is left with the sight he sought.

Arminian note: God may grant seeing to the one who has sought, while others taste only the fringe of glory. Their flight is a real human act under holy fear. Daniel does not congratulate himself; he is about to collapse too.

Reformed

God opens what He will. The others are not shown the vision, yet they are not untouched: a great trembling falls, and they hide. Calvin’s larger point in the chapter is that these outward signs train reverence and accredit the word. Particular seeing is God’s choice, not Daniel’s technique.

Reformed pastoral: you may stand near a work of God and still not be given the sight. Fear that flees is not the same as the fear that stays and hears. The Lord remains free with His visions.

Daniel 10:8

8So I was left alone and saw this great vision, and no strength was left in me. My radiant appearance was fearfully changed, and I retained no strength.

Arminian

Left alone, he sees—and no strength is left. Radiance turns toward ruin. A holy man can be undone by glory. Wesley’s mourner is not a stoic; the vision is “great,” and the body tells the truth.

Arminian pastoral: seeking God does not make the seeker equal to God. Daniel retained no strength: the confession is honest. Grace will raise him later; it does not pretend he stood tall. Weakness is the right creaturely answer, not a failure to believe hard enough.

Reformed

Calvin: this language assures us Daniel did not write rash comments; he was taught by the angel. He was reduced toward lifelessness because it is profitable—carnal feelings subdued, pride prostrate. God does not terrify sons as if He delighted in disquiet, but because we will not be docile until resistance gives way.

Reformed accent: the changed face is pedagogy. True hearing begins when our vigor is not the point. God blasts self-reliance so the prophet can become a faithful interpreter.

Daniel 10:9

9Then I heard the sound of his words, and as I heard the sound of his words, I fell on my face in deep sleep with my face to the ground.

Arminian

He hears the sound of the words, and hearing itself floors him: face to the ground, a deep sleep. The voice like a multitude now lands. Daniel does not negotiate his posture. Falling is what glory does to a man still in the flesh.

Classical Arminians: the same grace that granted the sight now grants the fall. This is not apathy; it is overwhelmed love and fear. God will touch and raise him in the next verses. Until then the right theology is on the ground.

Reformed

The sound of the words is enough. Calvin has already said God prepares the prophet by taking vigor away; here the taking is complete. Deep sleep with the face in the dust is the creature before the Holy. The word that will say “fear not” has not yet been spoken, and that order matters.

Reformed comfort: God can floor those He intends to use. The sleep is not abandonment; it is the last beat of this scene before a hand touches him. Authority for the coming word is being written on a man who cannot lift his own head.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on three weeks of mourning, a man clothed in linen by the Tigris, and a prophet fallen on his face—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Daniel 10:1–9.

Arminian Notable voices

“This chapter and the two next make one entire vision and prophecy, given Daniel about two years after the former. This chapter is introductory: the next has the prophecy itself, and the twelfth chapter, the conclusion of it. In this we have Daniel's solemn humiliation, ver. 1-3. A glorious appearance of the Son of God, ver. 4-9. Was mourning—Because he foresaw the many calamities that would befall the Jews for their sins, especially for destroying the Messiah, and rejecting his gospel. A certain man—Very probably Christ, who appeared to Daniel in royal and priestly robes, and in so great brightness and majesty.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Dan 10 intro, 2, 5)

Reformed Notable voices

“He calls him a man, but shews him to be endued, or adorned with attributes which inspire full confidence in his celestial glory. We have elsewhere stated, how angels are called men, whenever God wished them to put on this outward form. Daniel therefore says, he saw a man, and afterwards distinguishes him from the human race, and shews fixed and conspicuous marks inscribed upon him, which discover him to be an angel sent down from heaven, and not a mere earthly mortal. Some refer the linen garment to the priesthood of Christ, and treat the girdle as an emblem of rigor. But these are mere refinements, and seem to me destitute of all reality. Nothing can be better than a sober treatment of Scripture.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Daniel (on Dan 10:5–6)
