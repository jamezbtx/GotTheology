Passage

Daniel 6

Verses 1–10 · ESV · Voices

Darius sets 120 satraps over the kingdom and three high officials over them; Daniel is one of the three and is distinguished, so the king plans to set him over the whole realm. Enemies find no complaint except concerning the law of his God. They procure a thirty-day interdict: whoever petitions any god or man except the king is cast into the den of lions. The king signs. When Daniel knew the document was signed, he went to his house—windows open toward Jerusalem—knelt three times a day, prayed and gave thanks before his God, as he had done previously.

Daniel 6:1

1It pleased Darius to set over the kingdom 120 satraps, to be throughout the whole kingdom;

Arminian

It pleased Darius to set 120 satraps throughout the whole kingdom. Wesley’s nearest (v. 2): Daniel will be first among the presidents—Belshazzar had promised him the third place; the first was general of the army, the second president of the palace, the third of the land and provinces.

Arminian pastoral: a real king really orders his realm. Civil structure is not the enemy of faith. Daniel will serve inside it without treating the throne as God, and without despising the office Romans 13 and Titus 3 tell the church to honor.

Reformed

Calvin: God cared for his Prophet, not so much for private respect as that the wretched captives and exiles should be benefited. We may deservedly call Daniel God’s hand in sustaining the Jews. The Persians, being barbarians, were not naturally more merciful; God interposed his servant.

Reformed pastoral: the satrap-list is providence, not luck. Empires change hands; the Most High still rules. Public order is the stage on which God keeps his people—not a culture-war, and not a blank check for the king to become a god.

Daniel 6:2

2and over them three high officials, of whom Daniel was one, to whom these satraps should give account, so that the king might suffer no loss.

Arminian

Three high officials; Daniel is one; satraps give account, that the king suffer no loss. Wesley: Daniel was first. Belshazzar’s promise was third ruler in the kingdom; the first was general of the army, the second president of the palace, the third of the land and provinces.

Arminian pastoral: he takes the office. Faithful administration is not compromise. The same man who will keep praying also keeps the king from loss—the public good of Romans 13, not a plot and not a riot.

Reformed

Calvin: a multitude of governors would have brought confusion and loss; the king placed three prefects over the hundred and twenty. Darius would never have noticed a stranger and a captive unless he understood him to be a known Prophet of God, a herald in denouncing destruction on Belshazzar.

Reformed pastoral: God provided the office so the Jews should not be more oppressed by a change of masters. The king’s “no loss” is a real civic good; the deeper cause is the Lord’s care for his servant and his people.

Key contrast Both honor the office and the king’s “no loss.” Arminians typically hear Wesley’s ranking after Belshazzar’s third-place promise—Daniel first among the three as a real court fact. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin’s further word: God cared for his Prophet so the captives would be benefited, and Darius recognized a heaven-sent messenger.

Daniel 6:3

3Then this Daniel became distinguished above all the other high officials and satraps, because an excellent spirit was in him. And the king planned to set him over the whole kingdom.

Arminian

Daniel is distinguished because an excellent spirit is in him; the king plans to set him over the whole kingdom. Wesley’s nearest (v. 2) already has him first among the presidents.

Arminian pastoral: gifts used well distinguish a man without erasing his agency. He will freely keep the means of grace when the edict comes. Promotion is not a bribe to stop praying, and distinction is not a license to despise the king.

Reformed

Calvin: a more excellent spirit was in him. First, a greater portion of his Spirit was bestowed upon him; secondly, Darius acknowledged this and held his prudence in great repute. God divinely adorned him, and a king had discretion enough to set a captive over his satraps.

Reformed pastoral: God preserves and raises his servant. The later trap cannot oust the Most High’s rule. Distinction here is providence, not a self-made career and not a revolutionary platform.

Daniel 6:4

4Then the high officials and the satraps sought to find a ground for complaint against Daniel with regard to the kingdom, but they could find no ground for complaint or any fault, because he was faithful, and no error or fault was found in him.

Arminian

They hunt a ground for complaint with regard to the kingdom and find none. Wesley: concerning the kingdom—and so to have made him guilty of treason, or other high misdemeanors, in the king’s business. He was faithful; no error or fault.

Arminian pastoral: integrity is real. Daniel has not been a revolutionary in office. The coming charge will not be civic crime; it will be worship. Shared: he is faithful in the king’s business, the quiet godliness Titus 3 reminds.

Reformed

Calvin: they sought occasion and did not find it, because he was faithful. We shall find no better defense against the envious than to conduct ourselves righteously and innocently. Integrity is the best of all protectors. Envy, sprung from ambition, made them spies of his fortune.

Reformed pastoral: God keeps his servant’s record clean so the only remaining snare is piety. Envy does not invent a true fault in the books. The Most High is not ousted by a plot that cannot find a number.

Daniel 6:5

5Then these men said, “We shall not find any ground for complaint against this Daniel unless we find it in connection with the law of his God.”

Arminian

No ground except in connection with the law of his God. Wesley’s nearest (v. 4): they could not make him guilty of treason in the king’s business.

Arminian pastoral: conscience toward God is the only “opening.” That is not a license to riot. When an edict will contradict worship, the conflict is already named. He will keep praying; he will not plot, and he will not treat the king as God.

Reformed

Calvin: they plot against his holy Prophet and purposely wage war with God himself. Piety and an earnest desire for it ought to be of more value than life itself. This temporary life ought not to be more precious than that most sacred of all things—the preservation of God’s honor unstained.

Reformed pastoral: the trap is aimed at worship because God has left them no other handle. The law of his God is the higher claim. Civil order still stands; the Most High still rules. Daniel is not asked to rebel against taxes; he is being hunted for prayer.

Daniel 6:6

6Then these high officials and satraps came by agreement to the king and said to him, “O King Darius, live forever!

Arminian

They come by agreement: O King Darius, live forever! Flattery first. Wesley’s nearest (v. 10) will show Daniel does not treat this greeting as a reason to stop prayer.

Arminian pastoral: honor the king—the greeting is the language of the court, and subjection is real (Rom 13; Titus 3:1). Living forever is not the same as being God. The trap uses civic honor; Daniel will not return insult for it, and he will not sell the conscience.

Reformed

Calvin: they assembled, they made a conspiracy, and approached the king. Their malice was the more detestable, since they desired to destroy Daniel by this very pretense. They knew him pious and straightforward; they experienced the power of that God unknown to them, yet hatred urged them to cruelty.

Reformed pastoral: God is not fooled by “live forever.” The king is a magistrate, not a deity. The conspiracy uses civic honor as a net. The Most High still sits above the hall.

Daniel 6:7

7All the high officials of the kingdom, the prefects and the satraps, the counselors and the governors are agreed that the king should establish an ordinance and enforce an injunction, that whoever makes petition to any god or man for thirty days, except to you, O king, shall be cast into the den of lions.

Arminian

Whoever petitions any god or man for thirty days except the king shall be cast into the den of lions. They claim all officials agree. Wesley’s nearest (v. 10): he did not abate his prayers for the king’s command, nor did he break the law purposely—he did no more than he was wont.

Arminian pastoral: this is not ordinary tribute (Rom 13). It is a blank check for tyranny: the king as the only hearer of prayer. Daniel will not riot; he also will not obey an idol-decree. Conscience toward God when the edict contradicts worship. Prayer is not insurrection.

Reformed

Calvin: the king forbade any one to offer up any prayer during a whole month—that is, he exacts from every one a denial of God. Prayer constitutes the chief part of our adoration and worship of God. This was a gross and manifest denial of piety. How blind was the king’s pride when he could sign so impious an edict.

Reformed pastoral: the den is the threat; the deeper crime is treating the king as God. God has not handed his honor to Darius for thirty days. The trap does not oust the Most High’s rule. Daniel will keep praying; he will not seize the magistrate’s sword.

Daniel 6:8

8Now, O king, establish the injunction and sign the document, so that it cannot be changed, according to the law of the Medes and the Persians, which cannot be revoked.”

Arminian

Sign, so that it cannot be changed, according to the law of the Medes and the Persians, which cannot be revoked. Irrevocable paper is still paper. Wesley’s nearest (v. 10): Daniel will not abate his prayers, and he will not break the law purposely—he will do only what he was wont in serving his God.

Arminian pastoral: Christians honor standing law. They do not baptize an unchangeable blasphemy. Daniel’s later prayer is not insurrection against a just statute; it is perseverance in the means of grace when the statute forbids worship. Not a riot; not a quietist bow to the idol-decree.

Reformed

Calvin: constancy is praiseworthy in edicts if only preceded by prudence and equity. But we shall see how foolishly kings affect the fame of consistency, and how their obstinacy utterly perverts justice. They sought a signature of the writing, that it be unchangeable according to the law of the Medes and Persians, which does not pass away.

Reformed pastoral: an unchangeable law against prayer is still under the Most High. God is not bound by Media’s formula. The church does not need to riot to know that the king is not God.

Daniel 6:9

9Therefore King Darius signed the document and injunction.

Arminian

Therefore King Darius signed the document and injunction. Wesley’s nearest (v. 10): when Daniel knew the writing was signed, he did not abate his prayers for the king’s command, nor break the law purposely, because he did no more than he was wont to do in serving his God.

Arminian pastoral: a signed edict is still not God. Subjection (Titus 3:1) does not mean the magistrate may take prayer. Daniel will keep the usual hours. That is conscience, not a riot, and not a blank check for tyranny.

Reformed

Calvin: the king did not dispute long with his nobles but subscribed the edict; he thought it might prove useful to himself, if he found the Chaldeans rather prepared to deny the existence of every god than to refuse whatever he commanded. The minds of kings are inclined to fallacies when they think they can increase their own dignity.

Reformed pastoral: God did not abdicate when the stylus moved. Darius is still a man. The signature is the trap’s last knot; it is not the Most High’s surrender. He will preserve his servant’s prayer.

Daniel 6:10

10When Daniel knew that the document had been signed, he went to his house where he had windows in his upper chamber open toward Jerusalem. He got down on his knees three times a day and prayed and gave thanks before his God, as he had done previously.

Arminian

Windows open toward Jerusalem; knees three times a day; prayer and thanks, as he had done previously. Wesley: Toward Jerusalem—the temple was the place where the Lord placed his name, and promised to appear, and accept his people, all being a type of Christ, through whom only sinners are accepted. As he did aforetime—He did not abate his prayers for the king’s command, nor did he break the law purposely, because he did no more than he was wont to do in serving his God.

Arminian pastoral: he freely perseveres in the means of grace. The same hours, the same window, the same thanks. Prayer is not insurrection. He does not riot, plot, or treat the king as God. He also does not go quiet and obey the idol-decree. Conscience toward God when the edict contradicts worship: Romans 13 still stands; so does the first commandment.

Reformed

Calvin: Daniel was clothed in the boldness of the Spirit of God to offer his life as a sacrifice; he preferred meeting death rather than ceasing from the duty of piety. Prayer is the principal sacrifice God requires; the edict was a gross denial of piety. God requires not only faith in the heart but the witness and confession of our piety. Windows toward Jerusalem: not that God is more easily heard with open heaven, but as a stimulus to fervor, and to shew himself a stranger among the Chaldeans. Three times a-day, lest prayer slip from memory. As he had done aforetime: a regular constancy.

Reformed pastoral: God preserves his servant and his prayer. The trap does not oust the Most High’s rule. Daniel is not a revolutionary; he is not a quietist. The Spirit’s boldness keeps the usual confession when the king has tried to take God’s place.

Key contrast Both honor civil authority and the higher claim of the Most High; neither makes Daniel a revolutionary or a quietist. Arminians typically hear Wesley: he did not abate his prayers, nor break the law purposely—he did no more than he was wont, and Jerusalem’s temple is a type of Christ through whom sinners are accepted. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin: the Spirit clothed him; God requires the open confession of prayer as the chief sacrifice; the edict cannot unseat the Most High.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on satraps and presidents, a trap in the law of Daniel’s God, and prayer with windows toward Jerusalem—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Daniel 6:1–10.

Arminian Notable voices

“Daniel was first—Belshazzar’s promise to Daniel was, that he should be the third ruler in the kingdom. The first was general of the army, the second president of the palace, the third of the land and provinces. Concerning the kingdom—And so to have made him guilty of treason, or other high misdemeanors, in the king’s business. Toward Jerusalem—The temple was the place where the Lord placed his name, and promised to appear, and accept his people, all being a type of Christ, through whom only sinners are accepted. As he did aforetime—He did not abate his prayers for the king’s command, nor did he break the law purposely, because he did no more than he was wont to do in serving his God.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Daniel 6:2, 4, 10)

Reformed Notable voices

“Here again we may perceive how God cared for his Prophet, not so much for any private reason as by his aid the wretched captives and exiles should be benefited. A greater portion of his Spirit was bestowed upon him; Darius acknowledged this. We shall find no better defense against the envious than to conduct ourselves righteously; yet Daniel was in danger because he would not leave off the sincere worship of God. We must bravely undergo all dangers whenever the worship of God is at stake. The king forbade any one to pray—he exacts from every one a denial of God. Daniel was clothed in the boldness of the Spirit of God to offer his life as a sacrifice; he preferred meeting death rather than ceasing from the duty of piety. Prayer constitutes the chief part of our adoration; God requires not only faith in the heart but the witness and confession of our piety. He opened his windows toward Jerusalem as a stimulus to fervor, and to shew himself a stranger among the Chaldeans. He prayed three times a-day, because unless we fix certain hours, prayer easily slips from our memory—as he had done aforetime, by a regular constancy.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Daniel (on Daniel 6:1–10)
