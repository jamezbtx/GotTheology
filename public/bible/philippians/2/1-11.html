Passage

Philippians 2

Verses 1–11 · WEB

Building on the call to stand together, Paul urges the Philippians to reject rivalry and attend to one another’s good. Christ’s movement from divine dignity through servant-like humility and the cross to exaltation gives their shared life its pattern and foundation. Arminian and Reformed readers share the central confession of Christ’s deity, true humanity, saving obedience, and universal lordship.

Philippians 2:1

1If therefore there is any exhortation in Christ, if any consolation of love, if any fellowship of the Spirit, if any tender mercies and compassion,

Arminian

Paul appeals to blessings the church already knows in Christ and the Spirit. Arminian readers see encouragement, love, and compassion as grace-given grounds for unity. His conditional phrasing invites them to acknowledge these shared gifts and respond to them, rather than questioning whether God has provided them.

Reformed

Reformed readers see fellowship in the Spirit and encouragement in Christ as the foundation of Christian unity. Paul reasons from divine gifts to the conduct they should produce. The appeal rests on common participation in grace, not merely on shared interests or agreeable personalities.

Philippians 2:2

2make my joy full by being like-minded, having the same love, being of one accord, of one mind;

Arminian

Paul asks for a common love and purpose that will complete his joy. Arminian readers see unity as something believers must actively pursue through the grace already given. Like-mindedness does not require identical judgments on every subject; it means a shared commitment shaped by love.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the unity of the church calling for visible expression in affection and common purpose. God’s grace does not make the command unnecessary. Paul’s joy is bound to the health of the whole body, not to the success of competing groups.

Philippians 2:3

3doing nothing through rivalry or through conceit, but in humility, each counting others better than himself;

Arminian

Rivalry and conceit must give way to humble regard for others. Arminian readers see a concrete choice of self-giving love made possible by grace. Counting others better means refusing self-exaltation and giving their good priority, not pretending that gifts and responsibilities do not differ.

Reformed

Reformed readers see humility as the fitting response to grace that leaves no room for boasting. Paul attacks the competitive spirit rather than asking believers to deny reality about themselves. Esteeming others redirects attention from personal standing to service within Christ’s body.

Philippians 2:4

4each of you not just looking to his own things, but each of you also to the things of others.

Arminian

Paul tells believers to attend to others’ concerns as well as their own. Arminian readers see love taking practical form in choices about time, resources, and care. The command does not abolish legitimate personal needs; it rejects a life organized around self-interest alone.

Reformed

Reformed readers see grace producing neighbor-directed attention. The same Lord cares for all members of the church, so private interests cannot be the only measure of conduct. This verse prepares for Christ’s self-giving by identifying the everyday shape of humble service.

Philippians 2:5

5Have this in your mind, which was also in Christ Jesus,

Arminian

Christ’s attitude is the pattern for the church’s common life. Arminian readers see the call to imitate his humility as a real, grace-enabled summons. The following verses concern who Christ is and what he did, so the example rests on his saving action rather than mere moral inspiration.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christ as both the source and pattern of the believer’s renewed life. His humility addresses the rivalry Paul has just forbidden. The command does not reduce the incarnation to an example; it calls those who belong to Christ to reflect his self-giving disposition.

Philippians 2:6

6who, existing in the form of God, didn’t consider equality with God a thing to be grasped,

Arminian

Christ’s equality with God does not lead him to grasp at advantage. Arminian readers share the confession of his divine dignity and see his refusal of self-seeking as the foundation of the example. Different explanations of grasping are not a distinctive Arminian and Reformed dividing line.

Reformed

Reformed readers affirm Christ’s divine dignity and the voluntary character of his humility. He does not treat equality with God as grounds for self-advantage at others’ expense. The verse’s precise wording is debated, but its place in Paul’s appeal to self-giving love is clear.

Philippians 2:7

7but emptied himself, taking the form of a servant, being made in the likeness of men.

Arminian

Christ’s self-emptying is described through taking a servant’s form and becoming human. Arminian readers ordinarily affirm that the Son truly assumed humanity without ceasing to be divine. His humility consists in self-giving service, providing the pattern for a church tempted by rivalry.

Reformed

Reformed readers understand the emptying through what the Son takes on: the condition of a servant and true human life. He does not cease to be God. Both traditions see the wonder of the incarnation and its challenge to status-seeking within the church.

Philippians 2:8

8And being found in human form, he humbled himself, becoming obedient to the point of death, yes, the death of the cross.

Arminian

Christ’s humility reaches obedient death on the cross. Arminian readers see voluntary, costly obedience expressing his saving love and exposing the church’s self-centered ambitions. The cross is more than an example, yet Paul draws from it a powerful call to sacrificial service.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the incarnate Son obeying the Father through the humiliation of crucifixion. His saving obedience grounds the believer’s hope and shapes Christian conduct. Here the traditions share the central confession rather than finding a special dispute over the nature of humility.

Philippians 2:9

9Therefore God also highly exalted him, and gave to him the name which is above every name,

Arminian

God exalts the one who humbled himself and bestows supreme honor upon him. Arminian readers see the Father vindicating Christ’s obedient service. The movement from humiliation to exaltation confirms that self-giving is not defeat and calls the church away from securing its own status.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Father publicly honoring the obedient, incarnate Son. Exaltation does not mean that Christ first becomes divine; it follows the lowly service described above. God’s verdict overturns the shame of the cross and establishes the supremacy of the risen Lord.

Philippians 2:10

10that at the name of Jesus every knee should bow, of those in heaven, those on earth, and those under the earth,

Arminian

Every sphere of creation will acknowledge Jesus’ authority. Arminian readers see the universal reach of his lordship, which summons willing worship now. The bowing of every knee does not by itself establish that every person will finally share in salvation.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the comprehensive dominion of the exalted Christ. No creature is beyond his authority, whatever resistance appears in the present. Universal homage is not identical with universal saving faith; the verse emphasizes the scope of his lordship rather than erasing judgment.

Philippians 2:11

11and that every tongue should confess that Jesus Christ is Lord, to the glory of God the Father.

Arminian

Confessing Jesus Christ as Lord brings glory to the Father. Arminian readers see worship of the Son fulfilling rather than competing with the Father’s glory. The passage ends with shared Christian confession, giving a divided church a center greater than its own disputes.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Father glorified through the universal acknowledgment of the Son’s lordship. Christ’s humiliation does not diminish his rightful honor. The church’s present confession anticipates this final acknowledgment and calls its members to serve together under one Lord.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
