Passage

Philippians 4

Verses 21–23 · WEB

Paul closes with greetings to every believer in Christ and from the saints around him, including those associated with Caesar’s household. The exchange affirms fellowship beyond the local congregation. His final blessing returns the whole church to the grace of the Lord Jesus Christ, the source of its unity and life.

Philippians 4:21

21Greet every saint in Christ Jesus. The brothers who are with me greet you.

Arminian

Paul asks that every believer in Christ receive his greeting, extending fellowship to the whole congregation. Arminian readers see shared belonging that includes those who need reconciliation, with greetings from Paul’s companions reinforcing their unity.

Reformed

Every saint is greeted on the basis of belonging to Christ, not special rank within the congregation. Reformed readers see the fellowship of God’s people extending between Paul’s companions and the church, including its imperfect members.

Philippians 4:22

22All the saints greet you, especially those who are of Caesar’s household.

Arminian

The greetings include believers connected with Caesar’s household, showing fellowship reaching into the imperial establishment. Arminian readers see the gospel bearing fruit in unexpected settings; the phrase does not establish that Caesar or his relatives had become Christians.

Reformed

Believers associated with Caesar’s household join the wider church’s greeting. Reformed readers see Christ gathering his people even within imperial structures; Paul gives no basis for identifying these saints as Caesar himself or his family.

Philippians 4:23

23The grace of the Lord Jesus Christ be with you all. Amen.

Arminian

Paul closes by commending the whole church to Christ’s grace. Arminian readers see the same grace that began their life in Christ sustaining their unity, generosity, and continuing trust.

Reformed

The final blessing places the whole congregation under the grace of the Lord Jesus Christ. Reformed readers see grace as the foundation and sustaining power of the shared Christian life described throughout the letter.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
