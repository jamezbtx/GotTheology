Passage

Philippians 4

Verses 10–20 · WEB

Paul thanks the Philippians for their gift delivered by Epaphroditus and recalls their earlier support. He joins sincere gratitude to contentment learned through both scarcity and abundance, locating his strength in Christ. Their giving shares his affliction and becomes an offering pleasing to God. Confidence in God’s provision leads to praise, not a promise of effortless prosperity.

Philippians 4:10

10But I rejoice in the Lord greatly that now at length you have revived your thought for me; in which you did indeed take thought, but you lacked opportunity.

Arminian

Paul rejoices in the Lord over the Philippians’ renewed practical support, while acknowledging that they had cared all along. Arminian readers see love taking action when opportunity returns; the delay is not treated as evidence of indifference.

Reformed

Paul credits the Lord while warmly recognizing the church’s enduring concern. Reformed readers see God’s care working through willing generosity, and note Paul’s refusal to blame the Philippians for their earlier lack of opportunity.

Philippians 4:11

11Not that I speak because of lack, for I have learned in whatever state I am, to be content in it.

Arminian

Paul’s gratitude is not a disguised demand for more money: he has learned contentment in changing circumstances. Arminian readers see maturity formed through dependent trust and practice, not an innate ability to do without others.

Reformed

Paul distinguishes thankful reception of help from dependence on possessions for peace. Reformed readers see contentment learned under God’s providential care, a grace that grows through experience rather than automatic indifference to need.

Philippians 4:12

12I know how to be humbled, and I also know how to abound. In any and all circumstances I have learned the secret both to be filled and to be hungry, both to abound and to be in need.

Arminian

Both deprivation and abundance require learned faithfulness. Arminian readers see grace enabling Paul to live faithfully through hunger or plenty; contentment neither makes poverty desirable nor excuses neglect of the hungry.

Reformed

Paul has learned to receive plenty and endure deprivation without making either his ultimate security. Reformed readers see God sustaining him across these conditions, while the surrounding thanks affirms the real value of material help.

Philippians 4:13

13I can do all things through Christ who strengthens me.

Arminian

The surrounding verses define the scope: Christ strengthens Paul to remain faithful in hunger, need, and abundance. Arminian readers see active endurance enabled by Christ’s grace, not independent willpower or a guarantee of success in every ambition.

Reformed

Christ is the source of Paul’s strength to endure the circumstances just described. Reformed readers see dependence on the Savior rather than self-sufficiency; the assurance concerns faithful contentment, not unlimited achievement or freedom from suffering.

Philippians 4:14

14However you did well that you shared in my affliction.

Arminian

Paul’s contentment does not diminish the goodness of their help. Arminian readers see generosity as genuine participation in another believer’s suffering, a practical expression of the love grace calls forth.

Reformed

Their gift made them partners in Paul’s affliction rather than mere spectators. Reformed readers see God’s provision arriving through the church’s responsible care; dependence on Christ leaves ample room for receiving human assistance.

Philippians 4:15

15You yourselves also know, you Philippians, that in the beginning of the Good News, when I departed from Macedonia, no assembly shared with me in the matter of giving and receiving but you only.

Arminian

Paul recalls the Philippians’ distinctive financial partnership when he left Macedonia. Arminian readers see enduring fellowship expressed through giving and receiving, not merely shared sentiments or verbal support for the gospel.

Reformed

Paul remembers this congregation’s particular willingness to support his ministry. Reformed readers see material generosity as an expression of gospel fellowship, with the church sharing in work beyond its own local needs.

Philippians 4:16

16For even in Thessalonica you sent once and again to my need.

Arminian

Their repeated gifts reached Paul even during his time in Thessalonica. Arminian readers see sustained care rather than a single generous impulse, showing how faithful love responds to recurring needs.

Reformed

Paul remembers repeated assistance with ordinary material needs. Reformed readers see continuing generosity as fruit of grace and a concrete means by which God provided for his servant’s ministry.

Philippians 4:17

17Not that I seek for the gift, but I seek for the fruit that increases to your account.

Arminian

Paul seeks the spiritual fruit of their generosity more than the gift itself. Arminian readers see God graciously recognizing faithful love; the accounting image does not turn giving into a purchase of salvation.

Reformed

Paul’s concern is for the good their generosity bears in their own lives before God. Reformed readers see fruit and gracious reward arising from faith, not financial contributions earning acceptance with God.

Philippians 4:18

18But I have all things and abound. I am filled, having received from Epaphroditus the things that came from you, a sweet-smelling fragrance, an acceptable and well-pleasing sacrifice to God.

Arminian

Epaphroditus has delivered their gifts, and Paul describes their practical care in the language of worship and sacrifice. Arminian readers see loving generosity as pleasing to God, not as an atoning payment or a way to buy his favor.

Reformed

Paul acknowledges full receipt through Epaphroditus and treats the church’s help as an offering pleasing to God. Reformed readers see generosity flowing from grace as genuine worship, without competing with Christ’s atoning sacrifice.

Philippians 4:19

19My God will supply every need of yours according to his riches in glory in Christ Jesus.

Arminian

Paul assures the congregation that the God who supplied his needs will care for theirs in Christ. Arminian readers trust this rich provision without turning the promise into a financial bargain; Paul has just described faithfulness amid hunger as well as plenty.

Reformed

God’s riches in Christ ground Paul’s confidence that the generous church will not be forgotten. Reformed readers see providential care for every need, not a promise of luxury or exemption from the hardship Paul himself endures.

Philippians 4:20

20Now to our God and Father be the glory forever and ever! Amen.

Arminian

The response to God’s provision is praise to their shared Father. Arminian readers see both the gift and the ability to give leading back to God’s glory, leaving no place for self-congratulation.

Reformed

Paul ends his thanks by directing everlasting glory to God the Father. Reformed readers see the whole exchange of need, generosity, and provision culminating in worship of the giver rather than praise of human self-sufficiency.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
