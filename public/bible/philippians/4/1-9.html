Passage

Philippians 4

Verses 1–9 · WEB

Following the hope of Christ’s return in 3:20–21, Paul calls his beloved church to stand firm. He urges Euodia and Syntyche toward unity and asks a trusted partner to help these fellow gospel workers. Joy, gentleness, prayer, disciplined thought, and practiced obedience shape a community guarded by God’s peace. Both traditions ground this life in the Lord’s grace.

Philippians 4:1

1Therefore, my brothers, beloved and longed for, my joy and crown, stand firm in the Lord in this way, my beloved.

Arminian

Paul’s affection frames his appeal to stand firm, following the hope of Christ’s transforming return in 3:20–21. Arminian readers see perseverance as a continuing response sustained by grace, pursued together rather than through self-reliance.

Reformed

Paul calls the beloved congregation to steadfastness in the Lord, grounded in the hope of 3:20–21. Reformed readers see this affectionate exhortation as a means through which God strengthens his people to persevere.

Philippians 4:2

2I exhort Euodia, and I exhort Syntyche, to think the same way in the Lord.

Arminian

Paul appeals to Euodia and Syntyche alike to seek unity in the Lord. Arminian readers see reconciliation as a concrete response to grace; Paul does not disclose their disagreement or ask the church to take sides.

Reformed

Paul addresses both women with equal seriousness and locates their unity in the Lord. Reformed readers see reconciliation as an expression of their shared life in Christ, without speculating about the cause of their dispute.

Philippians 4:3

3Yes, I beg you also, true partner, help these women, for they labored with me in the Good News with Clement also, and the rest of my fellow workers, whose names are in the book of life.

Arminian

Paul asks a trusted partner, whose identity is uncertain, to help women he honors as fellow gospel workers. The book of life affirms their belonging to God; Arminian readers who affirm possible final apostasy understand that belonging in relation to continuing faith, though Paul does not discuss names being removed here.

Reformed

Paul honors the women’s gospel service alongside Clement and the other workers, and calls an unidentified trusted partner to assist them. Reformed readers understand the book of life as expressing God’s secure election, giving confidence in their belonging to him even as reconciliation remains necessary.

Key contrast Both readings recognize the workers as belonging to God. Arminian readings that allow final apostasy understand book-of-life membership as forfeitable through abandoning faith; Reformed readings understand it as secure election that God preserves. This verse affirms their names are enrolled without explaining whether removal is possible.

Philippians 4:4

4Rejoice in the Lord always! Again I will say, “Rejoice!”

Arminian

Joy rests in the Lord rather than in agreeable circumstances. Arminian readers see this repeated appeal as an invitation to practice grateful trust amid hardship and strained relationships, not to deny sorrow.

Reformed

Joy is grounded in the Lord, not in freedom from suffering or conflict. Reformed readers see grace sustaining this joy as believers repeatedly turn to Christ, without treating grief as incompatible with faith.

Philippians 4:5

5Let your gentleness be known to all men. The Lord is at hand.

Arminian

Gentleness should shape the church’s dealings with everyone, including those involved in conflict. Arminian readers hear the Lord’s nearness as encouragement toward patient, gracious conduct rather than insistence on personal advantage.

Reformed

The Lord’s nearness gives urgency and comfort to the call for visible gentleness. Reformed readers see a gracious disposition toward all people as fruit of belonging to Christ, especially when believers could demand their own way.

Philippians 4:6

6In nothing be anxious, but in everything, by prayer and petition with thanksgiving, let your requests be made known to God.

Arminian

Paul directs anxious believers toward prayer, specific requests, and thanksgiving. Arminian readers see dependence on God expressed through honest petition; the command invites burdened people to seek his care rather than pretend their needs are unreal.

Reformed

Prayer and thanksgiving entrust real needs to God’s care. Reformed readers understand petition as a God-appointed means of receiving help, fully consistent with his providence; confidence in him does not make prayer unnecessary.

Philippians 4:7

7And the peace of God, which surpasses all understanding, will guard your hearts and your thoughts in Christ Jesus.

Arminian

God’s peace guards hearts and thoughts in Christ as believers bring their concerns to him. Arminian readers see a gracious gift beyond what circumstances can explain, not a promise that every difficulty immediately disappears.

Reformed

God himself supplies the peace that guards believers inwardly in Christ Jesus. Reformed readers see divine care exceeding human calculation, protecting his people amid trouble rather than guaranteeing the removal of every hardship.

Philippians 4:8

8Finally, brothers, whatever things are true, whatever things are honorable, whatever things are just, whatever things are pure, whatever things are lovely, whatever things are of good report: if there is any virtue and if there is anything worthy of praise, think about these things.

Arminian

Paul directs attention toward what is true, pure, just, and worthy of love and praise. Arminian readers see the deliberate formation of thought as part of grace-enabled holiness, shaping how believers treat one another.

Reformed

Paul calls believers to cultivate thoughts ordered by truth and goodness. Reformed readers see renewed minds actively attending to what pleases God, so that inward habits support the gentle and peaceful life described above.

Philippians 4:9

9Do the things which you learned, received, heard, and saw in me, and the God of peace will be with you.

Arminian

The church must practice the teaching and example received from Paul, not merely admire them. Arminian readers connect obedient response with fellowship with the God of peace, while recognizing that his presence is a gift rather than an earned payment.

Reformed

Paul joins apostolic instruction to a visible pattern of life that the church should imitate. Reformed readers see obedience as the fruit of grace, accompanied by the God of peace whose presence enables faithful practice.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
