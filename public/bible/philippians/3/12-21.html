Passage

Philippians 3

Verses 12–21 · WEB

Paul insists he has not yet arrived, but presses on like a runner toward the prize, because Christ has already taken hold of him. He calls the mature to share this mindset, to imitate faithful examples, and to weep over enemies of the cross. Writing to a Roman colony, he reminds them their citizenship is in heaven, from which they await Christ, who will transform their bodies. Both traditions share the already/not-yet shape of the Christian life and the hope of bodily resurrection; they differ on whether Christian perfection in love is possible in this life and on how perseverance is secured.

Philippians 3:12

12Not that I have already obtained, or am already made perfect; but I press on, that I may take hold of that for which also I was taken hold of by Christ Jesus.

Arminian

Paul has not yet obtained or been made perfect, yet presses on to grasp that for which Christ grasped him. Arminian readers see grace taking initiative and the believer responding with real, ongoing effort. Wesleyans distinguish this final, resurrection perfection from Christian perfection in love, which they believe possible in this life.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christ’s prior grasp as the ground of Paul’s pressing on: he strives because he is already Christ’s. Perfection awaits glorification; all sanctification in this life remains incomplete. Both affirm that God’s grace comes first and human striving is real.

Key contrast Both deny sinless or final perfection now. Wesleyans hold that entire sanctification, perfect love, is attainable in this life. Reformed readers hold that sanctification remains partial until glorification, and that Christ’s grasp guarantees the believer’s perseverance.

Philippians 3:13

13Brothers, I don’t regard myself as yet having taken hold, but one thing I do: forgetting the things which are behind and stretching forward to the things which are before,

Arminian

Paul forgets what lies behind, both credentials and failures, and strains forward. Arminian readers stress the single-minded concentration required to persevere.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same focus, rooted in confidence that the past no longer defines the believer. Both read the athletic image as wholehearted effort, not anxious self-salvation.

Philippians 3:14

14I press on toward the goal for the prize of the high calling of God in Christ Jesus.

Arminian

The prize is the upward call of God in Christ. Arminian readers see the prize as final salvation and glory, which God promises to all who persevere in faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the prize as the consummation of the calling God has already effectually issued. Both affirm that the call originates with God and ends with God in Christ.

Philippians 3:15

15Let us therefore, as many as are perfect, think this way. If in anything you think otherwise, God will also reveal that to you.

Arminian

Paul asks the “perfect,” the mature, to share this mindset, trusting God to correct any who differ. Wesleyan readers sometimes see here a maturity Paul can call perfection even while denying final perfection in v. 12, a distinction central to Wesley’s teaching.

Reformed

Reformed readers take “perfect” as mature in contrast to final perfection, so maturity includes knowing one is not yet perfect. Both traditions note Paul’s gentle confidence that God will reveal what is lacking.

Philippians 3:16

16Nevertheless, to the extent that we have already attained, let’s walk by the same rule. Let’s be of the same mind.

Arminian

Believers are to keep living by what they have already reached. Arminian readers stress faithfulness to received light as the path to further growth.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a call to consistency with present attainment. Both see unity of mind as part of that walk.

Philippians 3:17

17Brothers, be imitators together of me, and note those who walk this way, even as you have us for an example.

Arminian

Paul invites imitation of himself and those who live this way. Arminian readers see embodied example as a means of grace for growth.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see apostolic example pointing beyond itself to Christ. Both note Paul offers his pressing on, not a finished perfection, as the model.

Philippians 3:18

18For many walk, of whom I told you often, and now tell you even weeping, as the enemies of the cross of Christ,

Arminian

Paul weeps over many who live as enemies of the cross. Arminian readers often see possible apostates or professing believers whose lives deny the cross, warning that profession without perseverance fails.

Reformed

Reformed readers often read these as people whose profession was never genuine, or as outsiders such as libertines. Both traditions take the tears as real grief and the warning as real.

Philippians 3:19

19whose end is destruction, whose god is the belly, and whose glory is in their shame, who think about earthly things.

Arminian

Their end is destruction, their god their appetite, their glory their shame, their mind on earthly things. Arminian readers see a sober outcome for those who abandon the cross.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same destiny for those not truly in Christ. Both read the description as a life governed by the present age.

Philippians 3:20

20For our citizenship is in heaven, from where we also wait for a Savior, the Lord Jesus Christ,

Arminian

In Roman Philippi, a colony proud of its citizenship, Paul says believers’ citizenship is in heaven, and they await a Savior and Lord, titles also claimed for Caesar. Arminian readers see a present identity shaping daily loyalty and hope.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same colonial image: believers live as an outpost of heaven under Christ’s rule. Both stress expectant waiting for Christ’s return rather than escape from the world.

Philippians 3:21

21who will change the body of our humiliation to be conformed to the body of his glory, according to the working by which he is able even to subject all things to himself.

Arminian

Christ will transform our lowly bodies to share his glorious body by the power that subjects all things. Arminian readers see the hope of bodily resurrection as the goal of persevering faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same power securing the outcome for all in Christ. Both affirm bodily resurrection, Christ’s universal sovereignty, and the completion of what Paul pressed toward in vv. 11–14.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
