Passage

Philippians 3

Verses 1–11 · WEB

Paul warns the Philippians against teachers insisting on circumcision and sets his own impressive former righteousness under the law beside the surpassing worth of knowing Christ. He renounces confidence in the flesh to be found in Christ with a righteousness from God by faith, and longs to share Christ’s sufferings and resurrection. Arminian and Reformed readers agree that justification rests on grace received by faith, not law-keeping, and that union with Christ transforms. They differ chiefly on how to describe imputed righteousness and how to read the conditional language about attaining the resurrection.

Philippians 3:1

1Finally, my brothers, rejoice in the Lord! To write the same things to you, to me indeed is not tiresome, but for you it is safe.

Arminian

Paul turns to joy in the Lord and to repeating warnings he has given before. Arminian readers see pastoral care in the repetition: believers are kept safe partly through faithful reminders they must heed. Both traditions read “rejoice in the Lord” as the frame for everything that follows.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same pastoral safeguard: God uses repeated teaching as a means of keeping his people. Joy rooted in the Lord, not in religious achievement, sets up the contrast Paul is about to draw.

Philippians 3:2

2Beware of the dogs; beware of the evil workers; beware of the false circumcision.

Arminian

“Dogs,” “evil workers,” and “false circumcision” turn the Judaizers’ own boundary language back on them. Arminian readers note Paul’s sharp tone guards the Philippians from teachers who would add circumcision to faith in Christ.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise identify the targets as those requiring circumcision of Gentile believers. Paul’s wordplay (katatomē, “mutilation,” against peritomē, “circumcision”) exposes a rite cut off from its meaning. Both traditions agree the danger is a gospel of Christ plus law.

Philippians 3:3

3For we are the circumcision, who worship God in the Spirit, and rejoice in Christ Jesus, and have no confidence in the flesh;

Arminian

True circumcision belongs to those who worship by the Spirit, boast in Christ, and trust nothing in the flesh. Arminian readers connect this to the Spirit’s renewing work received through faith, the heart circumcision promised in the prophets.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the covenant sign fulfilled in Christ and the Spirit’s inward work. The church, Jew and Gentile, is the true people of God. Both traditions read “flesh” here as human standing and performance before God.

Philippians 3:4

4though I myself might have confidence even in the flesh. If any other man thinks that he has confidence in the flesh, I yet more:

Arminian

Paul concedes for argument’s sake that he could out-boast his opponents. Arminian readers see a rhetorical move: if fleshly credentials counted, Paul would win, which makes his renunciation all the more striking.

Reformed

Reformed readers read the same rhetorical strategy. Paul is not boasting but exposing the emptiness of the category. The credentials that follow are real, which is precisely the point.

Philippians 3:5

5circumcised the eighth day, of the stock of Israel, of the tribe of Benjamin, a Hebrew of Hebrews; concerning the law, a Pharisee;

Arminian

Paul lists inherited privileges (circumcision on the eighth day, Israelite birth, Benjamite tribe, Hebrew upbringing) and a chosen one (Pharisee). Arminian readers note both gifts and choices are equally set aside.

Reformed

Reformed readers make the same observation: nothing given by birth or achieved by devotion secures standing before God. Paul’s Jewish identity is not despised but no longer trusted.

Philippians 3:6

6concerning zeal, persecuting the assembly; concerning the righteousness which is in the law, found blameless.

Arminian

His zeal led him to persecute the church; by the law’s outward standard he was blameless. Arminian readers take “blameless” as a measure of observable law-keeping, not sinless perfection, and note how sincere zeal could still oppose God.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise read “blameless” as external, communal righteousness under the law. The persecution shows that religious zeal without Christ is not neutral but hostile. Both traditions see Paul’s former righteousness as real in its own terms yet insufficient.

Philippians 3:7

7However, I consider those things that were gain to me as a loss for Christ.

Arminian

Paul re-labels his former gains as loss “for Christ.” Arminian readers stress the decisive reorientation of conversion, an encounter that Paul freely embraced and keeps embracing.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that this revaluation is the fruit of God’s revealing his Son to Paul. Both read the accounting language as a complete reversal of values.

Philippians 3:8

8Yes most certainly, and I count all things to be a loss for the excellency of the knowledge of Christ Jesus, my Lord, for whom I suffered the loss of all things, and count them nothing but refuse, that I may gain Christ

Arminian

The present tense “I count” shows the renunciation continues. Knowing Christ is worth more than everything, and the old things are “refuse.” Arminian readers emphasize the ongoing, relational choice to keep valuing Christ above all.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize the surpassing worth of Christ that captures the heart. Both traditions see “knowledge” here as personal union and fellowship, not mere information, and both see “gain Christ” as looking toward final completion.

Philippians 3:9

9and be found in him, not having a righteousness of my own, that which is of the law, but that which is through faith in Christ, the righteousness which is from God by faith,

Arminian

Paul seeks a righteousness from God received by faith, not his own from the law. Classical Arminians, following Arminius and Wesley, affirm justification by grace through faith alone and the imputation of righteousness. Many describe it as God reckoning faith for righteousness on account of Christ, with Christ’s whole merit as the ground.

Reformed

Reformed readers, with Calvin and the Westminster Confession, see here the imputation of Christ’s righteousness, his obedience and satisfaction, received by faith alone as the instrument. Both traditions reject works of the law as the basis of standing before God.

Key contrast Both affirm justification by faith alone with righteousness from God. Classical Arminians commonly describe faith as reckoned for righteousness on account of Christ, while some (including Wesley at points) also speak of Christ’s righteousness imputed. Reformed confessions specify the imputation of Christ’s active and passive obedience as the formal ground.

Philippians 3:10

10that I may know him and the power of his resurrection, and the fellowship of his sufferings, becoming conformed to his death,

Arminian

To know Christ includes resurrection power and sharing in his sufferings, being conformed to his death. Arminian readers see transforming grace that believers actively cooperate with, a real participation in Christ’s life and cross.

Reformed

Reformed readers see union with Christ producing sanctification: the power of his resurrection works in those joined to him. Both traditions hold that justification (v. 9) and transformation (v. 10) belong together and are not to be confused.

Philippians 3:11

11if by any means I may attain to the resurrection from the dead.

Arminian

“If by any means” expresses humble earnestness about reaching the resurrection. Arminian readers often see a real conditionality: final salvation is assured to those who continue in faith, so Paul presses on without presumption.

Reformed

Reformed readers read the phrase as humility about the path, not doubt about the outcome, since God who began a good work will complete it (1:6). The resurrection is certain for those in Christ; Paul expresses the means through which it comes.

Key contrast Both read the resurrection as the goal. Arminian readers see attaining it as conditioned on continuing faith, with apostasy a real possibility. Reformed readers see it as certain for the elect, with perseverance as the God-given means.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
