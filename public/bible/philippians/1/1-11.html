Passage

Philippians 1

Verses 1–11 · WEB

Paul and Timothy greet the church at Philippi before Paul gives thanks for its continuing partnership in the Good News. His confidence in God’s completing work leads into prayer for discerning love and righteous living until Christ’s day. Both traditions affirm the priority of grace and the need for growth, while differing over how the promise of completion relates to the possibility of abandoning faith.

Philippians 1:1

1Paul and Timothy, servants of Jesus Christ, to all the saints in Christ Jesus who are at Philippi, with the overseers and servants:

Arminian

Paul and Timothy identify themselves as servants before addressing the whole church, including its leaders. Arminian readers see holiness as belonging to Christ rather than holding office. All the saints share a calling to serve, and the leaders are included within that community, not placed above its dependence on grace.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the church defined by its union with Christ Jesus. Saints are people set apart in him, not a spiritual elite. The greeting includes recognized leaders while placing them alongside all believers under the same Lord and in the same service.

Philippians 1:2

2Grace to you and peace from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ.

Arminian

Grace and peace come from the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ. Arminian readers see divine generosity as the source of Christian life and fellowship. Paul begins with what God gives, not what believers achieve; their obedience will be a response to this grace.

Reformed

Reformed readers see grace as God’s unearned favor and peace as the blessing of reconciliation with him. Both come from the Father and the Son. The greeting grounds the church’s life in divine provision before Paul gives any commands.

Philippians 1:3

3I thank my God whenever I remember you,

Arminian

Remembering the Philippians leads Paul to thank God. Arminian readers see gratitude for a community whose faith and partnership display grace at work. Giving God thanks does not erase the believers’ willing response; it acknowledges the source that made their fellowship possible.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Paul directing thanks to God for the life of this church. Their faith and fellowship give evidence of God’s gracious work. His remembrance becomes worship rather than praise of human achievement, without diminishing his genuine affection for them.

Philippians 1:4

4always in every request of mine on behalf of you all, making my requests with joy,

Arminian

Paul prays joyfully for all of them, not merely those closest to him. Arminian readers see intercession as an active expression of love within the body of Christ. Prayer seeks God’s continuing help for a community already sharing in his grace.

Reformed

Reformed readers see joy and intercession belonging together. Confidence in God’s work does not make prayer unnecessary; Paul asks God to sustain those for whom he gives thanks. His concern embraces the whole church rather than a favored group.

Philippians 1:5

5for your partnership in furtherance of the Good News from the first day until now;

Arminian

Their partnership has continued from the beginning of their response to the gospel. Arminian readers see sustained, practical participation rather than a merely private profession. Their willing support of the Good News is a continuing response to God’s grace and a source of Paul’s joy.

Reformed

Reformed readers see ongoing gospel partnership as evidence that God’s work has taken root among them. Their sharing includes the practical fellowship developed later in the letter. Paul’s confidence is not detached from their lives; he sees the fruit of grace in their perseverance.

Philippians 1:6

6being confident of this very thing, that he who began a good work in you will complete it until the day of Jesus Christ.

Arminian

God both begins and completes the good work. Arminian readers find strong assurance of his faithful provision, read alongside the letter’s calls to continuing faith and obedience. Paul’s confidence concerns this gospel-sharing community; it need not mean that a believer is incapable of finally turning away.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a strong promise of God’s preserving grace. The God who began the work will bring it to completion at Christ’s coming. Their continuing partnership gives Paul grounds to recognize that work, while its completion rests on God’s faithfulness rather than their independent strength.

Key contrast Both ground assurance in God’s faithful work. Arminian readers understand his promised provision alongside the need for continuing faith that can be abandoned. Reformed readers understand God’s completing work as ensuring the final perseverance of those he has truly brought to faith.

Philippians 1:7

7It is even right for me to think this way on behalf of all of you, because I have you in my heart, because both in my bonds and in the defense and confirmation of the Good News, you all are partakers with me of grace.

Arminian

Paul’s confidence is shaped by shared grace in suffering and gospel service. Arminian readers see the Philippians actively joining his mission despite its cost. Their partnership is relational and practical, showing why his assurance about them is more than an abstract claim detached from their response.

Reformed

Reformed readers see shared grace binding Paul and the church together through imprisonment and the defense of the gospel. Their solidarity supports his confidence that God is working among them. Grace produces participation in Christ’s mission, not merely possession of religious privileges.

Philippians 1:8

8For God is my witness, how I long after all of you in the tender mercies of Christ Jesus.

Arminian

Paul calls God to witness the depth of his affection. Arminian readers see Christ’s compassion shaping human relationships through grace. Christian love is not only duty or agreement; it includes a sincere longing for the good of fellow believers.

Reformed

Reformed readers see union with Christ bearing fruit in deep affection for his people. Paul’s longing reflects Christ’s tender mercies rather than mere personal preference. The appeal to God underlines the sincerity of a love that embraces the entire congregation.

Philippians 1:9

9This I pray, that your love may abound yet more and more in knowledge and all discernment,

Arminian

Paul asks for love that grows in knowledge and discernment. Arminian readers see spiritual maturity as more than stronger feelings: believers need grace to recognize and choose what truly serves others. Growth remains something to seek in prayer rather than presume upon.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God as the source of increasing love and wise judgment. Paul prays because even a faithful church needs further sanctification. Knowledge and discernment serve love, guarding it from becoming sentiment detached from truth and holiness.

Philippians 1:10

10so that you may approve the things that are excellent, that you may be sincere and without offense to the day of Christ,

Arminian

Discerning love enables believers to approve what is excellent as they await Christ. Arminian readers see a call to responsible moral judgment and continued faithfulness. Being ready for Christ involves a grace-enabled life of sincerity, not simply an earlier profession of faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the goal of sanctification in a life prepared for the day of Christ. God’s preserving work includes developing discernment and integrity in his people. The prayer seeks more than avoidance of obvious wrong; it seeks what most truly honors Christ.

Philippians 1:11

11being filled with the fruits of righteousness which are through Jesus Christ, to the glory and praise of God.

Arminian

The fruits of righteousness come through Jesus Christ and bring glory to God. Arminian readers see obedience as the result of grace received and lived out, never a basis for boasting. The prayer holds human growth and dependence on Christ together.

Reformed

Reformed readers see righteous living as fruit produced through Christ, not an independent achievement. God receives the praise because Christ supplies what his people need for holiness. The verse completes Paul’s prayer by directing its whole outcome toward God’s glory.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
