Passage

Colossians 3

Verses 12–17 · ESV · Voices

The new self’s wardrobe: as God’s chosen ones, holy and beloved, believers clothe themselves in Christlike virtues, forgive as the Lord has forgiven, bind everything with love, let Christ’s peace rule the one body, let His word dwell richly, and do all in Jesus’ name with thanksgiving.

Colossians 3:12

12Put on then, as God's chosen ones, holy and beloved, compassionate hearts, kindness, humility, meekness, and patience,

Arminian

“Put on then” continues the clothing metaphor. Identity phrases—“God’s chosen ones, holy and beloved”—describe the people in Christ before listing virtues. Classical Arminians often hear corporate or conditional election accents: God chooses the people found in Christ, or chooses according to foreseen faith, while still naming them holy and dearly loved.

Arminian pastoral: chosenness motivates compassion, not pride. Grace that set them in the beloved community also enables the virtues they must actively wear.

Reformed

Indicative first: they are God’s chosen ones, holy and beloved—language of electing love and consecration. Imperatives of virtue flow from that prior grace.

Reformed reading: unconditional election in Christ grounds the “put on.” Compassion and patience are not ladders into chosenness but the fitting dress of those the Father has set apart in love.

Key contrast Both take “chosen ones, holy and beloved” as grace-identity that fuels virtue. Arminians typically accent corporate election in Christ and/or conditional election tied to faith, with chosenness describing the believing people; Reformed readers typically accent God’s unconditional election of particular persons in Christ, with holiness and love as fruits of that prior choice.

Colossians 3:13

13bearing with one another and, if one has a complaint against another, forgiving each other; as the Lord has forgiven you, so you also must forgive.

Arminian

Bearing and forgiving answer real complaints inside the body. The measure is the Lord’s forgiveness already received—“so you also must forgive.”

Arminian mandate: grace that forgave them enables and obliges them to forgive. The “must” is sincere and serious; withholding forgiveness contradicts the mercy they freely received.

Reformed

Forgiveness is patterned on the Lord’s prior pardon. Community patience and release of complaints mirror gospel grace already given.

Reformed weight: those forgiven in Christ cannot treat grievances as ultimate. The Spirit forms a people whose forbearance displays the same mercy that justified them.

Key contrast Both make the Lord’s forgiveness the template and ground of mutual pardon. Arminians typically stress a real obligation empowered by resistible-in-principle grace that believers must not refuse; Reformed readers typically stress Spirit-wrought forgiveness as the necessary fruit of effectual grace already shown in Christ.

Colossians 3:14

14And above all these put on love, which binds everything together in perfect harmony.

Arminian

Love is the outer garment “above all these,” binding the virtues into perfect harmony. Without love, kindness and patience can become performance.

Arminian warmth: enabling grace produces love that holds the community together. Believers are summoned to put it on deliberately as the bond of maturity.

Reformed

Love crowns the wardrobe and unifies the rest into mature wholeness. It is not vague sentiment but the binding grace of the new creation.

Reformed accent: love poured into hearts by the Spirit perfects the body’s harmony. Christlike charity is the elect’s distinguishing dress.

Colossians 3:15

15And let the peace of Christ rule in your hearts, to which indeed you were called in one body. And be thankful.

Arminian

Christ’s peace is to “rule”—to umpire—hearts called into one body. Thanksgiving seals the posture.

Arminian communal note: grace calls them into shared peace they must welcome and not disrupt. Letting peace rule means yielding disputes to Christ’s verdict rather than self-will.

Reformed

The peace of Christ is appointed as heart-ruler for those called into one body. Gratitude is the fitting atmosphere of that calling.

Reformed pastoral: union with Christ and calling into one body are God’s work; the peace that rules is His gift administering the community under His lordship.

Colossians 3:16

16Let the word of Christ dwell in you richly, teaching and admonishing one another in all wisdom, singing psalms and hymns and spiritual songs, with thankfulness in your hearts to God.

Arminian

Christ’s word is to dwell richly—saturating the community with teaching, admonition, and thankful song. Mutual ministry flows from a word-filled people.

Arminian practice: grace opens ears and hearts to Scripture; the church remains responsible to let that word lodge deeply and shape speech and praise.

Reformed

The word of Christ indwelling richly produces wise teaching, admonition, and grateful worship. Song is theology on the lips of the body.

Reformed confidence: Christ rules His church by His word. Rich dwelling is Spirit-given fullness that forms a wise, thankful people under Scripture’s authority.

Colossians 3:17

17And whatever you do, in word or deed, do everything in the name of the Lord Jesus, giving thanks to God the Father through him.

Arminian

All of life—word and deed—comes under Jesus’ name, with thanks to the Father through Him. No sacred/secular split excuses ungrateful speech or action.

Arminian vocation: enabling grace makes whole-life discipleship possible. Doing all in Jesus’ name is a sincere call to represent Him consciously in every sphere.

Reformed

Comprehensive lordship: everything in the name of the Lord Jesus, thanksgiving routed to the Father through the Son. Christ mediates both obedience and praise.

Reformed doxology: providence and redemption place every act under Jesus’ authority. Gratitude through Him is the Christian’s constant liturgy of ordinary life.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on The new self’s wardrobe: as God’s chosen ones, holy and beloved, believers clothe themselves in Christlike virtues—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Colossians 3:12–17.

Arminian Notable voices

“All who are thus renewed are elected of God, holy, and therefore the more beloved of him. Holiness is the consequence of their election, and God's superior love, of their holiness. Forbearing one another - If anything is now wrong. And forgiving one another - What is past.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Col 3:12–17)

Reformed Notable voices

“The rendering that has been given by others, "super omnia haec," (above all these things,) instead of insuper, (over and above,) is, in my opinion, meagre. It would be more suitable to render it, Before all these things. I have chosen, however, the more ordinary signification of the word epi.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Colossians (on Col 3:12–17)
