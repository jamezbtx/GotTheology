Passage

Colossians 3

Verses 18–25 · ESV · Voices

Paul’s household code under the Lordship of Christ—mutual duties shaped by “in the Lord,” with special weight on husbands’ love, fathers’ gentleness, and workers’ sincere service to Christ, who grants the inheritance as reward and judges without partiality. Read pastorally: acknowledge the first-century slavery setting carefully without baptizing injustice, and keep Christ as true Master and impartial Judge.

Colossians 3:18

18Wives, submit to your husbands, as is fitting in the Lord.

Arminian

Wives are addressed first within a mutual household pattern soon balanced by husbands’ love. Submission is qualified—“as is fitting in the Lord”—not as absolute surrender to sin or abuse.

Arminian pastoral: grace enables willing, Christ-shaped order in marriage without erasing the wife’s dignity or moral agency. “In the Lord” keeps Jesus’ character as the measure of what is fitting.

Reformed

Household order is placed under Christ’s lordship. Fitting submission is not worldly inferiority but a calling within the Lord’s household design.

Reformed care: “in the Lord” bounds and dignifies the command. Christ’s authority relativizes cultural tyranny; the wife’s allegiance to Jesus remains primary.

Key contrast Both refuse to treat submission as license for oppression and both keep “in the Lord” central. Arminians typically stress grace-enabled, responsible embrace of fitting order; Reformed readers typically stress Christ’s sovereign ordering of the household as the sphere where this calling is lived.

Colossians 3:19

19Husbands, love your wives, and do not be harsh with them.

Arminian

Husbands receive a twin charge: love, and refuse harshness. Authority without tenderness contradicts the Lord who defines the household.

Arminian weight: enabling grace makes self-giving love possible; husbands remain accountable not to weaponize headship. Harshness is named as sin to put away.

Reformed

Love and non-harshness restrain and redefine any claim of male power. The husband’s duty is cruciform care, not domination.

Reformed pastoral: Christlike love is the husband’s primary household calling. Providence may give responsibility; the cross forbids cruelty.

Colossians 3:20

20Children, obey your parents in everything, for this pleases the Lord.

Arminian

Children are called to comprehensive obedience that pleases the Lord—discipleship begins at home. “In everything” assumes parents leading toward what honors Christ, not into evil.

Arminian formation: grace teaches young hearts to honor parents as a sincere act of pleasing Jesus; obedience is real responsibility under enabling help.

Reformed

Filial obedience is God-pleasing worship in ordinary life. The Lord’s pleasure, not mere parental preference, is the stated motive.

Reformed note: the fifth commandment’s echo stands inside Christ’s household. Children learn the Lord’s rule first in the home He orders.

Colossians 3:21

21Fathers, do not provoke your children, lest they become discouraged.

Arminian

Fathers (and parental authority) are warned against provocation that crushes spirit. Discipline without discouragement is the aim.

Arminian pastoral: grace equips parents to correct without embittering. They are responsible not to use authority in ways that quench a child’s hope.

Reformed

Authority is bounded by care for the child’s heart. Provocation that breeds despair contradicts the Father whose discipline aims at life.

Reformed wisdom: household headship includes restraint. Christlike fathers refuse patterns that manufacture discouragement in the little ones entrusted to them.

Colossians 3:22

22Bondservants, obey in everything those who are your earthly masters, not by way of eye-service, as people-pleasers, but with sincerity of heart, fearing the Lord.

Arminian

Paul addresses bondservants inside a fallen social structure—not baptizing slavery as ideal, but directing those in it toward sincerity and fear of the Lord rather than mere eye-service.

Arminian pastoral: even constrained callings can be offered to God with integrity. Grace enables honest work before the true Lord; this verse must never be twisted to excuse trafficking or cruelty.

Reformed

Service is relocated under the fear of the Lord. Earthly masters are real, yet not ultimate; sincerity replaces performance for human eyes.

Reformed care: Scripture regulates a then-present institution without declaring it the moral climax of the gospel. Christ remains Master; the text calls for heart-deep obedience to Him amid hard structures, while His impartial justice (v. 25) hangs over every master.

Key contrast Both reject using this verse to sanctify injustice. Arminians typically stress sincere, grace-enabled integrity toward God within unjust structures while keeping moral agency; Reformed readers typically stress the Lord’s sovereign claim on the worker’s heart and the relative, non-ultimate status of earthly masters under providence.

Colossians 3:23

23Whatever you do, work heartily, as for the Lord and not for men,

Arminian

Hearty work “as for the Lord” reframes labor. Human bosses do not own the deepest why of the task.

Arminian vocation: enabling grace makes wholehearted work unto Jesus possible in ordinary jobs. Believers are responsible to aim their effort at Him, not at empty people-pleasing.

Reformed

Every task can be rendered unto the Lord. The horizontal workplace is verticalized by Christ’s claim.

Reformed dignity: work as worship under providence. Men are secondary audiences; the Lord’s pleasure defines heartiness.

Colossians 3:24

24knowing that from the Lord you will receive the inheritance as your reward. You are serving the Lord Christ.

Arminian

Inheritance as reward comes from the Lord—astonishing for bondservants who owned little. They are serving the Lord Christ Himself.

Arminian hope: grace guarantees a real inheritance for those who remain in Christ; faithful service is meaningfully rewarded without becoming meritorious purchase of salvation.

Reformed

The Lord Himself pays the inheritance-reward. Identity is restated: you are serving the Lord Christ—earthly status cannot cancel that.

Reformed comfort: eschatological inheritance is secure in Christ. Labor under harsh masters is not unseen; the true Master keeps the books of grace.

Key contrast Both affirm inheritance from the Lord for those serving Christ. Arminians typically hold reward together with persevering, grace-enabled faithfulness; Reformed readers typically stress the certainty of the inheritance God grants in Christ, with hearty service as evidence of belonging to Him.

Colossians 3:25

25For the wrongdoer will be paid back for the wrong he has done, and there is no partiality.

Arminian

Impartial payback lands on the wrongdoer—comfort for the oppressed and warning for abusers of power. God shows no favoritism to status.

Arminian justice: the sincere moral order of God’s judgment calls everyone—masters and servants—to repent. Grace offers mercy now; unrepented wrong will be repaid.

Reformed

Divine impartiality underwrites both consolation and terror. Wrongdoing is not shielded by rank; providence will settle accounts.

Reformed gravity: the Judge of all shows no partiality. Household power is temporary; Christ’s tribunal relativizes every earthly advantage and vindicates His righteousness.

Key contrast Both confess impartial judgment on wrongdoing. Arminians typically emphasize a real, grace-attended call to repent before that payday; Reformed readers typically emphasize God’s certain, sovereign justice that will repay without respect of persons.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Paul’s household code under the Lordship of Christ—mutual duties shaped by “in the Lord—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Colossians 3:18–25.

Arminian Notable voices

“It is properly a military term, alluding to that entire submission that soldiers pay to their general. Be not bitter - (Which may be without any appearance of anger) either in word or spirit. Lest they be discouraged - Which may occasion their turning either desperate or stupid.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Col 3:18–25)

Reformed Notable voices

“Now follow particular duties, as they are called, which depend on the calling of individuals. In handling these it were superfluous to take up many words, inasmuch as I have already stated in the Epistle to the Ephesians almost everything that was necessary.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Colossians (on Col 3:18–25)
