Passage

Colossians 3

Verses 1–11 · ESV · Voices

After Colossians 2’s warning against hollow philosophy, Paul grounds holy living in union with the risen Christ—seek the things above, put to death earthly vices, and put on the new self where ethnic and social barriers fall before Christ who is all and in all.

Colossians 3:1

1If then you have been raised with Christ, seek the things that are above, where Christ is, seated at the right hand of God.

Arminian

The “if” assumes a real sharing in Christ’s resurrection for those who are His. Because they have been raised with Him, they are summoned to seek what is above—where the seated Christ reigns.

Arminian pastoral: prevenient and renewing grace make this seeking possible; the command still calls for responsible pursuit. Heavenly-mindedness is not escape from earth but obedience oriented to the risen Lord.

Reformed

Union language frames the ethic: those raised with Christ already share His resurrection life. The imperative rests on indicative grace already given in Him.

Reformed accent: seek what is above because Christ is seated at God’s right hand. Sanctification flows from that union; the throne of the exalted Lord defines the believer’s true north.

Key contrast Both ground seeking in resurrection union with Christ. Arminians typically stress grace that enables a real, resistible-in-principle pursuit of heavenly things; Reformed readers typically stress effectual union that already relocates the believer’s life and makes seeking the fruit of that bond.

Colossians 3:2

2Set your minds on things that are above, not on things that are on earth.

Arminian

Mind-setting is deliberate. Paul contrasts above with earth—not as hatred of creation but as refusal to let earthly measures rule the heart.

Classical Arminians hear a grace-enabled charge: the Spirit illumines and strengthens, yet believers must not drift back to earthbound fixation. Attention is a moral act under assisting grace.

Reformed

The renewed mind is redirected to Christ’s realm. Earthly things are not denied as God’s gifts, but they must not claim lordship over affection and thought.

Reformed pastoral: providence and union teach where to look. The Spirit who raised Christ also reorders meditation so that earthly cares sit under heavenly priority.

Colossians 3:3

3For you have died, and your life is hidden with Christ in God.

Arminian

Death with Christ explains the new orientation: the old life’s claim is broken. Life “hidden with Christ in God” is secure yet not fully unveiled—identity is located in Him, not in visible status.

Arminian comfort and charge: grace places life in Christ’s keeping; believers remain responsible to live from that hidden identity and not dig up what died with Him.

Reformed

Participatory death and hidden life are gospel facts, not mere metaphors. The believer’s true life is concealed in Christ within God—safe beyond the world’s verdict.

Reformed assurance: what God has hidden in the Son cannot finally be lost to earthly failure. Identity is Christ-held; sanctification works out what union has already secured.

Key contrast Both confess death-with-Christ and a life hidden in Him. Arminians typically emphasize a grace-wrought status that calls for ongoing, non-coerced faithfulness; Reformed readers typically emphasize an effectual, God-kept union that grounds assurance while still commanding holy living.

Colossians 3:4

4When Christ who is your life appears, then you also will appear with him in glory.

Arminian

Christ is “your life”—present possession—and His appearing will unveil shared glory. Hope looks forward without denying present responsibility.

Arminian hope: the sincere promise of appearing with Him strengthens perseverance. Those who remain in Him by grace-enabled faith will share His glory when He is revealed.

Reformed

Christ Himself is the believer’s life; future appearing with Him in glory is certain for those united to Him. Eschatology crowns present union.

Reformed comfort: glory is not self-earned unveiling but the public display of what God has already hidden in His Son. Hope rests on Christ’s appearing, not on fragile self-assessment.

Colossians 3:5

5Put to death therefore what is earthly in you: sexual immorality, impurity, passion, evil desire, and covetousness, which is idolatry.

Arminian

“Therefore” ties mortification to resurrection life. Paul names desires that enthrone the self and calls covetousness idolatry—wrong love of goods is false worship.

Arminian urgency: enabling grace makes killing sin possible; the command is real. Believers must not toy with earthly lusts they once walked in, but actively put them to death.

Reformed

Mortification is the necessary fruit of having died and been raised with Christ. The catalogue exposes heart-idols, climaxing in covetousness as worship of created things.

Reformed realism: the Spirit works death to sin in those united to Christ. The imperative assumes regenerating power already given; holy severity toward lust is gratitude, not self-salvation.

Key contrast Both treat putting sin to death as non-negotiable. Arminians typically stress grace-enabled, responsible mortification that must not be resisted; Reformed readers typically stress Spirit-effectual killing of sin as the outworking of union with the crucified and risen Christ.

Colossians 3:6

6On account of these the wrath of God is coming.

Arminian

Wrath is coming—not theatrical scare but moral seriousness. Earthly vices invite God’s settled opposition to evil.

Arminian warning: the same God who sincerely offers mercy also judges unrepented idolatry. Grace that enables repentance makes the warning a real call to turn.

Reformed

Divine wrath against these practices is certain and just. Paul’s “is coming” keeps eschatological judgment before the church’s eyes.

Reformed sobriety: holiness is not optional polish. Those delivered from wrath in Christ still hear why the old walk was lethal—and why providence will not finally wink at idolatry.

Colossians 3:7

7In these you too once walked, when you were living in them.

Arminian

Paul reminds them of a former walk—living in the vices they must now kill. Memory of grace-rescued past fuels gratitude, not nostalgia.

Arminian pastoral: they once lived there; they need not return. Enabling grace has opened a new path; remaining in the old life would be culpable refusal of light already given.

Reformed

“Once” marks a decisive break wrought by God’s saving work. They lived in those things; they do so no longer as their defining realm.

Reformed remembrance: recalling the old walk magnifies mercy. Conversion is God’s transfer of dominion; the past is confessed so that present holiness looks like belonging to Christ.

Colossians 3:8

8But now you must put them all away: anger, wrath, malice, slander, and obscene talk from your mouth.

Arminian

“But now” shifts from sexual and covetous sins to relational toxins of speech and temper. Putting away is comprehensive—“all.”

Arminian charge: grace that cleansed the walk also summons clean mouths and hearts. Believers remain responsible to cast off anger’s chain before it hardens into malice and slander.

Reformed

The new era (“now”) demands disposal of old community poisons. Speech sins destroy the body Christ is forming.

Reformed pastoral: putting away is Spirit-wrought fruit of the new creation. The church’s speech should match those raised with Christ, not the old humanity’s rage and contempt.

Colossians 3:9

9Do not lie to one another, seeing that you have put off the old self with its practices

Arminian

Lying is banned among those who have already put off the old self. The indicative—“you have put off”—grounds the imperative against falsehood.

Arminian reading: conversion includes a real break with the old self’s practices; grace enabled that putting off, and ongoing honesty is the fitting response we must not refuse.

Reformed

Truth-telling belongs to those who have shed the old self. The aorist “put off” points to a decisive gospel change already accomplished in union with Christ.

Reformed logic: ethics follow identity. Because the old man is discarded with his practices, lying contradicts who they already are in Christ.

Key contrast Both tie honesty to having put off the old self. Arminians typically stress a grace-enabled change that still requires continual, non-coerced obedience; Reformed readers typically stress a decisive, God-wrought stripping of the old man whose practices no longer define the elect.

Colossians 3:10

10and have put on the new self, which is being renewed in knowledge after the image of its creator.

Arminian

Putting on the new self pairs with putting off the old. Renewal is ongoing—“being renewed”—in knowledge patterned after the Creator’s image.

Arminian hope: grace restores the image through growing knowledge of God; believers cooperate with that renewing work and must not resist the Spirit’s shaping.

Reformed

The new self is donned in Christ and continually renewed by God toward the Creator’s image. Knowledge here is transformative, not mere data.

Reformed confidence: sanctification is God’s progressive remaking of those united to Christ. The image spoiled in Adam is restored by the Creator’s own renewing power.

Key contrast Both celebrate the new self renewed toward God’s image. Arminians typically emphasize ongoing, grace-enabled cooperation in that renewal; Reformed readers typically emphasize God’s effectual renewing work in those already clothed with the new self in Christ.

Colossians 3:11

11Here there is not Greek and Jew, circumcised and uncircumcised, barbarian, Scythian, slave, free; but Christ is all, and in all.

Arminian

In the new-self community, ethnic, ritual, cultural, and status barriers lose their dividing power. Christ is all—and in all who belong to Him.

Arminian welcome: the gospel’s sincere breadth gathers every kind of person into one renewed people. No pedigree or chains define worth where Christ fills the field of vision.

Reformed

Union with Christ dissolves the old humanity’s ranking systems. Greek/Jew, rite, culture, and bondage/freedom are not ultimate; Christ is all and in all His people.

Reformed doxology: the church’s unity is Christ-centered, not sentiment. Those He indwells share one new identity that relativizes every earthly hierarchy without denying creaturely callings.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Paul grounds holy living in union with the risen Christ—seek the things above, put to death earthly vices—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Colossians 3:1–11.

Arminian Notable voices

“If ye are risen, seek the things above - As Christ being risen, immediately went to heaven. For ye are dead - To the things on earth. And your real, spiritual life is hid from the world, and laid up in God, with Christ - Who hath merited, promised, prepared it for us, and gives us the earnest and foretaste of it in our hearts.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Col 3:1–11)

Reformed Notable voices

“Ascension follows resurrection: hence, if we are the members of Christ we must ascend into heaven, because he, on being raised up from the dead, was received up into heaven, (Mark 16:19,) that he might draw us up with him. Now, we seek those things which are above, when in our minds we are truly sojourners in this world, and are not bound to it.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Colossians (on Col 3:1–11)
