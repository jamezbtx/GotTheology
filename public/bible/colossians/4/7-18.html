Passage

Colossians 4

Verses 7–18 · ESV · Voices

Paul’s final greetings: trusted coworkers (Tychicus, Onesimus, Aristarchus, Mark, Justus, Epaphras, Luke, Demas), sister churches in the Lycus Valley, a charge to Archippus, and a handwritten close from prison—grace as the last word.

Colossians 4:7

7Tychicus will tell you all about my activities. He is a beloved brother and faithful minister and fellow servant in the Lord.

Arminian

News travels by a trusted person. Tychicus is beloved, faithful, and a fellow servant—titles that honor character more than status.

Arminian pastoral reading: grace forms reliable partners in mission. Faithfulness is real human responsibility under the Lord; the church thrives when such servants are received with trust.

Reformed

God provides ministers for the church’s comfort and continuity. Tychicus’s triple description—beloved brother, faithful minister, fellow servant—sketches gospel partnership under Christ’s Lordship.

Reformed note: providence uses named people. The same Lord who authored the letter also sends the messenger who will explain Paul’s circumstances.

Colossians 4:8

8I have sent him to you for this very purpose, that you may know how we are and that he may encourage your hearts,

Arminian

Purpose is pastoral: information and encouragement. Knowing how the apostle fares strengthens anxious hearts.

Arminian warmth: God enables mutual care through messengers. Encouragement is a grace we both receive and actively give; hearts remain responsible to take heart when truth arrives.

Reformed

Paul sends with deliberate purpose. Knowledge of the apostle’s state and encouragement of hearts are means of grace for a young church.

Reformed comfort: God who elects and preserves also shepherds through news and presence. Encouragement is not fluff; it is appointed strengthening for saints He will keep.

Colossians 4:9

9and with him Onesimus, our faithful and beloved brother, who is one of you. They will tell you of everything that has taken place here.

Arminian

Onesimus—once a runaway slave in Philemon’s story—is now “faithful and beloved,” “one of you.” Grace restores and re-identifies.

Classical Arminians celebrate transforming grace that enables a new walk: the gospel really changes people who respond, and the church must welcome the restored as family, not as a permanent underclass.

Reformed

The gospel remakes social identity. Onesimus is named brother and faithful—proof that God’s effectual call creates new kinship in Christ.

Reformed accent: what grace begins, grace marks publicly. “One of you” is covenant belonging; the Colossians are to receive him as God has already claimed him.

Key contrast Both glory in Onesimus’s new status in Christ. Arminians typically stress grace-enabled transformation and the church’s duty to welcome the restored; Reformed readers typically stress effectual calling that creates irreversible belonging, displayed in faithful brotherhood.

Colossians 4:10

10Aristarchus my fellow prisoner greets you, and Mark the cousin of Barnabas (concerning whom you have received instructions—if he comes to you, welcome him),

Arminian

Greetings from a fellow prisoner and from Mark—once a source of sharp disagreement (Acts 15)—now to be welcomed. Restoration is possible.

Arminian hope: grace enables second chances. Prior failure need not define a servant forever; the church’s instructions to welcome Mark assume real reconciliation and responsible trust.

Reformed

The network of grace includes prisoners and restored coworkers. Mark’s welcome is commanded because God has useful purposes for him again.

Reformed note: providence writes long stories. Past fracture does not cancel future fruitfulness when the Lord restores; the church obeys by receiving whom God sends.

Colossians 4:11

11and Jesus who is called Justus. These are the only men of the circumcision among my fellow workers for the kingdom of God, and they have been a comfort to me.

Arminian

Jewish coworkers stand with Paul for the kingdom and bring comfort. Ethnic identity is neither erased nor made a barrier to gospel partnership.

Arminian reading: grace gathers diverse laborers who freely serve together. Comfort in hardship is a real gift the church should cultivate through loyal presence.

Reformed

Kingdom coworkers from the circumcision minority among Paul’s helpers underscore unity in Christ without erasing history. Their comfort is God’s kindness to the imprisoned apostle.

Reformed pastoral use: God supplies particular people as means of consolation. Fellowship is not accidental; it is providence timed for need.

Colossians 4:12

12Epaphras, who is one of you, a servant of Christ Jesus, greets you, always struggling on your behalf in his prayers, that you may stand mature and fully assured in all the will of God.

Arminian

Epaphras wrestles in prayer for maturity and full assurance in God’s will. Intercession is labor, not a casual wish.

Classical Arminians hear enabling grace in persistent prayer and a real aim: that believers stand mature. Assurance grows as grace is received and walked in; prayer partners labor so others will not drift.

Reformed

Agonizing prayer seeks what God alone gives: maturity and full assurance in His will. Epaphras’s struggle is the church’s appointed means under sovereignty.

Reformed comfort: assurance is not self-talk; it is Spirit-wrought certainty God is pleased to grant. Prayer “struggles” because the stakes are holiness and steadfastness God has promised to work.

Key contrast Both prize Epaphras’s praying for maturity and assurance. Arminians typically emphasize persevering, resistible partnership in prayer toward a stand believers must maintain; Reformed readers typically emphasize prayer as God’s means to secure the maturity and assurance He decrees to give.

Colossians 4:13

13For I bear him witness that he has worked hard for you and for those in Laodicea and in Hierapolis.

Arminian

Paul certifies Epaphras’s hard work across the Lycus Valley churches. Love shows up as labor for neighboring congregations too.

Arminian charge: grace-enabled zeal is measurable in service. Regional care is a responsible calling, not optional extracurricular piety.

Reformed

Apostolic witness confirms Epaphras’s toil. God raises servants whose sweat matches their prayers.

Reformed note: true ministry leaves a trail of work for particular people and places. Providence ties Colossae, Laodicea, and Hierapolis together under one gospel laborer.

Colossians 4:14

14Luke the beloved physician greets you, as does Demas.

Arminian

Luke the beloved physician and Demas send greetings. Later Demas will desert (2 Tim 4:10); here he still stands with Paul.

Arminian sobriety without straw men: present faithfulness is real and to be received; perseverance remains a live responsibility. Grace enables continuance, yet Scripture itself warns that some turn aside.

Reformed

Named coworkers again: Luke beloved for healing skill and gospel partnership; Demas included in the greeting.

Reformed reading (without smug hindsight): God’s people appear in the record at different stages. Final perseverance is God’s preserving work; interim greetings still honor real fellowship while warning that not all who start remain.

Colossians 4:15

15Give my greetings to the brothers at Laodicea, and to Nympha and the church in her house.

Arminian

Greetings extend to Laodicea and to Nympha’s house church. The gospel creates household congregations and networked affection.

Arminian warmth: grace forms hospitable communities. Hosting the church is a real ministry women and households may render; mutual greeting strengthens shared life.

Reformed

The body is larger than one city. House churches under hosts like Nympha are true churches under Christ’s headship.

Reformed accent: God plants gatherings in homes and ties them by apostolic greeting. Locality matters; isolation is not the Reformed ideal.

Colossians 4:16

16And when this letter has been read among you, have it also read in the church of the Laodiceans; and see that you also read the letter from Laodicea.

Arminian

Letters are for public reading and sharing. Scripture’s authority spreads as churches exchange and hear the apostolic word.

Arminian note: God gives a clear word the churches must actually read and heed. Responsibility to circulate and receive the letter is part of faithful response to grace.

Reformed

Canonical instinct in seed form: apostolic letters are read aloud and shared between congregations. The word rules the churches together.

Reformed conviction: God preserves and distributes His word through obedient churches. Reading is not optional enrichment; it is how the Lord governs His people.

Colossians 4:17

17And say to Archippus, “See that you fulfill the ministry that you have received in the Lord.”

Arminian

A pointed charge: fulfill the ministry received in the Lord. Calling is gift and task.

Classical Arminians stress real responsibility—grace confers ministry, and the servant must not neglect it. “See that you fulfill” assumes Archippus can attend or drift; enabling grace makes obedience possible without canceling the warning.

Reformed

Ministry is received “in the Lord”—origin and sphere are Christ’s. Fulfillment is obedience to a trust God assigned.

Reformed charge and comfort: God who gives the ministry also supplies strength to complete it. The public reminder is a means of perseverance for a named servant.

Key contrast Both treat ministry as received from the Lord and requiring fulfillment. Arminians typically emphasize resistible neglect as a real danger the servant must watch against; Reformed readers typically emphasize God’s effectual sustaining of the ministry He assigned, using exhortation as His means.

Colossians 4:18

18I, Paul, write this greeting with my own hand. Remember my chains. Grace be with you.

Arminian

A handwritten seal, a plea to remember his chains, and a final benediction of grace. Suffering and grace stand side by side.

Arminian close: grace is the atmosphere of the Christian life—freely offered, sincerely meant, sufficient for those who continue in it. Remembering the prisoner’s chains stirs prayer and solidarity without despair.

Reformed

Authenticity (own hand), solidarity (remember my chains), and doxology (grace be with you) close the letter. Grace has the last word because grace authored the whole.

Reformed doxology: chains cannot cancel grace; the God who imprisoned Paul for the mystery still keeps His church by that same grace to the end.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Paul’s final greetings: trusted coworkers (Tychicus, Onesimus, Aristarchus, Mark, Justus, Epaphras, Luke, Demas)—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Colossians 4:7–18.

Arminian Notable voices

“Aristarchus my fellowprisoner - Such was Epaphras likewise for a time, Phil. i, 23. Ye have received directions - Namely, by Tychicus, bringing this letter. The ancients adapted their language to the time of reading the letter; not, as we do, to the time when it was written.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Col 4:7–18)

Reformed Notable voices

“I do not agree with those who understand this to be Luke the Evangelist; for I am of opinion that he was too well known to stand in need of such a designation, and he would have been signalized by a more magnificent eulogium. He would, undoubtedly, have called him his fellow-helper, or at least his companion and participant in his conflicts.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Colossians (on Col 4:7–18)
