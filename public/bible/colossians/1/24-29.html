Passage

Colossians 1

Verses 24–29 · ESV · Voices

Paul turns from cosmic Christ to his own ministry: he rejoices in sufferings for the church, fills up what remains of Christ’s afflictions in his flesh (never an atoning lack), stewards the once-hidden mystery now revealed—Christ in you, the hope of glory—and toils to proclaim, warn, and teach everyone mature in Christ by God’s own energy.

Colossians 1:24

24Now I rejoice in my sufferings for your sake, and in my flesh I am filling up what is lacking in Christ's afflictions for the sake of his body, that is, the church,

Arminian

Paul rejoices in sufferings for the Colossians’ sake—not grim endurance but glad participation. “What is lacking” is not a deficit in the cross’s atoning value; Christ’s once-for-all sacrifice is complete. The lack is the still-unfolding afflictions His body bears as the gospel advances.

Classical Arminians hear a willing steward: grace enables Paul to offer his flesh for the church without adding to Calvary. We may likewise choose, under assisting grace, to suffer for others’ good while resting wholly in Christ’s finished work.

Reformed

Joy in affliction is not stoicism; it is union with Christ for the church’s sake. Reformed readers insist with Paul that nothing is lacking in the atonement—the “lack” is the remaining share of Messianic sufferings appointed for the body until the mission is done.

Christ’s afflictions continue in His members under the Father’s providence. Paul’s flesh fills what the Head still assigns to His body; the cross remains sufficient, and the church is served by sufferings God orders for her good.

Colossians 1:25

25of which I became a minister according to the stewardship from God that was given to me for you, to make the word of God fully known,

Arminian

Ministry is stewardship from God, given for the Colossians, aimed at making the word fully known. Office is gift and trust, not self-appointment.

Arminian accent: God gives the stewardship; Paul must faithfully discharge it. Grace confers the charge and enables the labor, while the steward remains responsible to open the word completely to those he serves.

Reformed

Paul became a minister by divine assignment—stewardship given, not seized. The goal is full disclosure of God’s word to the church.

Reformed note: vocation and content both come from God. The apostle’s authority and the completeness of the word he proclaims rest on the Giver who appointed him for their sake.

Colossians 1:26

26the mystery hidden for ages and generations but now revealed to his saints.

Arminian

The mystery was hidden across ages, then revealed to the saints. God times disclosure; the church receives what prophets longed to see.

Classical Arminians rejoice that revelation is now public among the holy ones. Grace opens the secret; saints are called to receive, guard, and share what God has made known rather than chase private shortcuts around the gospel.

Reformed

Mystery language marks redemptive history: long concealment, then revelation to the saints. God is the revealer; the church is the audience He chose.

Reformed comfort: what was hidden is now given. Saints do not invent the mystery; they receive God’s unveiling in the fullness of time according to His wise counsel.

Colossians 1:27

27To them God chose to make known how great among the Gentiles are the riches of the glory of this mystery, which is Christ in you, the hope of glory.

Arminian

God chose to make known among the Gentiles the glory-rich mystery: Christ in you, the hope of glory. Indwelling Christ is present riches and future hope.

Arminian pastoral stress: the invitation reaches the nations in sincerity. Those who receive Christ by faith have Him within as hope—not mere optimism, but confident expectation of glory sustained by grace that can be resisted or embraced.

Reformed

God’s choice governs the disclosure: Gentile inclusion and “Christ in you” as hope of glory. The mystery’s center is union with Christ now and glory ahead.

Reformed emphasis: God made known what He willed to reveal. Indwelling presence and hope of glory are gifts of sovereign grace to the saints, securing assurance that glory will follow Christ in them.

Colossians 1:28

28Him we proclaim, warning everyone and teaching everyone with all wisdom, that we may present everyone mature in Christ.

Arminian

The content is Him—Christ proclaimed—with warning and teaching for everyone, aimed at maturity in Christ. The thrice-repeated “everyone” presses a wide, sincere ministry.

Classical Arminians hear a real offer and a real goal: warn and teach so that people may be presented mature. Grace enables the labor and the growth; hearers remain accountable to heed the warning and receive the teaching.

Reformed

Proclamation centers on Christ: warn, teach, with all wisdom, toward presenting everyone mature in Him. Scope is universal in address; maturity is conformity to Christ.

Reformed reading: the same gospel confronts and instructs. God uses this ministry to bring His people to maturity; the “everyone” of the apostolic charge matches the breadth of the church Christ is forming.

Colossians 1:29

29For this I toil, struggling with all his energy that he powerfully works within me.

Arminian

Paul toils and struggles—real human effort—yet with Christ’s energy powerfully at work in him. Labor and divine power are not rivals.

Arminian balance: we strive earnestly, and enabling grace supplies the strength. Paul’s sweat is genuine; so is the power within. Neither laziness nor self-reliance fits the verse.

Reformed

Toil and struggle are Paul’s; the energy is Christ’s, powerfully working within. Sanctified labor is energized from above.

Reformed comfort: effectual divine power does not cancel effort—it produces it. The apostle’s ministry runs on the strength God works in him, so boasting lands on the One who energizes the struggle.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Paul turns from cosmic Christ to his own ministry: he rejoices in sufferings for the church—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Colossians 1:24–29.

Arminian Notable voices

“Now I rejoice in my sufferings for you, and fill up - That is, whereby I fill up. That which is behind of the sufferings of Christ - That which remains to be suffered by his members. These are termed the sufferings of Christ, Because the suffering of any member is the suffering of the whole; and of the head especially, which supplies strength, spirits, sense, and motion to all.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Col 1:24–29)

Reformed Notable voices

“He has previously claimed for himself authority on the ground of his calling. Now, however, he provides against the honor of his apostleship being detracted from by the bonds and persecutions, which he endured for the sake of the gospel. For Satan, also, perversely turns these things into occasions of rendering the servants of God the more contemptible.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Colossians (on Col 1:24–29)
