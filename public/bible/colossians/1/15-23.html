Passage

Colossians 1

Verses 15–23 · ESV · Voices

Paul lifts the Colossians’ eyes to the cosmic Christ hymn: the Son as image of the invisible God, firstborn over creation and from the dead, Creator and Sustainer, Head of the church, in whom all fullness dwells. Through His cross God reconciles all things, making peace; then the hymn turns personal—once alienated, now reconciled in His body of flesh, to be presented holy if they continue in the faith and hope of the gospel Paul serves.

Colossians 1:15

15He is the image of the invisible God, the firstborn of all creation.

Arminian

Christ is the image of the invisible God—not a lesser copy but the true revealing of the Father—and “firstborn of all creation,” a title of rank and priority, not of coming into being as a creature. The hymn opens with shared high Christology: Jesus shows us God and stands over all that is made.

Classical Arminians gladly confess the Son’s eternal deity and supremacy. The image who reveals the Father is the Mediator through whom sinners may truly know God; firstborn language crowns Him, it does not demote Him.

Reformed

Image and firstborn name Christ’s unique glory: the invisible God is made known in Him, and He holds the rights of the Firstborn over creation. Neither title allows Arian reduction; both exalt the Son.

Reformed confession: Christ alone is the mirror in whom we see God, and firstborn marks His begotten priority and dominion, not creaturehood. Seek God nowhere apart from this Son.

Colossians 1:16

16For by him all things were created, in heaven and on earth, visible and invisible, whether thrones or dominions or rulers or authorities—all things were created through him and for him.

Arminian

All things—heaven and earth, visible and invisible, including thrones and powers—were created by Him, through Him, and for Him. Angels and empires alike are His handiwork and goal, not rivals to worship.

Arminian pastoral weight: creation’s breadth undercuts every lesser lordship that would dilute Christ. The same Lord who made all sincerely offers reconciliation; creaturely powers must not become substitutes for the Creator-Son.

Reformed

Paul stacks spheres and ranks to leave nothing outside Christ’s creative agency: by Him, through Him, for Him. Invisible authorities are creatures, not co-creators.

Reformed accent: cosmic scope belongs to the Son by right. Every throne exists for His glory; the church need not fear or flatter powers that He made and that serve His ends.

Colossians 1:17

17And he is before all things, and in him all things hold together.

Arminian

He is before all things—preexistent priority—and in Him all things hold together. Continuance as well as origin depends on the Son; the universe is not self-sustaining.

Classical Arminians hear both comfort and call: the One who upholds creation also upholds believers who cling to Him. Dependence on Christ is the creature’s true posture, gladly confessed and continually lived.

Reformed

“Before all things” and “hold together” confess Christ’s eternal precedence and present providence. Nothing coheres apart from Him.

Reformed comfort: the Son’s sustaining power is as sure as His creating word. The same Lord who keeps galaxies keeps His people; coherence in Christ is gift before it is grit.

Colossians 1:18

18And he is the head of the body, the church. He is the beginning, the firstborn from the dead, that in everything he might be preeminent.

Arminian

Head of the body—the church—He is also beginning and firstborn from the dead, so that in everything He might be preeminent. Cosmic Lord and risen Head meet in one Person for His people’s good.

Arminian reading: union with the Head is living relationship. Preeminence invites trust and obedience in the body; the church thrives by holding fast to Him who rose first, not by drifting toward angel lore or hollow philosophy.

Reformed

Headship over the church pairs with firstborn-from-the-dead status: resurrection primacy aims at universal preeminence. Christ is not Head in name only.

Reformed note: the body’s life flows from the risen Head God has exalted. Preeminence is the Father’s design for the Son in creation and new creation alike—comfort that the church’s center is fixed.

Colossians 1:19

19For in him all the fullness of God was pleased to dwell,

Arminian

The Father was pleased that all the fullness of God should dwell in Him—not a partial share of deity but fullness at home in the Son. Pleasure language marks willing divine design, not reluctant concession.

Classical Arminians and Reformed stand together against every thinning of Christ: fullness dwells in Him. The Arminian accent adds that this full Christ is sincerely set forth for sinners to receive by faith.

Reformed

Fullness of God dwelling in Him is the ground of the hymn’s claims. Nothing of God is missing from the Son; the Father’s good pleasure places that fullness there.

Reformed confession: hypostatic fullness answers every rival mediator. If all fullness dwells in Christ, seeking supplements elsewhere is not humility—it is unbelief.

Colossians 1:20

20and through him to reconcile to himself all things, whether on earth or in heaven, making peace by the blood of his cross.

Arminian

Through Him God reconciles all things to Himself—earth and heaven—making peace by the blood of His cross. The cross is the peace-making center; cosmic language shows the reach of that peace, not a vague universalism that erases faith.

Classical Arminians often hear “all things” as the wide, sincere scope of Christ’s reconciling work and gospel offer, while personal application still requires faith (vv. 21–23). Peace is blood-bought and truly held out; enemies must receive it.

Reformed

Reconciliation of all things through the cross’s blood centers peace in Christ’s once-for-all sacrifice. Cosmic wording displays the cross’s supremacy over every estranged realm under God’s purpose.

Reformed reading typically ties this reconciling purpose to Christ’s particular mediatorial work for His people, while confessing that the same blood will bring final cosmic peace under His headship—not that every rebel is automatically saved apart from faith.

Colossians 1:21

21And you, who once were alienated and hostile in mind, doing evil deeds,

Arminian

The hymn turns to “you”: once alienated and hostile in mind, doing evil deeds. Estrangement was real—mind and practice allied against God—not a mild misunderstanding.

Arminian pastoral honesty: hostility can be left behind when grace is received, and it can be rekindled if the heart drifts. Remembering what we were fuels gratitude and watchfulness.

Reformed

Paul names their former state without flattery: alienated, hostile in mind, evil deeds. Grace shines brighter against that backdrop.

Reformed diagnosis: enmity was deep and deserved. The “once” prepares the “now” of reconciliation—God’s initiative toward those who were not seeking Him aright.

Colossians 1:22

22he has now reconciled in his body of flesh by his death, in order to present you holy and blameless and above reproach before him,

Arminian

He has now reconciled them in His body of flesh by His death—real incarnation, real death—aiming to present them holy, blameless, and above reproach before Him. Reconciliation is accomplished and applied toward a holy presentation.

Classical Arminians stress that this peace is received by faith and aims at a real holiness of life. The goal is not bare pardon on paper but people presented fit for His presence, by grace that enables and calls for continuance.

Reformed

“Now reconciled” in Christ’s fleshly body by His death states a finished reconciling act with a holy presentation as its purpose. The body of flesh is the church’s Savior, not a metaphor.

Reformed comfort: justification’s peace and sanctification’s aim belong together in union with the crucified Son. He reconciles in order to present—God’s purpose holds both the cross and the final blameless standing.

Colossians 1:23

23if indeed you continue in the faith, stable and steadfast, not shifting from the hope of the gospel that you heard, which has been proclaimed in all creation under heaven, and of which I, Paul, became a minister.

Arminian

“If indeed you continue in the faith”—stable, steadfast, not shifting from gospel hope. The worldwide proclamation and Paul’s ministry frame a real condition: reconciled people must cling to what they heard.

Classical Arminians hear a genuine warning and invitation. Grace enables perseverance; continuance in faith is the path of those who will be presented holy. Shifting from hope is a real danger the apostle refuses to soft-pedal.

Reformed

The “if” of continuance does not make salvation fragile guesswork; it describes the ordinary path of those truly reconciled—stable faith, steadfast hope, no shift from the gospel.

Reformed accent: perseverance of the saints is Spirit-wrought stability in the hope heard. Warnings are means God uses to keep His people; Paul’s ministry and the cosmic proclamation serve that persevering hold on Christ.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Christ’s person, supremacy, and reconciling work—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside the hymn.

Arminian Notable voices

“For God has unfolded in Christ all his own goodness. “For it pleased the Father, that in him should all fullness dwell” (Col. i. 19)… For this reason he is called “the image of the invisible God” (Col. i. 15)… in whom the Father condescends to afford to us his infinite majesty, his immeasurable goodness, mercy and philanthropy, to be contemplated… even as Christ himself says to Philip, “He that hath seen me, hath seen the Father.””
Jacobus Arminius · Works, Vol. 1 (Oration on the Object of Theology)
“The image of the invisible God—Whom none can represent, but his only begotten Son; in his divine nature the invisible image, in his human the visible image, of the Father. The first begotten of every creature—That is, begotten before every creature; subsisting before all worlds, before all time, from all eternity… Through death—Whereby he purchased the reconciliation which we receive by faith.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Col 1:15, 22)

Reformed Notable voices

“God in himself, that is, in his naked majesty, is invisible… and… is revealed to us in Christ alone, that we may behold him as in a mirror. For in Christ he shews us his righteousness, goodness, wisdom, power, in short, his entire self. We must, therefore, beware of seeking him elsewhere… Hence, he is not called the first-born, simply on the ground of his having preceded all creatures in point of time, but because he was begotten by the Father, that they might be created by him.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Colossians (on Col 1:15)
“The Lord Jesus, by his perfect obedience and sacrifice of himself, which he through the eternal Spirit once offered up unto God, hath fully satisfied the justice of his Father, and purchased not only reconciliation, but an everlasting inheritance in the kingdom of heaven, for all those whom the Father hath given unto him.”
Westminster Confession of Faith · 8.5 (1647)

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
