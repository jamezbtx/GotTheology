Passage

Colossians 1

Verses 1–14 · WEB · Voices

Paul greets the Colossian saints with Timothy, then thanks God for their faith, love, and hope rooted in the gospel that is bearing fruit worldwide. He keeps praying that they would be filled with knowledge of God’s will, walk worthy of the Lord, and remember the Father’s rescue: delivered from darkness, transferred into the Son’s kingdom, and given redemption, the forgiveness of our sins.

Colossians 1:1

1Paul, an apostle of Christ Jesus through the will of God, and Timothy our brother,

Arminian

Paul opens not with self-appointment but with vocation: an apostle of Christ Jesus “through the will of God,” writing with Timothy as brother. Authority serves the church; ministry is gift before it is status.

Classical Arminians gladly confess that God’s will initiates the apostolic office and the gospel’s advance. That same gracious will sincerely offers Christ to the world—calling hearers to respond in faith rather than treating grace as a closed circle.

Reformed

Paul’s apostleship rests on God’s will, not human vote. The letter begins under divine initiative: Christ Jesus sends; God wills; Timothy stands as brother in the same mission.

Reformed readers hear sovereignty already in the greeting. The messenger and the message arrive because God determined to speak through His apostle—comfort that the church’s foundation is God’s purpose, not Paul’s ambition.

Colossians 1:2

2to the saints and faithful brothers in Christ at Colossae: Grace to you and peace from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ.

Arminian

They are addressed as saints and faithful brothers “in Christ”—identity grounded in union with Jesus, located in an ordinary town. Grace and peace come from God our Father as gift, not mere formality.

In practice: holiness and fidelity describe those who have received the gospel in faith. The greeting assumes a living relationship that can be nurtured—and that must be guarded—under the Father’s ongoing favor.

Reformed

“Saints” and “faithful brothers in Christ” name a people set apart by God’s grace and kept in the Son. The blessing of grace and peace flows from the Father as covenant kindness.

Their standing is “in Christ” before it is “at Colossae.” What God has begun in them He addresses with fatherly peace—identity secured by union with the beloved Son.

Colossians 1:3

3We give thanks to God the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, praying always for you,

Arminian

Thanksgiving precedes correction. Paul and Timothy pray with gratitude to God, the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ—Trinitarian faith already warm and personal.

Arminians hear prayer as partnership with God’s work among a people who have freely embraced the gospel. Gratitude fuels intercession: what God has done invites continual asking for growth, not presumption that the race is finished.

Reformed

Paul’s first movement toward the Colossians is thanks to God. The Father of the Lord Jesus Christ is the Giver behind their new life; prayer acknowledges that.

Thanksgiving is theological before it is sentimental. If fruit appears in Colossae, God deserves the praise—grace that actually produced faith and love, not mere opportunity left unused.

Colossians 1:4

4having heard of your faith in Christ Jesus and of the love which you have toward all the saints,

Arminian

Faith in Christ Jesus and love for all the saints are the evidences that moved Paul to thanks. Trust directed to Christ overflows toward the whole household of faith.

Faith is the living condition by which sinners receive Christ, and love is its necessary fruit. Grace enables this response; it does not erase the call to believe and to love without partiality.

Reformed

Faith toward Christ and love toward the saints are twin signs of true conversion. Paul does not invent their story; he hears of what God has wrought.

Reformed reading stresses gift and fruit together: faith itself is God’s work in them, and love for all the saints displays the Spirit’s bonding power in the body of Christ.

Colossians 1:5

5because of the hope which is laid up for you in the heavens, of which you heard before in the word of the truth of the Good News

Arminian

Hope laid up in heaven fuels present faith and love. The gospel—the word of the truth—is how they first heard of this secure future.

Wesleyan Arminians gladly affirm an objective hope stored with God, sincerely announced in the gospel. That announcement calls for enduring trust; warnings elsewhere in the letter take perseverance seriously as the path of those who cling to this hope.

Reformed

Hope is “laid up”—reserved, kept, not fragile self-optimism. It is announced in the gospel as the word of the truth, so assurance rests on God’s promise more than on mood.

Comfort: heavenly hope is as secure as the God who stores it. The same gospel that informs also anchors; perseverance of the saints is sustained by a hope God Himself guards.

Colossians 1:6

6which has come to you, even as it is in all the world and is bearing fruit and growing, as it does in you also, since the day you heard and knew the grace of God in truth,

Arminian

The gospel comes, is heard, and is understood—grace of God grasped “in truth.” Worldwide it bears fruit and increases; locally the same pattern began the day they received it.

Pastorally: hearing and understanding are human responses under prevenient and assisting grace. The sincere offer goes out; fruit follows where the word is welcomed and not resisted.

Reformed

The gospel’s global fruitfulness and its local increase among them display God’s living power. From the day they heard and understood, grace has been productive—not sterile information.

The word that bears fruit does so because God makes it effectual. Understanding the grace of God in truth is itself a gift that issues in growth the apostle can celebrate.

Colossians 1:7

7even as you learned from Epaphras our beloved fellow servant, who is a faithful servant of Christ on your behalf,

Arminian

Epaphras taught them the gospel as a beloved fellow servant and faithful minister of Christ. Ordinary messengers matter; truth arrives through trusted servants.

Arminian readers honor such ministry as means of grace—Christ sincerely offered through faithful preaching. The Colossians’ responsibility remains: hold what they learned and not trade it for hollow philosophy later in the letter.

Reformed

God used Epaphras as Christ’s faithful minister on their behalf. Apostolic thanksgiving includes the human instrument without confusing instrument with source.

Faithful ministry is Christ’s gift to His church. Through such servants the Lord effectually plants the word; Epaphras’s fidelity is itself evidence of grace at work for the Colossians’ good.

Colossians 1:8

8who also declared to us your love in the Spirit.

Arminian

Their love is “in the Spirit”—affection generated and shaped by the Holy Spirit, reported back to Paul. Christian love is more than natural liking; it is Spirit-wrought charity.

The Spirit works in believers who yield to His leading. Love can grow or cool; hence the need for ongoing prayer (vv. 9–11) and watchfulness so grace is not quenched.

Reformed

Love in the Spirit is reported as fact: the Spirit has produced recognizable affection in them. Paul receives news of God’s interior work made public in community.

The Spirit’s fruit validates the gospel’s power among them. What Epaphras made known is not mere sentiment but evidence of the Spirit who applies Christ’s redemption.

Colossians 1:9

9For this cause, we also, since the day we heard this, don’t cease praying and making requests for you, that you may be filled with the knowledge of his will in all spiritual wisdom and understanding,

Arminian

Unceasing prayer seeks that they be filled with knowledge of God’s will—spiritual wisdom and understanding, not speculative trivia. Knowing God’s will is unto obedience and discernment.

Arminian interpreters see prayer as God’s appointed means that truly engages human and divine agency. Believers must keep seeking light; grace enables understanding that can still be neglected if the heart grows dull.

Reformed

From the day they heard, Paul has not ceased to pray—asking God to fill them with knowledge of His will in all spiritual wisdom and understanding.

Illumination is God’s work requested in prayer. The apostle does not leave growth to unaided resolve; he petitions the Father to supply the very knowledge that shapes a worthy walk.

Colossians 1:10

10that you may walk worthily of the Lord, to please him in all respects, bearing fruit in every good work and increasing in the knowledge of God,

Arminian

Knowledge aims at walking worthy of the Lord—fully pleasing Him in all respects, bearing fruit in every good work and still increasing in knowing God. Doctrine and ethics belong together.

Justified by faith, believers are summoned to a life that can please the Lord. Enabling grace makes obedience possible; the warnings of the letter treat that walk as a serious, resistible responsibility.

Reformed

The goal of filled knowledge is a walk worthy of the Lord, to please him, fruitful in every good work, and growing in the knowledge of God.

Good works are fruit of union with Christ, not the root of acceptance. Yet the accepted are genuinely called to please Him—sanctification powered by the same grace that saved them.

Colossians 1:11

11strengthened with all power, according to the might of his glory, for all endurance and perseverance with joy,

Arminian

Strength for endurance and perseverance with joy comes from God’s power according to His glorious might—not from stoic self-help. Joyful perseverance is Spirit-enabled stamina.

Arminians affirm strengthening offered and given in the Christian life, while urging believers not to resist or neglect that help. Endurance matters; apostasy texts elsewhere are heard as warnings to cling to the strength God supplies.

Reformed

Believers are strengthened with all power according to God’s glorious might—divine energy matched to the need for endurance, patience, and joy.

The might that upholds them is God’s own glory-strength. Perseverance of the saints is not bare human grit; it is the Lord sustaining His people for the long obedience with joy.

Colossians 1:12

12giving thanks to the Father, who made us fit to be partakers of the inheritance of the saints in light,

Arminian

Thanksgiving rises to the Father, who made us them to share the inheritance of the saints in light. Fitness for inheritance is gift, not self-made résumé.

The Father graciously qualifies those who are in Christ by faith. The inheritance is sincerely held out in the gospel; sharing it is tied to belonging to the Son and continuing in the hope they heard.

Reformed

The Father has qualified them—past tense grace—for a share in the saints’ inheritance in light. Unworthy people are made fit by God’s act, not by climbing into light on their own.

Comfort: qualification is the Father’s achievement for His people. Election and effectual grace stand behind the right to inherit with the saints; thanksgiving is the fitting response.

Colossians 1:13

13who delivered us out of the power of darkness, and translated us into the Kingdom of the Son of his love,

Arminian

Rescue language is decisive: delivered from darkness’s domain, transferred into the kingdom of the Father’s beloved Son. Conversion is a change of realm, not a mere mood shift.

Classical Arminians rejoice in this deliverance accomplished in Christ and received by faith. Grace that rescues can still be refused by those who remain in darkness; for those transferred, the call is to live as kingdom citizens and not drift back toward the old dominion.

Reformed

The Father has delivered and transferred—decisive verbs of salvation. Domain of darkness yields into the Kingdom of the Son of his beloved Son by God’s powerful act.

This is effectual rescue. Those transferred were not merely invited out of darkness; they were brought into the Son’s kingdom by sovereign grace that actually saves and relocates.

Colossians 1:14

14in whom we have our redemption, the forgiveness of our sins.

Arminian

In the Son we have redemption—defined as the forgiveness of sins. The heart of the gospel is release purchased and applied through Christ, received by faith.

Arminians and Reformed stand together here on justification’s core: forgiveness in Christ alone. The Arminian accent adds that this redemption is sincerely offered to all who believe, and that forgiven people must continue in Him who redeemed them.

Reformed

Redemption is located “in whom”—in the beloved Son—and unpacked as the forgiveness of sins. Union with Christ is the sphere of release from guilt.

Forgiveness is a present possession for those in Christ, grounded in His redeeming work. Grace does not merely make pardon possible; in the Son, the redeemed actually have it.

Classic Arminian and Reformed comment on Colossians 1:1–14, in the writers’ own words.

Arminian Notable voices

“The saints-This word expresses their union with God. And brethren — this, their union with their fellow-Christians. We give thanks - There is a near resemblance between this epistle, and those to the Ephesians and Philippians. In the word of truth, of the gospel - The true gospel preached to you.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Col 1:1–14)

Reformed Notable voices

“I have already, in repeated instances, explained the design of such inscriptions. As, however, the Colossians had never seen him, and on that account his authority was not as yet so firmly established among them as to make his private name by itself sufficient, he premises that he is an Apostle of Christ set apart by the will of God.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Colossians (on Col 1:1–14)
