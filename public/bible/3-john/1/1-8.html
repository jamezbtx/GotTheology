Passage

3 John 1

Verses 1–8 · ESV · Voices

The elder writes to the beloved Gaius, whom he loves in truth: prayer that body and affairs would match a prospering soul; joy that the brothers testified to Gaius’s walking in the truth; then praise for faithful work toward traveling brothers—strangers who have gone out for the Name, taking nothing from the Gentiles—so that those who send them are fellow workers for the truth.

3 John 1:1

1The elder to the beloved Gaius, whom I love in truth.

Arminian

The elder to the beloved Gaius, whom I love in truth. Wesley: Caius was probably that Caius of Corinth Paul mentions (Rom 16:23)—either removed into Asia, or this letter went to Corinth. Love in the truth matches 2 John 1:1: unfeigned, holy, because of the truth.

No extra fight. Gaius is named, beloved, loved in truth. The greeting is personal and already doctrinal only in that love and truth are not split.

Reformed

The elder to the beloved Gaius, loved in truth. Calvin did not comment 3 John; the Johannine pair is the same as 2 John: love located in the truth, not in temperament.

Shared: a real man, a real elder, love that has the truth as its cause. No extra fight. The letter will show what that love does for brothers on the road.

3 John 1:2

2Beloved, I pray that all may go well with you and that you may be in good health, as it goes well with your soul.

Arminian

Beloved, I pray that all may go well with you and that you may be in good health, as it goes well with your soul. The soul is the measure: Gaius’s inward life is already prospering; the prayer is that body and affairs would catch up.

Pastoral warmth, not a prosperity program. Wesley’s later note on v. 3—he uses all his talents to God’s glory—fits a soul that is already well. Shared joy in a brother’s health, inward first.

Reformed

Pray that all may go well, and health, as it goes well with the soul. The comparison assumes the soul’s well-being is visible enough to be the standard.

Shared: this is a pastor’s ordinary prayer, not a treatise. Calvin’s 1 John: the gospel’s knowledge is not ineffectual; here the soul’s prosperity is already the elder’s confidence. Body and affairs are asked to match what grace has already done within.

3 John 1:3

3For I rejoiced greatly when the brothers came and testified to your truth, as indeed you are walking in the truth.

Arminian

He rejoiced greatly when the brothers testified to Gaius’s truth, as indeed he is walking in the truth. Wesley: I know thou usest all thy talents to his glory. The truth that is in thee—the true faith and love.

Match 2 John 1:4 and 1 John: truth is walked, not claimed. The brothers’ testimony and the walk agree. No extra fight. Joy is the elder’s because the truth has become a path.

Reformed

Joy at testimony to Gaius’s truth, and he is walking in the truth. Calvin: he that saith he abides in Him ought to walk as He walked; likeness in life proves we abide in Christ.

Shared with Wesley and with 2 John 1:4: walking is the evidence. Reformed accent: the walk is fruit of abiding, not a ladder into the truth. The traveling brothers can see it because it is public.

3 John 1:4

4I have no greater joy than to hear that my children are walking in the truth.

Arminian

No greater joy than to hear that my children walk in the truth. Wesley: such is the spirit of every true Christian pastor. Caius was probably converted by Paul; when John calls him and other believers his children, it is the tender style of paternal love, whoever were the instruments. Using that name while writing as the elder has its peculiar beauty.

Match 2 John 1:4. Shared: the pastor’s greatest joy is children in the truth, not a fight over whose converts they were.

Reformed

No greater joy than children walking in the truth. Calvin’s pastoral match: the gospel is not ineffectual; obedience proceeds from it. The elder’s joy is that the truth has children who walk.

Shared: this is 2 John 1:4 in another house. Children is pastoral, not a metaphysical claim. Walking remains the Johannine test. No extra fight.

3 John 1:5

5Beloved, it is a faithful thing you do in all your efforts for these brothers, strangers as they are,

Arminian

Beloved, it is a faithful thing you do in all your efforts for these brothers, strangers as they are. Wesley: faithfully—uprightly and sincerely—whatsoever thou doest to the brethren and to strangers.

This is the inverse of 2 John 1:10–11: there, do not receive the deceiver who denies the flesh; here, receive true brothers, even strangers, who go out for the Name. Do not collapse both into a culture-war on hospitality politics. Faithful work toward gospel workers is the point. Strangers are still brothers in the truth.

Reformed

Faithful work for these brothers, strangers as they are. Calvin: let us not love in word or tongue, but in deed and in truth. Love is not idle.

Inverse of frozen 2 John 1:10–11: refuse the denier of the incarnation; welcome those who go out for the Name. Not a brief on modern hosting manners. Reformed: deed-love toward Christ’s workers is what 1 John 3:18 looked like in a doorway. Shared: Gaius’s efforts are faithfulness, not fashion.

3 John 1:6

6who testified to your love before the church. You will do well to send them on their journey in a manner worthy of God.

Arminian

They testified to his love before the church. Wesley: the congregation with whom I now reside. If thou send them forward, supplied with what is needful, thou shalt do well—how tenderly does the apostle enjoin this.

Love is shown in sending, worthy of God: provision for the road, not a slogan. Match 1 John: love walks and spends. The church has already heard the testimony; the elder asks Gaius to keep doing it well.

Reformed

Love testified before the church; send them in a manner worthy of God. Calvin: men are rightly loved when God holds the primacy; love of God is not idle.

Worthy of God sets the standard of the send-off: not stingy, not theatrical. Shared: the church already knows Gaius’s love because the travelers said so. Deed, not talk. Sending is the next act of the same love.

3 John 1:7

7For they have gone out for the sake of the name, accepting nothing from the Gentiles.

Arminian

They have gone out for the sake of the name, accepting nothing from the Gentiles. Wesley: they went forth to preach the gospel.

The Name is Jesus, confessed in the flesh (2 John 7). They take no living from the Gentile mission field so the gospel is not for sale. That is why Gaius’s purse matters. Inverse of 2 John 10: those who bring the teaching of Christ are to be helped, not housed as deceivers. No culture-war: this is support for named workers of the Name.

Reformed

Gone out for the Name, accepting nothing from the Gentiles. The Name is the same Christ 1 John and 2 John confess. They will not make the Gentiles pay for the message, so the churches of the Name must.

Shared: motive is the Name, not a hospitality ideology. Reformed: this is love in deed toward those in whom God holds the primacy. Taking nothing from the Gentiles keeps the work from looking like a trade.

3 John 1:8

8Therefore we ought to support people like these, that we may be fellow workers for the truth.

Arminian

Therefore we ought to support people like these, that we may be fellow workers for the truth. Wesley: to receive with all kindness. The truth—which they preach.

“We ought”: Gaius is not a special hobbyist; the church shares the work by sending. Fellow workers for the truth is the inverse of taking part in the deceiver’s wicked works (2 John 11). Receive the true; refuse the false. Both are about the teaching of Christ, not about contemporary politics of welcome.

Reformed

We ought to support such, that we may be fellow workers for the truth. Calvin’s 1 John: true love is toward God and is not idle; deed and truth, not tongue.

Shared: supporting Name-workers is partnership in the truth they preach. Reformed: the “ought” is the church’s, because the truth is God’s. Inverse of 2 John 1:10–11 remains the editorial key: same elder, two doors—one shut to antichrist-teaching, one open to those who go out for the Name. Neither door is a culture-war.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Gaius walking in the truth, joy over children in the truth, and love that sends workers for the Name—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside 3 John 1:1–8.

Arminian Notable voices

“The truth that is in thee—the true faith and love. I have no greater joy than this—such is the spirit of every true Christian pastor. To hear that my children walk in the truth. Faithfully—uprightly and sincerely. Who have testified of thy love before the church: whom if thou send forward on their journey, supplied with what is needful, thou shalt do well. They went forth—to preach the gospel. To receive—with all kindness. The truth—which they preach.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on 3 John 1–8)

Reformed Notable voices

“He that saith he abideth in him ought himself also so to walk, even as he walked. A likeness in life and deeds will prove that we abide in Christ. Little children, let us not love in word, neither in tongue, but in deed and in truth. He briefly shows what true love is, even that which is towards God; men are rightly loved when God holds the primacy. The love of God is not idle or inactive.”
John Calvin · Commentary on 1 John (on 1 John 2:6; 3:18; 5:2–3)

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named. Calvin left no commentary on 3 John; the Reformed voice here is his comment on walking in the truth and love in deed in 1 John, which this letter enacts.
