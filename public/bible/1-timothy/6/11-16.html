Passage

1 Timothy 6

Verses 11–16 · ESV · Voices

Timothy, addressed as a man of God, must flee greed and pursue righteousness, godliness, faith, love, steadfastness, and gentleness. He is to fight the good fight of the faith, take hold of the eternal life to which he was called, and keep the commandment unstained until Christ appears: before God who gives life to all things, and before the blessed and only Sovereign.

1 Timothy 6:11

11But as for you, O man of God, flee these things. Pursue righteousness, godliness, faith, love, steadfastness, gentleness.

Arminian

The man of God is set over against the craving just named. Flee these things as from a serpent; pursue a cluster of graces: righteousness, godliness, faith, love, steadfastness, gentleness. Wesley: a man devoted to God, a man of another world; faith is a supernatural sight of God's mercy in Christ, and love is the spring of inward and outward holiness.

Arminian pastoral: flight and pursuit are real acts under assisting grace. The title man of God does not cancel effort; it summons a messenger to a holy chase that can be neglected if the heart turns back to gold.

Reformed

Calvin: man of God adds weight. Follow righteousness, piety, faith, patience: by contrast he tells Timothy what riches to desire, spiritual ones, and so corrects avarice. He who is earnestly employed about necessary things will easily abstain from superfluous ones.

Reformed ethic: these virtues are not a self-made ladder into office. God names the fruit He works in His servant. Fleeing greed and pursuing gentleness belong together as the Spirit's training of a man already claimed.

1 Timothy 6:12

12Fight the good fight of the faith. Take hold of the eternal life to which you were called and about which you made the good confession in the presence of many witnesses.

Arminian

The fight is of the faith, not about words. Take hold of the eternal life to which you were called, and about which you already made the good confession before many witnesses. Wesley: lay hold, just before thee; the confession (perhaps at baptism) was one to which the witnesses assented.

Arminian urgency: calling and confession do not finish the race. Grace enables a resistible perseverance: keep fighting, keep laying hold. Eternal life is held out to be grasped, not presumed as an immovable possession apart from continuing faith.

Reformed

Calvin: Christ calls His servants to warfare, not to serve at ease. The fight is good, that is, successful, under Christ's banner where victory is certain and the reward is glorious immortality. Lay hold: God calls thee to eternal life; nothing has been done till we have obtained the life to come. The calling itself should animate us, because God directs the labor and stretches out His hand.

Reformed assurance: the God who called effectually also keeps. Taking hold is the appointed way the preserved actually obtain what He secured. Former confession adds obligation: the end should match the beginning.

Key contrast Both hear a summons to fight and to take hold of the eternal life already named in calling and confession. Arminians typically stress grace-enabled, resistible perseverance: lay hold and keep laying hold. Reformed readers typically stress that the God who called effectually also keeps; taking hold is how the preserved obtain what God has secured.

1 Timothy 6:13

13I charge you in the presence of God, who gives life to all things, and of Christ Jesus, who in his testimony before Pontius Pilate made the good confession,

Arminian

The charge is sworn in two presences: God who gives life to all things, and Christ Jesus who made the good confession before Pilate. Life-giving goodness is as wide as creation; the Son's public testimony is the pattern for Timothy's. Wesley: who hath quickened thee, and will quicken thee at the great day.

Arminian reading of gives life to all: God's kindness toward all He has made is sincere, not a mask. The same God who quickens creatures also offers living mercy in the gospel. Timothy answers before a generously life-giving Father and a confessing Christ.

Reformed

Calvin: ascribing to God that He quickens all things meets the offense of the cross, which shows only the appearance of death. Shut your eyes when ungodly men threaten death; fix them on God who restores the dead to life. Christ's confession before Pilate was ratified by His blood, a seal better than many words.

Reformed reading of gives life to all: providence and common kindness quicken every creature without converting that gift into a saving intention toward every person equally. The charge still stands: Timothy must confess as Christ confessed, because the Giver of life is Judge and Keeper.

Key contrast Both confess God as the Giver of life before whom Timothy is charged. Arminians typically hear sincere universal goodness: the God who gives life to all things is kindly toward all He has made. Reformed readers typically hear providence and common kindness that quickens creatures without implying a universal saving intent.

1 Timothy 6:14

14to keep the commandment unstained and free from reproach until the appearing of our Lord Jesus Christ,

Arminian

The charge's content is to keep the commandment unstained and free from reproach until Christ appears. Ministry is a long obedience under the eye of the coming Lord, not a short burst of zeal.

Arminian pastoral: grace enables a clean keeping, and the command can still be stained if Timothy grows careless. The appearing of Jesus is both comfort and deadline: hold the trust until He shows Himself.

Reformed

Calvin takes the commandment as the whole office committed to Timothy: show yourself a faithful minister to Christ and the church, spotless and unblameable, until the revelation of the Lord. In an age of hatred and little visible fruit, the mind must be fixed on that day.

Reformed stamina: the only remedy for a difficult, dangerous office is to keep the eyes on Christ's appearing. God who assigned the charge also appoints the appearing; perseverance is loyalty until then.

1 Timothy 6:15

15which he will display at the proper time—he who is the blessed and only Sovereign, the King of kings and Lord of lords,

Arminian

The appearing will be displayed at the proper time, not on our clock. He who times it is the blessed and only Sovereign, King of kings and Lord of lords. Wesley: the power, knowledge, and revelation of that appearing remain in His eternal mind.

Arminian patience: God sincerely governs history and will show Christ when He chooses. The church waits without seizing the timetable; enabling grace teaches hope that does not dictate to the only Sovereign.

Reformed

Calvin: we are hasty and almost prescribe a day to God; Paul restrains that haste. The revelation of Christ has its appointed time. Blessed and only Prince: titles that exalt God's authority so that earthly princes do not dazzle us. Only Sovereign does not abolish magistrates; it means He alone reigns from Himself.

Reformed comfort: all governments depend on Him and fall at His bidding. When the wicked seem to mock God, the church looks past transitory splendor to the King of kings whose authority endures.

1 Timothy 6:16

16who alone has immortality, who dwells in unapproachable light, whom no one has ever seen or can see. To him be honor and eternal dominion. Amen.

Arminian

God alone has underived, independent immortality and dwells in light no creature approaches. No one has seen Him with bodily eyes; yet, Wesley adds, we shall see Him as He is. Honor and eternal dominion close the doxology.

Arminian worship: the gap between God and us is real, and grace still brings us near in the Son. Unapproachable light does not cancel the promise of seeing; it humbles speculation and calls for holy fear until the appearing.

Reformed

Calvin: God alone is immortal from Himself; creatures only borrow life. Take away His communicated power and the soul fades. Unapproachable light means God is concealed from us, yet the cause of obscurity is in our dullness, not in darkness in Him. We look by faith to Him whom we cannot see with bodily eyes or even with the soul's native powers.

Reformed doxology: no happiness, dignity, or life exists out of God. Honor and eternal dominion are His. Curiosity must not outrun peace and sanctification, without which no one will see God.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the charge to the man of God: flee, pursue, fight, and take hold of eternal life—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside 1 Timothy 6:11–16.

Arminian Notable voices

“But thou, O man of God - Whatever all the world else do. A man of God is either a prophet, a messenger of God, or a man devoted to God; a man of another world. Flee - As from a serpent, instead of coveting these things. Follow after righteousness - The whole image of God. Faith - A divine, supernatural sight of God, chiefly in respect of his mercy in Christ. This faith is the foundation of righteousness, the support of godliness, the root of every grace of the Spirit. Fight the good fight of faith - Not about words. Lay hold on eternal life - Just before thee. I charge thee before God, who quickeneth all things - Who hath quickened thee, and will quicken thee at the great day.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on 1 Tim 6:11–16)

Reformed Notable voices

“By calling him man of God he adds weight to the exhortation. In order to withdraw Timothy from excessive solicitude about earthly things, he reminds him that he must fight; for the greater part wish to serve Christ at ease, whereas Christ calls all his servants to warfare. Lay hold on eternal life: God calls thee to eternal life, and therefore, despising the world, strive to obtain it. When he ascribes this to God, that he quickeneth all things, he wishes to meet the offense of the cross, which presents to us nothing but the appearance of death. We should fix our eyes on God alone, because it is he who restoreth the dead to life.”
John Calvin · Commentary on 1 Timothy (on 1 Tim 6:11–16)
