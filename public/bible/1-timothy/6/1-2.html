Passage

1 Timothy 6

Verses 1–2 · ESV · Voices

Paul addresses believers already living under a first-century yoke: honor masters so that the name of God and the teaching are not reviled. Those with believing masters must not despise them as mere equals; they should serve all the better, because those who benefit are believers and beloved. Teach and urge these things.

1 Timothy 6:1

1Let all who are under a yoke as bondservants regard their own masters as worthy of all honor, so that the name of God and the teaching may not be reviled.

Arminian

Paul speaks pastorally to Christians already under a first-century yoke. Honor is aimed at protecting the Name and the teaching from mockery, not at baptizing the institution. The gospel's public reputation is at stake in how believers inhabit a hard setting.

Classical Arminians hear a call the Spirit enables: costly respect so that Christ is not blamed. Grace that saves also trains a holy witness. Neither camp should turn this verse into a defense of slavery or into a modern culture-war speech; it is counsel about the Name among watching neighbors.

Reformed

The aim is explicit: that God's name and the teaching may not be reviled. Paul does not pause to theorize the social order; he shepherds people already in it toward honor that adorns the gospel.

Reformed pastoral reading: God cares how His Word looks in a household. Obedience here is witness under providence, not a brief for the yoke itself. The church's first loyalty is the Name; contempt that makes the gospel look rebellious would slander the very Lord who bought them.

1 Timothy 6:2

2Those who have believing masters must not be disrespectful on the ground that they are brothers; rather they must serve all the better since those who benefit by their good service are believers and beloved. Teach and urge these things.

Arminian

Brotherhood in Christ is real: believing masters are brothers, believers, and beloved. That new family must not be twisted into contempt. Serve all the better, Paul says, because the ones who benefit belong to the same Savior. Teach and urge these things: practical holiness is not a footnote to faith.

Arminian accent: the gospel sincerely creates one household of faith without erasing the setting they inhabit. Greater grace is needed, as Wesley noted, to honor a brother than a stranger. Love for the beloved, not a defense of rank, is the motive.

Reformed

Calvin: the name brother might seem to cancel dominion; Paul argues the opposite from shared grace. They are children of God together, bound by brotherly love, partakers of the same adoption. Service should be better, not worse, when the beneficiary is beloved in the faith.

Reformed comfort: the new family in Christ is the deeper fact. God joins people across stations in one salvation; that bond is reason for faithful work and for refusing disdain. Timothy must teach and urge this, because the church's common life is a gospel argument.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on honor under a yoke, the Name that must not be reviled, and believing masters who are brothers—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside 1 Timothy 6:1–2.

Arminian Notable voices

“Let servants under the yoke - Of heathen masters. Account them worthy of all honour - All the honour due from a servant to a master. Lest the name of God and his doctrine be blasphemed - As it surely will, if they do otherwise. Let them not despise them - Pay them the less honour or obedience. Because they are brethren - And in that respect on a level with them. But rather do them service - Serve them so much the more diligently. Because they are joint partakers of the great benefit - Salvation.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on 1 Tim 6:1–2)

Reformed Notable voices

“They ought to obey unbelieving masters, in order that the name of God and the gospel may not be evil spoken of; as if God, whom we worship, incited us to rebellion, and as if the gospel rendered obstinate those who ought to be subject to others. The name of brother may be thought to constitute equality. Paul argues, on the contrary, that they ought the more willingly to subject themselves to believing masters, because they acknowledge them to be children of God, and are bound to them by brotherly love, and are partakers of the same grace.”
John Calvin · Commentary on 1 Timothy (on 1 Tim 6:1–2)
