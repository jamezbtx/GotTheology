Passage

1 Timothy 6

Verses 3–10 · ESV · Voices

Paul names the teacher who will not consent to the sound words of Jesus and the teaching that accords with godliness: conceit, controversy, and the fantasy that godliness is a means of gain. True gain is godliness with contentment; we brought nothing in and take nothing out. The love of money is a root of evils, and by that craving some have already wandered from the faith.

1 Timothy 6:3

3If anyone teaches a different doctrine and does not agree with the sound words of our Lord Jesus Christ and the teaching that accords with godliness,

Arminian

A different doctrine is measured against the sound (healthful) words of the Lord Jesus and the teaching that accords with godliness. Sound words have no taint that encourages sin; they train real piety. Novelty that will not consent to Christ's words is already a departure.

Arminian pastoral: grace offers a gospel that produces holiness. Teachers remain responsible to stay with Jesus' words. Wesley: if any teach otherwise than strict practical holiness in all its branches, they have left healthful doctrine.

Reformed

Calvin: Paul condemns even those who do not openly oppose sound doctrine yet corrupt it by vain babbling. Words are sound because they give health. Doctrine according to godliness must instruct in the fear of God, edify faith, and train patience, humility, and love.

Reformed weight: whoever does not strive to teach usefully does not teach as he ought. Brilliance without profit is neither godly nor sound. The test is Christ's words and a teaching that actually forms piety.

1 Timothy 6:4

4he is puffed up with conceit and understands nothing. He has an unhealthy craving for controversy and for quarrels about words, which produce envy, dissension, slander, evil suspicions,

Arminian

The mark of the false teacher is swelling pride that understands nothing as it ought. An unhealthy craving for controversy and word-fights yields envy, dissension, slander, and evil suspicions. Dispute becomes a disease when practice is forgotten.

Classical Arminians hear a warning the church must obey: grace enables humility, but conceit can be indulged. Love of argument is not love of truth. Fruit exposes the tree.

Reformed

Calvin: such persons are charged with foolish, empty pride. All they delight in proves their ignorance; they are swollen with subtleties and have nothing solid, mere wind. Questions that only sharpen worthless disputes beget envy and brawling.

Reformed counsel: remain steadfast in the simplicity of the gospel. Ambition is the mother of envy. The church is not required to treat windy ostentation as depth.

1 Timothy 6:5

5and constant friction among people who are depraved in mind and deprived of the truth, imagining that godliness is a means of gain.

Arminian

Constant friction belongs with a mind already depraved and deprived of the truth. The fantasy is that godliness is a tool for profit: religion as a trade. Wesley: thinking the best religion is the getting of money, a far more common case than is usually supposed.

Arminian refusal of a prosperity gospel: grace is not a ladder to wealth. The sincere offer of Christ trains contentment and holiness, not a scheme to monetize piety. Withdraw from that imagination.

Reformed

Calvin: they measure the whole of Christianity by gain, as if the oracles of the Spirit were recorded to serve covetousness. They traffic in godliness as merchandise. Such society is infectious even when it still wears a Christian name.

Reformed severity: sacrilege is using piety for pay. Godliness is not a means of gain; treating it so already signals a mind deprived of the truth. The servants of Christ should make the distance plain.

1 Timothy 6:6

6But godliness with contentment is great gain,

Arminian

Paul turns the slogan inside out. Godliness with contentment is great gain: unspeakable profit in time and eternity. Contentment is the companion of true, vital religion, not a consolation prize for those who failed to get rich.

Arminian pastoral: assisting grace can form a satisfied heart, and the heart must not refuse that gift. Gain is Christlike piety plus enough, not piety plus a bigger barn.

Reformed

Calvin's irony: they make merchandise as if godliness were gain; yet rightly estimated, godliness is great and abundant gain, because it brings full blessedness and the enjoyment of Christ and all His riches.

Reformed comfort: true happiness consists in piety. The Lord is present with His people and, as far as necessity requires, bestows from His fullness. Contentment is not stoicism; it is receiving Christ as wealth.

1 Timothy 6:7

7for we brought nothing into the world, and we cannot take anything out of the world.

Arminian

Birth and death fence greed. We brought nothing in; we take nothing out. Heaping is folly if the exit is empty-handed. Wesley: to what purpose, then, do we heap together so many things?

Arminian wisdom: grace teaches a pilgrim heart. Desire can still outrun need; the verse calls for a responsible, enabled contentment that remembers the grave.

Reformed

Calvin: covetousness is an insatiable gulf if it is not restrained. The best bridle is to desire nothing more than this life demands. We imagine a thousand lives, and so we transgress the bound.

Reformed realism: everyone admits this sentence, then swallows the world with wishes. God sets the limit; the heart must be regulated to what supports life, not to fantasy estates.

1 Timothy 6:8

8But if we have food and clothing, with these we will be content.

Arminian

Food and covering: a modest floor, not a prosperity ceiling. With these we will be content. Wesley read covering as raiment and a house, all a Christian needs and all religion allows him to desire as necessity.

Arminian holiness: grace can wean the appetite. Contentment here is chosen under God's help, not a law against grateful enjoyment of further gifts, but a refusal to make extras into a right.

Reformed

Calvin: food and raiment exclude luxuries and overflowing abundance; nature is content with a little, and what goes beyond natural use is superfluous. Using more is not condemned on its own account; lusting after more is always sinful.

Reformed balance: providence may give more than bread, and thanksgiving is due. The sin is the craving that will not say enough. Contentment is a learned grace, not a tax on every comfort.

1 Timothy 6:9

9But those who desire to be rich fall into temptation, into a snare, into many senseless and harmful desires that plunge people into ruin and destruction.

Arminian

The danger is the desire to be rich, not a bank balance by itself. Desire banishes contentment and opens a sad gradation: temptation, snare, foolish and hurtful desires, then ruin and destruction. Wesley: farewell all hope of content.

Arminian warning: grace can check greed, and greed can still be indulged until it drowns a soul. This is not a sneer at the poor who need wages; it is a pastoral alarm against a will set on wealth.

Reformed

Calvin: the cause of the evils is not riches but an eager desire of them, even in a poor man. Every man who has resolved to become rich gives himself up as a captive to the devil. Foolish, even mad, desires at length plunge them into perdition.

Reformed sharpness, especially for ministers: when gold dazzles, they stick at nothing. The snare is spiritual before it is financial. God names ruin so that His people will flee the desire, not merely manage it.

1 Timothy 6:10

10For the love of money is a root of all kinds of evils. It is through this craving that some have wandered away from the faith and pierced themselves with many pangs.

Arminian

Not money, but love of money, is a root of all kinds of evils. Through this craving some have wandered from the faith and pierced themselves. Wesley: they missed the mark; they aimed not at faith but at something else, and a guilty conscience with tormenting passions followed.

Arminian seriousness: wandering is spoken of as a real departure. Grace that began a profession does not make greed harmless. The pangs are self-inflicted; flee the craving while mercy still calls.

Reformed

Calvin: Paul does not squeeze every vice under avarice, but means that innumerable evils arise from it: frauds, perjury, cruelty, hatred, almost every sort of crime. The most aggravated evil is revolting from the faith; those diseased with this illness degenerate until they renounce it, and frightful torments of conscience follow.

Reformed reading of the wanderers: some who seemed in the way prove, by a final swerve, that the faith they named was not the faith God keeps. The warning is still a real means; the elect are preserved, and the craving remains a snare to be fled.

Key contrast Both treat love of money as spiritually lethal and admit that this craving has already led some to wander from the faith. Arminians typically hear a genuine danger that those who once believed may miss the mark and fall; Reformed readers typically hear that those who finally wander were not effectually kept, while the warning is a means God uses to preserve His own.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on false teachers, godliness with contentment, and the love of money—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside 1 Timothy 6:3–10.

Arminian Notable voices

“If any teach otherwise - Than strict practical holiness in all its branches. And consent not to sound words - Literally, healthful words; words that have no taint of falsehood, or tendency to encourage sin. But godliness with content - The inseparable companion of true, vital religion. Is great gain - Brings unspeakable profit in time, as well as eternity. Love of money - Commonly called prudent care of what a man has. Is the root - The parent of all manner of evils. Which some coveting have erred - Literally, missed the mark. They aimed not at faith, but at something else. And pierced themselves with many sorrows.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on 1 Tim 6:3–10)

Reformed Notable voices

“They do wrong and wickedly, who make merchandise of the doctrine of Christ, as if godliness were gain; though, undoubtedly, if we form a correct estimate of it, godliness is a great and abundant gain. Those men are guilty of sacrilege, who, being bent on acquiring money, make godliness contribute to their gain. But for our part, godliness is a very great gain to us, because by means of it we obtain the benefit, not only of being heirs of the world, but likewise of enjoying Christ and all his riches. The cause of the evils is not riches, but an eager desire of them. He simply meant that innumerable evils arise from avarice.”
John Calvin · Commentary on 1 Timothy (on 1 Tim 6:3–10)
