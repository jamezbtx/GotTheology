Passage

1 Timothy 2

Verses 1–7 · ESV · Voices

Paul turns from charge to Timothy toward the church’s public life: first of all, pray for all people, including kings, so that a quiet, godly life may flourish. The reason is soteriological and Christological: God our Savior desires all people to be saved; there is one mediator, the man Christ Jesus, who gave himself as a ransom for all—the testimony Paul was appointed to preach among the Gentiles.

1 Timothy 2:1

1First of all, then, I urge that supplications, prayers, intercessions, and thanksgivings be made for all people,

Arminian

“First of all” makes intercession the church’s opening work, not an afterthought. Four kinds of prayer—need, desire, advocacy, thanks—are urged for all people, not for a favored circle. Wesley hears thanksgiving “for all men, in the full sense of the word,” because God “willeth all men to be saved” and Christ is “the Mediator of all.”

Classical Arminians take the command at face value: if God sincerely wills the salvation of every person, the church must actually pray for every person. Intercession is partnership with that gracious will. Narrowing “all” into a private list would shrink both the prayer meeting and the heart of God our Savior.

Reformed

Paul speaks of public prayers offered not only for believers but for all mankind. Calvin notes the perverse thought, “We have nothing to do with strangers,” and hears Paul meet it: include all, and do not limit petitions to the body of the church already gathered.

Reformed readers still pray for all, because God commands it and because the gospel is to be preached without fencing off ranks or nations. The wideness of intercession matches promiscuous proclamation. It does not, on this reading, require that every individual be elect; it requires that no class of neighbor be treated as beyond prayer.

Key contrast Both take the command to pray for “all people” as real and wide. Arminians typically hear a sincere universal saving will behind that wideness; Reformed readers typically hear prayer matching a gospel preached to all kinds of people, without concluding that every individual is elect.

1 Timothy 2:2

2for kings and all who are in high positions, that we may lead a peaceful and quiet life, godly and dignified in every way.

Arminian

Kings and high officials are named because they might be the last people a pressured church would bless. Even “the lowest country magistrates,” Wesley says, “frequently do much good or much harm.” Prayer for them aims at a peaceful and quiet life—godly and dignified—not at Christian withdrawal from public order.

Arminian pastoral use: pray for rulers as neighbors God sincerely desires to save, not merely as obstacles to manage. Peace is sought so the gospel can be lived and spoken. Authority is not baptized as holy by default; it is brought before God our Savior who can change hearts.

Reformed

Calvin hears why kings are singled out: the magistrates of that hour were often sworn enemies of Christ, and believers might conclude they should not pray for those who fight His kingdom. Paul forbids that conclusion. God’s ordinance of government is still to be loved, however far particular rulers fall short.

Reformed comfort and duty: pray even for bad rulers, asking God to make them a blessing they are not yet. Peace, godliness, and public decency are fruits of well-ordered rule under providence. The church’s quiet life is not apathy; it is space to worship without the sword of the state as the church’s enemy.

1 Timothy 2:3

3This is good, and it is pleasing in the sight of God our Savior,

Arminian

Praying for all is not optional courtesy; it is “good” and “pleasing” before God our Savior. Wesley ties the clause to the question, “Why are not more converted?”—“We do not pray enough.” The God who has “actually saved us that believe” is the same God who “willeth all men to be saved.”

Classical Arminians press Wesley’s sting: it is strange that any whom He has actually saved should doubt the universality of His grace. “Savior” here names a God whose saving character is sincerely turned toward the world. The church’s prayers should match that character, not a smaller private decree.

Reformed

After showing the usefulness of such prayer, Paul gives a stronger argument: it pleases God. Calvin treats the will of God as the rule of duty. Calling Him “our Savior” reminds the church that salvation is undeserved kindness already tasted—and that the same God may yet extend grace to those now far off.

Reformed reading does not empty “Savior.” It locates saving power in God, not in the church’s intercession as a lever. We pray because He is pleased to be asked, and because the Savior who drew us can draw others of every rank. The pleasure of God, not our speculation about hidden decrees, governs the prayer list.

Key contrast Both agree that prayer for all pleases God our Savior. Arminians typically take “Savior” with a sincere will that all be saved; Reformed readers typically take it as the God who actually saves His people and who may yet extend that same saving grace to other classes as the gospel runs.

1 Timothy 2:4

4who desires all people to be saved and to come to the knowledge of the truth.

Arminian

Here the fork is named in the text: God “desires all people to be saved” and to come to the knowledge of the truth. Wesley: He “willeth seriously all men—Not a part only, much less the smallest part.” Salvation meant is eternal; coming to the truth is not compulsion—“They are not compelled.”

Classical Arminians hear a sincere universal saving will. The gospel is a real offer; knowledge of the truth is the appointed way people are saved, and people may refuse it. Prayer for all (vv. 1–2) would be theater if God did not actually desire the salvation of those named. Prevenient grace makes the coming possible; it does not drag the unwilling.

Reformed

Calvin refuses to set this verse against predestination. “The present discourse relates to classes of men, and not to individual persons”: no people and no rank is excluded, because God wishes the gospel proclaimed to all without exception. Preaching gives life; therefore God invites all equally to partake salvation—princes and foreign nations included.

Reformed readers typically hear “all people” as all kinds: Jew and Gentile, high and low. The gospel is promiscuously preached; the effectual saving of any person remains God’s work among the elect. The verse fuels missions and prayer, not the claim that God’s decree is a frustrated wish that every individual be saved.

Key contrast Both refuse to skip the words “desires all people to be saved.” Arminians typically take them as God’s sincere will that every person be saved, with coming to the truth uncoerced; Reformed readers typically take “all” as all kinds of people, with a gospel promiscuously preached and salvation effectual for the elect.

1 Timothy 2:5

5For there is one God, and there is one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus,

Arminian

One God and one mediator: the fourth verse, Wesley says, is proved by the fifth. Those who do not have this God through this Mediator have no God. The “man Christ Jesus” stands between God and humankind to reconcile and to transact the whole affair of salvation. All other mediators—saints, angels, or self-appointed peacemakers—are excluded.

Classical Arminians hear access held out to all: “all men are to apply to this mediator, who gave himself for all.” The humanity of Jesus is pastoral welcome, not a limit on His deity. There is one door, and the door is sincerely open. Prayer for all people (v. 1) rests on a Mediator given for all people.

Reformed

Calvin hears an implied comparison: one God over the whole world and its nations. Believers are connected with all because there is one God of all; those under His power are not forever shut out of hope. Likewise there is one Mediator through whom we have access—given not to one nation or a small rank, but to all classes, especially those then alienated.

Reformed confession: Christ alone is Mediator of redemption and of intercession. The gospel is preached to every kind of person; the Mediator actually brings His own to the Father. Historic Reformed preaching still says, Do not shut any rank from the hope of salvation—while taking the “all” of this paragraph as classes of humanity, not a claim that He mediates equally for every individual.

Key contrast Both confess one God and one Mediator, the man Christ Jesus, and both shut out rival advocates. Arminians typically present Him as Mediator sincerely given for every person who will come; Reformed readers typically present Him as the one Mediator of the elect, while the invitation to come is preached to all kinds of people.

1 Timothy 2:6

6who gave himself as a ransom for all, which is the testimony given at the proper time.

Arminian

The Mediator “gave himself as a ransom for all.” Wesley lingers on the word: a ransom in which a like is given—life for life—and, from the dignity of the Person redeeming, “more than equivalent to all mankind.” The cross is not a narrowly calculated payment for a secret list; it is a sufficient ransom sincerely intended for all.

Classical Arminians therefore pray for all and preach to all without a double message. “At the proper time” marks God’s wisdom in revealing what was always His gracious heart. The testimony is public: Christ gave Himself for all. Those who perish do not perish because the ransom was never for them, but because they will not come to the knowledge of the truth.

Reformed

Calvin keeps “all” on the same track as verses 1–4: classes of men, not every person taken one by one. Jews and Gentiles, humble and princes, were redeemed by Christ’s death. The ransom language also binds sacrifice to continuing intercession—the two parts of His priesthood—and will not share that glory with other satisfactions.

Reformed readers typically hold a ransom that actually redeems those for whom it was offered—the elect from every kindred—while the testimony is given in due time through promiscuous preaching. Calvin still warns that an insult is offered to Christ by those who, in opinion, shut any person from the hope of salvation. The offer is wide; the atonement, on this reading, is effectual.

Key contrast Both glory in a real ransom given by Christ Himself. Arminians typically take “for all” as a universal atonement sincerely intended for every person; Reformed readers typically take “for all” as all kinds of people, with a ransom effectual for the elect while the testimony is preached without restriction of rank or nation.

1 Timothy 2:7

7For this I was appointed a preacher and an apostle (I am telling the truth, I am not lying), a teacher of the Gentiles in faith and truth.

Arminian

“For this”—the ransom testified in due time—Paul was appointed preacher, apostle, and teacher of the Gentiles. The oath (“I am telling the truth, I am not lying”) underlines how unlikely and how central the Gentile mission is. If Christ gave Himself for all, the nations must hear.

Classical Arminians hear vocation in the service of a sincere universal gospel. Faith and truth are the atmosphere of the teaching, not ethnic privilege. The appointment does not save anyone automatically; it brings the knowledge of the truth to people who must still come. The church inherits the same outward task: tell all kinds of people, because the ransom was for all.

Reformed

Calvin hears Paul laboring to prove his apostleship so that the Gentile calling will not be treated as a rash claim. God appointed him to bring those formerly alienated into a share of the gospel. The Gentile mission is therefore a “sure foundation of the divine calling,” not Paul’s private experiment.

Reformed accent: the nations are in the plan. God had always determined the season for revealing Christ to Jews and Gentiles without distinction of rank. Paul’s office is the appointed means by which God gathers a people from the Gentiles. The preacher addresses all hearers in faith and truth; God effectually saves those He calls from among them.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on prayer for all people, God our Savior who desires all to be saved, and the one Mediator who gave himself as a ransom for all—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside 1 Timothy 2:1–7.

Arminian Notable voices

“Who has actually saved us that believe, and willeth all men to be saved. It is strange that any whom he has actually saved should doubt the universality of his grace! Who willeth seriously all men — Not a part only, much less the smallest part. To be saved — Eternally. And, in order thereto, to come — They are not compelled. Who gave himself a ransom for all — Such a ransom, the word signifies, wherein a like or equal is given; as an eye for an eye, or life for life: and this ransom, from the dignity of the person redeeming, was more than equivalent to all mankind.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on 1 Tim 2:3–6)

Reformed Notable voices

“The Apostle simply means, that there is no people and no rank in the world that is excluded from salvation; because God wishes that the gospel should be proclaimed to all without exception. Now the preaching of the gospel gives life; and hence he justly concludes that God invites all equally to partake salvation. But the present discourse relates to classes of men, and not to individual persons; for his sole object is, to include in this number princes and foreign nations. The universal term all must always be referred to classes of men, and not to persons; as if he had said, that not only Jews, but Gentiles also, not only persons of humble rank, but princes also, were redeemed by the death of Christ.”
John Calvin · Commentary on 1 Timothy (on 1 Tim 2:4–6)
