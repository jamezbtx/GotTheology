Passage

1 Timothy 5

Verses 17–25 · ESV · Voices

Paul moves from enrolled widows to elders: those who rule well, especially in preaching and teaching, are worthy of double honor, for Scripture forbids muzzling the ox and says the laborer deserves his wages. Charges need two or three witnesses; persistent sin is rebuked publicly; Timothy must not prejudge, not lay hands hastily, and remember that both sins and good works will come to light.

1 Timothy 5:17

17Let the elders who rule well be considered worthy of double honor, especially those who labor in preaching and teaching.

Arminian

Elders who rule well are worthy of double honor, especially those who labor in preaching and teaching. Wesley: faithful stewards, a more abundant provision, because they will employ it to God's glory; especially those who labor diligently in the word.

Arminian pastoral: honor is both respect and support. Elders can rule well or poorly; the "well" is a real condition. Grace that calls a man to teach also calls the church not to starve the labor by which they heard the gospel.

Reformed

Calvin: elder here is office, not merely age. Double honor is compared with the honor already due to widows; elders who rule well, especially those who labor in word and doctrine, should receive even more. Honor is due to the work, not to a bare title.

Reformed church order: God gives pastors and ruling elders as gifts. The Spirit who called them to labor also commands the flock to support them. Ingratitude that pinches the teacher is one of Satan's old tricks against the Word.

1 Timothy 5:18

18For the Scripture says, "You shall not muzzle an ox when it treads out the grain," and, "The laborer deserves his wages."

Arminian

Scripture says, "You shall not muzzle an ox when it treads out the grain," and, "The laborer deserves his wages." Moses and the Lord's proverb stand side by side as God's own voice for the preacher's keep.

Arminian reading: the church is commanded, not merely invited, to pay those who labor. This duty can be neglected. Grace does not make oxen or laborers magically full; people must obey the text they hear.

Reformed

Calvin: if God forbids unkindness to the brute, how much more humanity toward men. "The laborer is worthy of his hire" is proverbial equity. They are cruel who suck out a man's sweat and then refuse him bread—and worse when the man is a pastor.

Reformed accent: God has spoken in Scripture; the church's purse is under that Word. Support of ministers is not a tip for charisma. It is obedience to the Lord who uses means to keep instruction in His church.

1 Timothy 5:19

19Do not admit a charge against an elder except on the evidence of two or three witnesses.

Arminian

Do not admit a charge against an elder except on the evidence of two or three witnesses. Elders are easy targets; the Torah's witness rule is pastoral protection, not a clergy exemption from sin.

Arminian church life: accusations can be true or false. The congregation must not swallow a rumor, and the elder must not hide behind the rule. Grace trains both courage to speak with witnesses and patience not to ruin a man by a single tongue.

Reformed

Calvin: godly teachers are more liable to slander than others. Satan labors to draw hearts from ministers so that instruction falls into contempt. The two-or-three rule, though common to all, is applied here because elders especially need it.

Reformed order: God protects the office for the sake of the Word. This is not sacerdotal immunity. Sufficient proof still stands; the Spirit who hates false witness also hates a church that will not hear a well-attested charge.

1 Timothy 5:20

20As for those who persist in sin, rebuke them in the presence of all, so that the rest may stand in fear.

Arminian

Those who persist in sin are to be rebuked in the presence of all, so that the rest may stand in fear. Wesley: elders that sin scandalously and are duly convicted, rebuke before the church.

Arminian discipline: an elder can persist in sin. Public rebuke is a real means to restore or to warn. Perseverance is required; office does not place a man beyond falling. The fear in the rest is healthy: grace can be neglected even in the pulpit.

Reformed

Calvin modifies the protection of v. 19: none may escape the punishment due to sin. Open, glaring transgression by elders should be openly reproved so that others fear. Private faults are another matter; public scandal is not.

Reformed preservation: God keeps His church by discipline as a means. The elect are not left to rot in open sin without the Father's rod. Public rebuke is love for the flock and, where the man is Christ's, a severe mercy that preserves rather than a proof that grace has run out.

Key contrast Shared: persistent, public sin in an elder requires public rebuke so that others fear. Arminians typically stress that an elder can fall and must persevere; the rebuke treats apostasy and scandal as real dangers. Reformed readers typically see discipline as a means God uses to preserve His church and His people, not as evidence that the regenerate are finally left to themselves.

1 Timothy 5:21

21In the presence of God and of Christ Jesus and of the elect angels I charge you to keep these rules without prejudging, doing nothing from partiality.

Arminian

In the presence of God, Christ Jesus, and the elect angels, Timothy is charged to keep these rules without prejudging, doing nothing from partiality. The unseen court is already in session.

Arminian pastoral: impartiality is a grace that can be refused. Friends can be shielded, enemies rushed to judgment. The charge assumes Timothy might fail; heaven's witnesses make the duty weighty and the obedience free and responsible.

Reformed

Calvin: Paul adjures him because the subject is both important and extremely difficult. God, Christ the appointed Judge, and elect angels as witnesses stand before Timothy's eyes so that he executes the office as in their presence. No haste, no acceptance of persons.

Reformed gravity: election appears even in "elect angels"—kept by God, unlike the fallen. The same Lord who elects also demands impartial discipline. The pastor's integrity is lived under a sovereignty that sees every leaning.

1 Timothy 5:22

22Do not be hasty in the laying on of hands, nor take part in the sins of others; keep yourself pure.

Arminian

Do not be hasty in laying on of hands, nor take part in the sins of others; keep yourself pure. Wesley: appoint no man without full trial, else you are accessory to his misbehavior; keep yourself pure from the blood of all men.

Arminian caution: ordination is a responsible act. Haste can share another man's sins. Purity here is moral and official: Timothy can keep himself, and he can also become complicit. Grace assists the slow, testing process.

Reformed

Calvin: laying on of hands is ordination. Do not receive too easily one barely tried. He who consents to an unlawful ordination shares the guilt. Keep yourself pure: if you cannot hinder others from polluting themselves, at least keep your counsel uncontaminated.

Reformed order: God calls, and the church tests. Rash hands do not force the Spirit; they implicate the ordainer. Purity is the Lord's gift and command for the man who would not share other men's sins.

1 Timothy 5:23

23No longer drink only water, but use a little wine for the sake of your stomach and your frequent ailments.

Arminian

No longer drink only water, but use a little wine for the stomach and frequent ailments. Even an austere young minister is told not to harm the body God uses for ministry.

Arminian pastoral: this is liberty for health, not a license to excess. "A little" remains. Grace can be neglected either by intemperance or by a harshness toward the body that leaves the pastor too sick to serve. God cares for Timothy's stomach as well as his doctrine.

Reformed

Calvin: Timothy was austere, even careless of health, not from ambition or superstition. Paul does not forbid water absolutely, but as the ordinary drink, and he adds "a little" to meet mockers. Excessive abstinence that breeds disease is blamed; intemperance is worse.

Reformed liberty: the Spirit does not require a show of severity. God preserves His servants through ordinary means, including medicine. Use the body as an instrument for service, not as a project of proud self-denial or of appetite.

1 Timothy 5:24

24The sins of some people are conspicuous, going before them to judgment, but the sins of others appear later.

Arminian

The sins of some are conspicuous, going before them to judgment; the sins of others appear later. Character is not always obvious on ordination day.

Arminian pastoral: hidden sin will surface. Judgment is real, and some men's guilt arrives in time for the church to see. The verse trains patience and watchfulness: do not rush hands (v. 22), because grace neglected in secret still comes to light.

Reformed

Calvin consoles Timothy: some evils cannot be corrected at once; hypocrites must sometimes be endured until God brings them to public view. Other sins follow after more covertly, yet they will not always be concealed. Wait for the time God has appointed.

Reformed comfort: providence, not pastoral omniscience, unmasks the hypocrite. Hidden sin hurries or limps to judgment under God's hand. The church need not panic or play God; the Judge will not miss what we cannot yet prove.

Key contrast Both expect God to expose sin in due time. Arminians typically hear a pastoral warning that some who seemed sound will later show neglected grace. Reformed readers typically hear consolation that providence will unmask hypocrites and that the church must wait God's timing rather than force a verdict.

1 Timothy 5:25

25So also good works are conspicuous, and even those that are not cannot remain hidden.

Arminian

So also good works are conspicuous, and even those that are not cannot remain hidden. Hidden obedience has a future tense: it will be seen.

Arminian encouragement: the widow's unnoticed feet-washing and the elder's quiet labor will not stay buried. Continue in good works; this is how grace, used, becomes visible. Neglect hides for a while; faithfulness will not stay secret.

Reformed

Calvin: sometimes piety wins early applause; if not, the Lord will not suffer uprightness to be always oppressed. It shall shine like the dawn. We need a calm spirit, and we must not go beyond the limit of our knowledge.

Reformed hope: God will vindicate the good works He Himself prepared. Hidden fruit is still His. Pastors wait for the day of revelation rather than demand the world's immediate thanks.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on double honor, accusations, and laying on of hands—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside 1 Timothy 5:17–25.

Arminian Notable voices

“Let the elders that rule well—who approve themselves faithful stewards of all that is committed to their charge—be counted worthy of double honour, a more abundant provision, seeing that such will employ it all to the glory of God. Especially those who labour diligently and painfully in the word and teaching. Those elders that sin scandalously, and are duly convicted, rebuke before all—the church. Lay hands suddenly on no man: appoint no man to church offices without full trial and examination; else thou wilt be accessary to his misbehaviour in his office.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on 1 Tim 5:17–25)

Reformed Notable voices

“For preserving the good order of the Church, it is highly necessary that elders should not be neglected. Elders who rule well are more worthy of being honored than widows, and ought therefore to receive double honor. Against an elder receive not an accusation but what is supported by sufficient proof. Those that sin rebuke before all, that others, warned by such an example, may fear the more. Lay not hands suddenly on any man: he forbids him to receive too easily any one that has not been fully tried.”
John Calvin · Commentary on 1 Timothy (on 1 Tim 5:17–25)
