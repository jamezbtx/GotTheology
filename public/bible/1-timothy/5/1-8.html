Passage

1 Timothy 5

Verses 1–8 · WEB · Voices

Paul trains Timothy to treat the congregation as a household: older men as fathers, younger as brothers, women as mothers and sisters in all purity. He then honors true widows, insists that families provide first, and warns that neglect of one's own house is a denial of the faith.

1 Timothy 5:1

1Don’t rebuke an older man, but exhort him as a father; the younger men as brothers;

Arminian

Correction in God's household is not legendary, but the tone is filial. Timothy's youth is no license to scold an older man as if office erased reverence. Encourage him as a father: truth without contempt.

Grace that saves also trains the manner of ministry. A young pastor can neglect that grace and become harsh; the same grace, used, produces courage wrapped in honor. The church is not first a courtroom; it is a family under Christ.

Reformed

Calvin: correction is a medicine with bitterness; Timothy's youth made untempered severity harder to bear. The milder word is exhort. Age is due some reverence, yet old men are not to sin with impunity.

God shepherds His house through means. Gentleness is not indifference. The Spirit-wrought pastor honors fathers in the faith while still applying the medicine the Great Physician prescribed.

1 Timothy 5:2

2the elder women as mothers; the younger as sisters, in all purity.

Arminian

The household metaphor continues: affection without a familiarity that stains. Purity here protects the flock and the watching world.

Holiness in mixed company is a grace that must be practiced, not presumed. Timothy can keep himself pure, and he can also fail; the charge treats purity as living obedience, not a finished badge.

Reformed

Calvin hears "with all chastity" as a fence against suspicion, not a hint that Timothy was immoral. A chaste gravity through all conversation lets a young minister speak freely without giving the wicked a handle for laughter.

God orders the church's speech and nearness. Purity is fruit of a heart the Spirit keeps. The pastor's life is public; the Lord uses visible chastity to guard both the man and the flock's good name.

1 Timothy 5:3

3Honor widows who are widows indeed.

Arminian

Honor widows who are widows. Honor is not a nod of pity; Wesley: maintain them from the public stock. The church's purse is a means of grace toward those who have no family left to lean on.

For the hearer: this is neither harsh screening nor sentimental charity. True widowhood is a need God sees. Congregations can neglect this honor; enabling grace calls them to open the hand and not grow cold.

Reformed

Calvin: honor here is the ancient church's special care, support from common funds. "Widows" alludes to the Greek for the deprived. The church is to distinguish those who are truly destitute.

Reformed charity is Spirit-wrought and ordered. God loves the widow; He also loves a church that does not scatter the treasury without discernment. Provision is covenant kindness, not a vague mood of pity.

1 Timothy 5:4

4But if any widow has children or grandchildren, let them learn first to show piety toward their own family and to repay their parents, for this is acceptable in the sight of God.

Arminian

If any widow has children or grandchildren, they first learn godliness at home by making some return to their parents. Family duty is not a lesser piety; it is pleasing in God's sight.

Arminians hear this as grace perfecting natural duty, not canceling it. Children can refuse this call. The gospel trains them to repay care; neglected grace leaves parents hungry while the church is asked to carry what the household should have borne.

Reformed

Calvin: before the church is burdened, let children and grandchildren do their duty. Nature itself teaches a return of filial love; Paul adds that God has sanctioned it. Piety toward parents is the second degree of piety, leading toward the worship of God.

The Spirit does not erase the household; He writes God's law on the heart so that provision becomes worship. Church order protects true widows by first requiring families to act.

1 Timothy 5:5

5Now she who is a widow indeed and desolate, has her hope set on God and continues in petitions and prayers night and day.

Arminian

The true widow indeed and desolate, has her hope set on God and continues in prayers night and day. Hope is personal and practiced: she actually looks to God because human props are gone.

This is honor, not a test designed to shame. Continual prayer is a grace that can be cherished or neglected. The church comes alongside those whose hope is in God, while still calling every widow to that living trust rather than to idleness.

Reformed

Calvin: widows are solitary and have no children. He does not mean every childless woman hopes in God, but that such widows, finding no worldly refuge, are free to look to God alone and to continue in prayer. The church then stretches out her hand.

Hope in God is the Spirit's work in the desolate. Night-and-day prayer is not a technique to earn enrollment; it marks those the Lord has taught to rest on Him when every other stay is gone.

1 Timothy 5:6

6But she who gives herself to pleasure is dead while she lives.

Arminian

She who gives herself to pleasure is dead while she lives. Wesley: living in pleasure—delicately, even in elegant regular sensuality, not only in open vice. A body can move while the soul is asleep toward God.

Grace received can be neglected until a professing widow is spiritually dead in the midst of her days. This is pastoral, not scornful: luxury can choke the life God gave. The call is to wake, repent, and use the grace still offered.

Reformed

Calvin: the luxurious woman who uses widowhood for idle ease is dead while she lives—useless, yielding no good. He refuses to reduce the phrase to a mere label of unbelief, yet he will not call indolent ease "life."

True spiritual life is Spirit-wrought fruit. Where self-indulgence reigns, we do not treat it as a living faith on pause. God makes alive; a church ordered by the Word will not baptize a living death as if it were piety.

Key contrast Both refuse to sentimentalize a self-indulgent widow. Arminian readers find a spiritual death that can overtake a professing believer who neglects grace. Reformed readers hear that Spirit-given life produces useful charity; persistent luxury is not living faith waiting on willpower, but evidence that true life is missing or that God must yet make her alive.

1 Timothy 5:7

7Also command these things, that they may be without reproach.

Arminian

Command these things so that they may be without reproach. Timothy must teach widows and households, not merely administer a list. Reproach is an actual danger to the gospel's name.

Pastorally: command is a means of grace. People can hear and still refuse; the pastor still speaks so that those who yield may live blamelessly. Instruction aims at a holy life, not at clever speculation.

Reformed

Calvin: Timothy must teach the women carefully, not only block the ambitious. The pastor guards by instruction and warning so that the unworthy are not put forward and the church remains blameless.

The Word creates the blameless community God requires. Command is not scolding for its own sake; it is how the Lord keeps His house from scandal and trains charity that matches the gospel.

1 Timothy 5:8

8But if anyone doesn’t provide for his own, and especially his own household, he has denied the faith and is worse than an unbeliever.

Arminian

Anyone who will not provide for relatives, especially the household, has denied the faith and is worse than an unbeliever. Wesley: he has virtually denied the faith, which does not destroy but perfects natural duties.

A baptized person can deny the faith by what he fails to do. This is not a slogan about wealth; it is household piety. Grace neglected here is grace refused; the warning is meant to be believed and obeyed.

Reformed

Calvin: no piety toward God remains when humanity is laid aside. Faith that makes us sons of God cannot render us worse than beasts. Such a one has denied the faith and is worse than an infidel, because nature itself teaches this duty and gospel light makes the refusal more guilty.

Spirit-wrought faith produces household care. "Denied the faith" names open contempt of God, not a small lapse. Those God has truly made His own are not left in such brutality; the verse unmasks a profession that never became love of one's own.

Key contrast On this both agree: failing to provide for one's household is a denial of Christian faith, worse than ordinary unbelief. Arminians typically treat the warning as a possibility for a professing believer who neglects grace. Reformed readers typically treat such inhumanity as evidence that living faith is absent, since the Spirit does not leave the regenerate worse than an infidel in this duty.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Arminian and Reformed writers of the past on honoring true widows and the household's first duty, in their own words.

Arminian Notable voices

“Rebuke not—considering your own youth, with such a severity as would otherwise be proper. Honour—that is, maintain out of the public stock. Widows indeed—who have no near relations to provide for them, and who are wholly devoted to God. Desolate—having neither children nor grandchildren to relieve her. If any provide not food and raiment for his own, he hath virtually denied the faith, which does not destroy, but perfect, natural duties.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on 1 Tim 5:1–8)

Reformed Notable voices

“He now recommends to Timothy gentleness and moderation in correcting faults. Correction is a medicine, which has always some bitterness. Honor widows that are really widows: by the word honor he does not mean any expression of respect, but that special care of them which bishops took in the ancient Church. They who do not care about any of their relatives, and especially about their own house, have denied the faith. And justly; for there is no piety towards God when a person can thus lay aside the feelings of humanity.”
John Calvin · Commentary on 1 Timothy (on 1 Tim 5:1–8)
