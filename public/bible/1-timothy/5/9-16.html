Passage

1 Timothy 5

Verses 9–16 · ESV · Voices

Paul sets an enrollment for older widows of proven life—one-husband faithfulness, hospitality, care for the afflicted—while refusing younger widows whose passions may pull them from a pledged service. He would have them marry and manage homes, and he keeps the church's burden for those who are truly widows.

1 Timothy 5:9

9Let a widow be enrolled if she is not less than sixty years of age, having been the wife of one husband,

Arminian

Enrollment is not for every bereaved woman. Sixty years, the wife of one husband. Wesley reads the phrase as lawful marriage, whether with one or more successively, and hears an order of deaconesses who served the sick and traveling preachers.

Arminian pastoral: age and proven fidelity are gifts of a long walk, not a slight against younger grief. The church may honor a dedicated widow without turning enrollment into sentiment or into a harsh caste. Grace trained her over decades; the list asks for that history.

Reformed

Calvin: supported at public expense, they should have reached old age, and they consecrated themselves to ministry. Sixty guards against a later desire to marry. "Wife of one man" is a pledge of chastity; he does not disgrace second marriage, and he will soon advise younger widows to marry.

Reformed order: the Spirit loves widows and also loves a church that will not bind the young to a vow they cannot bear. Enrollment is covenant care with conditions, not a prize for pity.

1 Timothy 5:10

10and having a reputation for good works: if she has brought up children, has shown hospitality, has washed the feet of the saints, has cared for the afflicted, and has devoted herself to every good work.

Arminian

Reputation for good works: bringing up children, hospitality, washing the saints' feet, caring for the afflicted, devoted to every good work. The church enrolls a life already spent in lowly love.

Arminian emphasis: these works are the fruit of assisting grace, practiced over years. A widow can grow in them or neglect them. Enrollment looks backward at a walk, not at a sudden burst of charity meant to qualify.

Reformed

Calvin: the assembly of widows was honorable; they were not appointed to lazy inactivity but to minister to the poor and sick. Washing feet stands for all humble services; then liberality; then every good work. Barrenness is not condemned; dainty refusal to raise children is.

Reformed accent: good works are Spirit-wrought and public enough to be attested. God prepared these works; the church recognizes them. Charity here is ordered service, not a sentimental resume.

1 Timothy 5:11

11But refuse to enroll younger widows, for when their passions draw them away from Christ, they desire to marry

Arminian

Refuse to enroll younger widows: when their passions draw them away from Christ, they desire to marry. Wesley: waxed wanton against Christ, to whose more immediate service they had addicted themselves, and so withdraw from that entire service.

Arminian pastoral: this is not scorn of youth or of marriage. Marriage is honorable; the danger is a pledged, undivided service abandoned when desire rises. Grace can be drawn away from; the refusal protects both the woman and the church's name.

Reformed

Calvin: he does not excommunicate them; he only withholds the honor of the order. They become wanton against Christ who forget the condition to which they were called. Paul had seen many such cases; the remedy is not to admit those still of marriageable age.

Reformed caution: the Spirit who orders the church also refuses rash vows. Compulsory celibacy for the young is, in Calvin's reading, rash and cruel. God is not honored by an enrollment that sets a snare.

1 Timothy 5:12

12and so incur condemnation for having abandoned their former faith.

Arminian

They incur condemnation for having abandoned their former faith. Wesley: they have rejected their first faith, deserted their trust in God, and acted contrary to the first conviction that entire devotion was the most excellent way.

Arminian soteriology hears a real falling: first faith can be abandoned. The condemnation is not a small social shame. When we first receive power to believe, the Spirit points to excellent things; sinking below that call is unwise and, here, damnable if they revolt.

Reformed

Calvin takes "having condemnation" as severity, even the terror of eternal death. They revolt from the faith of baptism and from Christianity, not merely from a church pledge of singleness. He rejects using the verse to prop a papal vow of perpetual celibacy for the young.

Reformed reading: a broken profession can damn. Yet those who finally abandon Christ show they were not kept in living union with Him. The warning is a real means God uses; preservation of the saints is not a license to treat revolt as a harmless mood.

Key contrast Shared: abandoning the pledged "first faith" brings condemnation, not a shrug. Arminians typically hear a believer's first trust actually deserted. Reformed readers typically hear revolt from baptismal profession, and they hold that those God preserves in Christ will not finally walk away, even while the warning is meant to terrify hypocrisy and rash vows.

1 Timothy 5:13

13Besides that, they learn to be idlers, going about from house to house, and not only idlers, but also gossips and busybodies, saying what they should not.

Arminian

Besides that, they learn to be idlers, going house to house, then gossips and busybodies, saying what they should not. Idle time is not neutral; it trains the tongue.

Arminian pastoral: this is caution, not caricature of all young widows. Grace that could fill a day with prayer and work can be neglected into wandering speech. The remedy is honest occupation, not public shaming of grief.

Reformed

Calvin: nothing is more becoming in women than keeping the house. From sloth springs curiosity, the mother of talkativeness. The church's kindness, which opened doors, was abused for idleness.

Reformed order: the Spirit produces a quiet, useful life. Church enrollment that creates a class of wandering talkers harms the gospel. God cares how the household of faith sounds in other people's houses.

1 Timothy 5:14

14So I would have younger widows marry, bear children, manage their households, and give the adversary no occasion for slander.

Arminian

Paul would have younger widows marry, bear children, manage households, and give the adversary no occasion for slander. Wesley: younger women, widows or virgins not disposed to live single, will then have sufficient employment of their own.

Arminian pastoral: marriage and motherhood are holy callings, not consolation prizes. Grace assists a chaste married life. Choosing this path is obedience for many; treating it as spiritual second-class is the error Paul blocks.

Reformed

Calvin: he does not invite them to nuptial delights or to lust, but, given weakness and the slipperiness of the age, to chaste marriage and its burdens. Their married life will be not less acceptable to God than remaining in widowhood.

Reformed comfort: God values obedience in one's calling above superstitious celibacy. The adversary hunts a pretext; a well-ordered home shuts a mouth that wanted to slander the gospel.

1 Timothy 5:15

15For some have already strayed after Satan.

Arminian

For some have already strayed after Satan. Wesley: some widows have turned aside after Satan, who has drawn them from Christ. The "already" makes the danger concrete, not theoretical.

Arminian warning: souls who were with Christ can be drawn away. Satan is a real shepherd of the straying. The church refuses enrollment of the young not because it hates them, but because some have already left the path, and grace neglected ends in following the enemy.

Reformed

Calvin: the expression is worth notice: no one turns aside from Christ, even in the smallest degree, without following Satan, for he has dominion over all who do not belong to Christ. Turning aside makes slaves of Satan out of those who were called children of God in profession.

Reformed confession: final stray after Satan reveals that they are not Christ's. The warning is sharp because the issue is dominion, not a mild lapse. God preserves His elect; this verse describes those who, leaving Christ, show they were under another lord.

Key contrast Both take "strayed after Satan" with full seriousness. Arminians typically hear people drawn from Christ who had been His in a real, resistible walk. Reformed readers typically hear that whoever turns aside from Christ is under Satan's dominion and does not belong to Christ—preservation of the saints standing behind the grim diagnosis.

1 Timothy 5:16

16If any believing woman has relatives who are widows, let her care for them. Let the church not be burdened, so that it may care for those who are truly widows.

Arminian

If any believing woman has relatives who are widows, let her care for them. The church must not be burdened, so it may care for those who are truly widows. Private charity protects public mercy.

Arminian pastoral: believing women can neglect this grace or practice it. The point is not to shame the poor but to keep the church free for the desolate. Households that provide are living the faith they confess.

Reformed

Calvin: people willingly throw their own burdens on the whole church; Paul guards against that. Relieving one's own widows is duty; those who spare themselves and load the church act sinfully. Still worse is fraud against what was dedicated to the church.

Reformed order: Spirit-wrought generosity starts at home so that the common fund can reach true widows. God loves a church that is neither stingy toward the desolate nor naive toward those who can and should provide.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the enrolled widow, younger widows, and the church's burden—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside 1 Timothy 5:9–16.

Arminian Notable voices

“Let not a widow be chosen into the number of deaconesses, who attended sick women or travelling preachers, under threescore. Having been the wife of one husband—that is, having lived in lawful marriage, whether with one or more persons successively. They have rejected their first faith—have deserted their trust in God, and have acted contrary to the first conviction that wholly to devote themselves to his service was the most excellent way. Some widows have turned aside after Satan, who has drawn them from Christ.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on 1 Tim 5:9–16)

Reformed Notable voices

“Let a widow be chosen. He again points out what kind of widows should be taken under the care of the Church. Not under sixty years of age; for they consecrated themselves to the ministry of the Church, which would have been altogether intolerable if there were still a likelihood of their being married. After Satan: no one can turn aside from Christ, in the smallest degree, without following Satan; for he has dominion over all who do not belong to Christ.”
John Calvin · Commentary on 1 Timothy (on 1 Tim 5:9–16)
