Passage

1 Timothy 5

Verses 9–16 · WEB · Voices

Paul sets an enrollment for older widows of proven life—one-husband faithfulness, hospitality, care for the afflicted—while refusing younger widows whose passions may pull them from a pledged service. He would have them marry and manage homes, and he keeps the church's burden for those who are truly widows.

1 Timothy 5:9

9Let no one be enrolled as a widow under sixty years old, having been the wife of one man,

Arminian

Enrollment is not for every bereaved woman. Wesley reads the phrase as lawful marriage, whether with one or more successively, and hears an order of deaconesses who served the sick and traveling preachers.

Age and proven fidelity are gifts of a long walk, not a slight against younger grief. The church may honor a dedicated widow without turning enrollment into sentiment or into a harsh caste. Grace trained her over decades; the list asks for that history.

Reformed

Calvin: supported at public expense, they should have reached old age, and they consecrated themselves to ministry. Sixty guards against a later desire to marry. "Wife of one man" is a pledge of chastity; he does not disgrace second marriage, and he will soon advise younger widows to marry.

The Spirit loves widows and also loves a church that will not bind the young to a vow they cannot bear. Enrollment is covenant care with conditions, not a prize for pity.

1 Timothy 5:10

10being approved by good works, if she has brought up children, if she has been hospitable to strangers, if she has washed the saints’ feet, if she has relieved the afflicted, and if she has diligently followed every good work.

Arminian

Reputation for good works: bringing up children, hospitality, washing the saints' feet, caring for the afflicted, devoted to every good work. The church enrolls a life already spent in lowly love.

These works are the fruit of assisting grace, practiced over years. A widow can grow in them or neglect them. Enrollment looks backward at a walk, not at a sudden burst of charity meant to qualify.

Reformed

Calvin: the assembly of widows was honorable; they were not appointed to lazy inactivity but to minister to the poor and sick. Washing feet stands for all humble services; then liberality; then every good work. Barrenness is not condemned; dainty refusal to raise children is.

Good works are Spirit-wrought and public enough to be attested. God prepared these works; the church recognizes them. Charity here is ordered service, not a sentimental resume.

1 Timothy 5:11

11But refuse younger widows, for when they have grown wanton against Christ, they desire to marry,

Arminian

Refuse younger widows: when they have grown wanton against Christ, they desire to marry. Wesley: waxed wanton against Christ, to whose more immediate service they had addicted themselves, and so withdraw from that entire service.

In practice: this is not scorn of youth or of marriage. Marriage is honorable; the danger is a pledged, undivided service abandoned when desire rises. Grace can be drawn away from; the refusal protects both the woman and the church's name.

Reformed

Calvin: he does not excommunicate them; he only withholds the honor of the order. They become wanton against Christ who forget the condition to which they were called. Paul had seen many such cases; the remedy is not to admit those still of marriageable age.

The Spirit who orders the church also refuses rash vows. Compulsory celibacy for the young is, in Calvin's reading, rash and cruel. God is not honored by an enrollment that sets a snare.

1 Timothy 5:12

12having condemnation, because they have rejected their first pledge.

Arminian

They incur condemnation, because they have rejected their first pledge. Wesley: they have rejected their first faith, deserted their trust in God, and acted contrary to the first conviction that entire devotion was the most excellent way.

Arminian soteriology hears a falling: first faith can be abandoned. The condemnation is not a small social shame. When we first receive power to believe, the Spirit points to excellent things; sinking below that call is unwise and, here, damnable if they revolt.

Reformed

Calvin takes "having condemnation" as severity, even the terror of eternal death. They revolt from the faith of baptism and from Christianity, not merely from a church pledge of singleness. He rejects using the verse to prop a papal vow of perpetual celibacy for the young.

A broken profession can damn. Yet those who finally abandon Christ show they were not kept in living union with Him. The warning is a means God uses; preservation of the saints is not a license to treat revolt as a harmless mood.

Key contrast Abandoning the pledged "first faith" brings condemnation, not a shrug. Arminians find a believer's first trust actually deserted. Reformed interpreters hear revolt from baptismal profession, and they hold that those God preserves in Christ will not finally walk away, even while the warning is meant to terrify hypocrisy and rash vows.

1 Timothy 5:13

13Besides, they also learn to be idle, going about from house to house. Not only idle, but also gossips and busybodies, saying things which they ought not.

Arminian

Besides, they also learn to be idle, going house to house, then gossips and busybodies, saying things which they ought not. Idle time is not neutral; it trains the tongue.

This is caution, not caricature of all young widows. Grace that could fill a day with prayer and work can be neglected into wandering speech. The remedy is honest occupation, not public shaming of grief.

Reformed

Calvin: nothing is more becoming in women than keeping the house. From sloth springs curiosity, the mother of talkativeness. The church's kindness, which opened doors, was abused for idleness.

The Spirit produces a quiet, useful life. Church enrollment that creates a class of wandering talkers harms the gospel. God cares how the household of faith sounds in other people's houses.

1 Timothy 5:14

14I desire therefore that the younger widows marry, bear children, rule the household, and give no occasion to the adversary for insulting.

Arminian

Paul would have younger widows marry, bear children, rule household, and give no occasion to the adversary for insulting. Wesley: younger women, widows or virgins not disposed to live single, will then have sufficient employment of their own.

Marriage and motherhood are holy callings, not consolation prizes. Grace assists a chaste married life. Choosing this path is obedience for many; treating it as spiritual second-class is the error Paul blocks.

Reformed

Calvin: he does not invite them to nuptial delights or to lust, but, given weakness and the slipperiness of the age, to chaste marriage and its burdens. Their married life will be not less acceptable to God than remaining in widowhood.

God values obedience in one's calling above superstitious celibacy. The adversary hunts a pretext; a well-ordered home shuts a mouth that wanted to slander the gospel.

1 Timothy 5:15

15For already some have turned away after Satan.

Arminian

For already some have turned away after Satan. Wesley: some widows have turned aside after Satan, who has drawn them from Christ. The "already" makes the danger concrete, not theoretical.

Souls who were with Christ can be drawn away. Satan is a shepherd of the straying. The church refuses enrollment of the young not because it hates them, but because some have already left the path, and grace neglected ends in following the enemy.

Reformed

Calvin: the expression is worth notice: no one turns aside from Christ, even in the smallest degree, without following Satan, for he has dominion over all who do not belong to Christ. Turning aside makes slaves of Satan out of those who were called children of God in profession.

Final stray after Satan reveals that they are not Christ's. The warning is sharp because the issue is dominion, not a mild lapse. God preserves His elect; this verse describes those who, leaving Christ, show they were under another lord.

Key contrast Both take "away after Satan" with full seriousness. Arminians hear people drawn from Christ who had been His in a resistible walk. Reformed commentators typically hear that whoever turns aside from Christ is under Satan's dominion and does not belong to Christ—preservation of the saints standing behind the grim diagnosis.

1 Timothy 5:16

16If any man or woman who believes has widows, let them relieve them, and don’t let the assembly be burdened, that it might relieve those who are widows indeed.

Arminian

If any believing man or woman has widows in the family, they should relieve them, so the assembly is not burdened and can relieve those who are widows indeed. The church must not be burdened, so that it can help those who are widows indeed. Private charity protects public mercy.

Believing women can neglect this grace or practice it. The point is not to shame the poor but to keep the church free for the desolate. Households that provide are living the faith they confess.

Reformed

Calvin: people willingly throw their own burdens on the whole church; Paul guards against that. Relieving one's own widows is duty; those who spare themselves and load the church act sinfully. Still worse is fraud against what was dedicated to the church.

Spirit-wrought generosity starts at home so that the common fund can reach true widows. God loves a church that is neither stingy toward the desolate nor naive toward those who can and should provide.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Classic comment on the enrolled widow, younger widows, and the church's burden, from both sides of the debate.

Arminian Notable voices

“Let not a widow be chosen into the number of deaconesses, who attended sick women or travelling preachers, under threescore. Having been the wife of one husband—that is, having lived in lawful marriage, whether with one or more persons successively. They have rejected their first faith—have deserted their trust in God, and have acted contrary to the first conviction that wholly to devote themselves to his service was the most excellent way. Some widows have turned aside after Satan, who has drawn them from Christ.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on 1 Tim 5:9–16)

Reformed Notable voices

“Let a widow be chosen. He again points out what kind of widows should be taken under the care of the Church. Not under sixty years of age; for they consecrated themselves to the ministry of the Church, which would have been altogether intolerable if there were still a likelihood of their being married. After Satan: no one can turn aside from Christ, in the smallest degree, without following Satan; for he has dominion over all who do not belong to Christ.”
John Calvin · Commentary on 1 Timothy (on 1 Tim 5:9–16)
