Romans 9: Election, Mercy, and the Potter

How Arminian and Reformed readers interpret Jacob and Esau, Pharaoh, and the potter and the clay in Paul's argument about Israel.

About 7 min read

Romans 9 is the chapter most often cited when Christians argue about election. Readers in the Reformed tradition have long treated it as the clearest biblical statement of God’s unconditional choice of particular people for salvation. Readers in the Arminian tradition have argued that the chapter is about God’s freedom to set the terms of salvation and to shape the history of Israel and the nations, not about a decree that settles each person’s destiny apart from faith. Both sides have serious exegetes, and both take the text as the word of God. This article sets out how each reads the chapter and where the real disagreements lie.

The question Paul is answering

Romans 9 does not begin with a doctrine of predestination. It begins with grief. Paul says he has great sorrow and unceasing pain because most of his Jewish kinsmen have not believed in their Messiah. That raises a question he has to answer if his gospel is to stand: if Israel was God’s chosen people and most of Israel has rejected Christ, has God’s word failed? Paul’s answer, stated in verse 6, is that it has not, because not all who are descended from Israel belong to Israel.

Both traditions agree on this starting point. They disagree about what Paul does with it. Reformed readers see Paul answering the problem of Israel’s unbelief by appealing to God’s sovereign election of individuals within Israel. Arminian readers see Paul defending God’s right to define his people by faith rather than by ancestry, which is the theme he returns to at the end of the chapter.

Jacob and Esau

The first test case is the choice of Jacob over Esau. Paul notes that the choice was announced before the twins were born, “neither having done anything good or bad, that the purpose of God according to election might stand, not of works, but of him who calls” (9:11). He then quotes Malachi: “Jacob I loved, but Esau I hated” (9:13).

Reformed interpreters press the timing. The choice was made before either child had done anything, so it cannot rest on their conduct. John Calvin argued that if election were based on foreseen faith or works, Paul’s careful point about the twins being unborn would lose its force. The phrase “not of works, but of him who calls” places the cause of the distinction in God. On this reading, Jacob and Esau illustrate the way God deals with every person: some are chosen for mercy, and the choice rests on his purpose alone.

Arminian interpreters notice that the texts Paul quotes are about nations. Genesis 25:23 says “Two nations are in your womb” and that the elder will serve the younger, which never happened to Esau personally. Malachi 1, written centuries later, speaks of Edom and Israel. Many Arminian readers therefore argue that Paul is talking about God’s choice of the line through which the promise would come, and about his freedom to choose the younger over the elder, not about the eternal salvation of two individuals. Even where Arminians grant that individuals are in view, they note that “not of works” excludes merit, and that faith is never treated in Paul as a work. Election can be free of works and still be in Christ through faith.

Reformed readers reply that Paul’s argument in the rest of the chapter, especially the objections he anticipates, makes most sense if the election in view is personal and unconditional. If Paul were only speaking about national roles, no one would accuse God of injustice.

“Is there unrighteousness with God?”

Paul’s next move is to answer an objection: is God unjust? His answer comes from Exodus 33:19: “I will have mercy on whom I have mercy, and I will have compassion on whom I have compassion” (9:15). He draws the conclusion: “So then it is not of him who wills, nor of him who runs, but of God who has mercy” (9:16).

For Reformed readers this is the heart of the chapter. The objection only arises because Paul has taught that God distinguishes between people for reasons found in himself. Paul does not answer by saying that God foresaw who would believe. He answers by appealing to God’s freedom in mercy. The human will and human effort do not originate salvation. Faith is real and necessary, but it is the fruit of mercy, not the condition that secures it.

Arminian readers agree that salvation is not of the one who wills or runs, if that means salvation cannot be earned or self-generated. John Wesley read the verse as saying that God has mercy according to the terms he himself has fixed, and those terms are faith in Christ. On this reading Paul is defending God’s right to show mercy to believing Gentiles and to withhold it from those who seek righteousness by works, which is exactly how Paul summarizes the matter in 9:30–32. Israel did not attain righteousness “Because they didn’t seek it by faith, but as it were by works of the law” (9:32). The objection of unfairness, on this view, came from Jews who could not accept that God would welcome Gentiles on the same footing.

Pharaoh and hardening

Paul then cites Pharaoh, whom God raised up to display his power, and concludes, “he has mercy on whom he desires, and he hardens whom he desires” (9:18).

Reformed interpreters take this as a parallel to the mercy shown to the elect. God’s hardening is a judicial act, not the creation of evil in an innocent heart, but it is God’s act. Most Reformed theologians distinguish between election, in which God actively brings people to faith, and reprobation, in which he passes over others and leaves them in their sin, which he then justly punishes. Pharaoh is the example of the second.

Arminian interpreters point to the Exodus narrative, where Pharaoh hardens his own heart several times before God is said to harden it. They read divine hardening as God confirming a person in a course already chosen, as Paul himself describes in Romans 1, where God “gave them up” to what they desired. Hardening, on this view, is God’s response to persistent rebellion. Arminians also note that in Romans 11 the hardening of Israel is partial and temporary, and that those hardened can be grafted back in “if they don’t continue in their unbelief” (11:23).

The potter and the clay

The most debated image in the chapter is the potter. Paul asks, “hasn’t the potter a right over the clay, from the same lump to make one part a vessel for honor, and another for dishonor?” (9:21). He continues with vessels of wrath “prepared for destruction” and vessels of mercy “which he prepared beforehand for glory” (9:22–23).

Reformed readers see in the image God’s sovereign right over his creatures. The same lump stands for fallen humanity, all equally deserving of judgment. God shows mercy to some and leaves others to justice, and no one can accuse him of wrongdoing, because mercy is never owed. Many Reformed interpreters also note the asymmetry in the verbs: God himself “prepared beforehand” the vessels of mercy, while the vessels of wrath are described as “prepared for destruction” without naming the one who prepared them. That asymmetry is often taken to support the distinction between active election and passive reprobation.

Arminian readers point out that the potter image comes from Jeremiah 18, where God says he will change what he intends to do with a nation if it repents. In that passage the potter’s freedom is precisely the freedom to respond to the clay’s response. Arminians also observe that God “endured with much patience” the vessels of wrath (9:22), language that fits people being given time to repent, as Romans 2:4 says of God’s kindness. Wesley glossed the vessels of wrath as fitted for destruction by their own willful and final impenitence. For Arminians, the vessels of mercy are believers, and the vessels of wrath are those who refuse to believe.

Where the chapter ends

Both traditions insist that the chapter must be read with chapters 10 and 11. Romans 10 says, “Whoever will call on the name of the Lord will be saved” (10:13), and laments that Israel did not obey the gospel. Romans 11 ends with the statement that “God has bound all to disobedience, that he might have mercy on all” (11:32), followed by a doxology to the depth of God’s wisdom.

Arminian readers see this as confirmation that Romans 9 is about God’s plan for Israel and the nations, a plan that moves toward mercy offered to all and received by faith. Reformed readers see chapters 10 and 11 as describing the means by which God’s election is carried out: the gospel is preached to all, everyone who calls will be saved, and those who call do so because God has had mercy on them. Romans 9 explains why some believe, and Romans 10 explains how.

Common ground

The disagreement is serious, but the two traditions share more than the debate sometimes suggests. Both affirm that salvation is by grace and not by human merit. Both affirm that faith is necessary, and that the gospel must be preached to everyone. Both deny that God is the author of sin. Both read Paul’s anguish over Israel as a model for Christian compassion toward those who do not believe. And both end where Paul ends, with worship before a God whose judgments are unsearchable.

What the debate turns on

In the end, the readings divide on three questions. First, is Paul speaking of individuals or of groups and historical roles? Second, does “not of works” exclude faith as a condition of election, or only merit? Third, is God’s hardening a sovereign act that precedes human resistance, or a response to it? Thoughtful readers in both traditions have answered these questions differently for four centuries. The chapter rewards anyone who reads it slowly, with attention to its Old Testament quotations and to its place in Paul’s larger argument about the faithfulness of God.
