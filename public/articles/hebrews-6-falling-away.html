Hebrews 6: Can a Believer Fall Away?

The sharpest warning in Hebrews, read by both traditions: who is described in 6:4–6, what “impossible” means, and how God’s oath gives assurance.

About 7 min read

Hebrews 6:4–6 may be the most sobering passage in the New Testament. It speaks of people “who were once enlightened and tasted of the heavenly gift, and were made partakers of the Holy Spirit,” who “tasted the good word of God and the powers of the age to come,” and who “then fell away.” Of them the writer says, “it is impossible to renew them again to repentance; seeing they crucify the Son of God for themselves again, and put him to open shame” (6:4–6). Few texts have been more central to the debate over whether a true believer can finally fall away. This article explains how Arminian and Reformed readers understand the warning, and why both traditions insist it must be preached with full force.

The letter’s purpose

Hebrews was written to Christians under pressure, probably Jewish believers tempted to drift back from Christ to the old covenant forms of worship. The writer’s strategy is to show that Christ is greater than angels, Moses, the Levitical priesthood, and the old sacrifices, and then to warn his readers not to turn away from such a Savior. There are five major warning passages in the letter (2:1–4; 3:7–4:13; 5:11–6:12; 10:26–39; 12:14–29). Hebrews 6 is the third and perhaps the most severe.

Both traditions agree that the warnings are addressed to the church, that they are meant to be taken seriously, and that they are designed to produce perseverance. The question is what they imply about the people being warned.

The descriptions in verses 4–5

The writer lists five things true of the people in view. They were enlightened. They tasted the heavenly gift. They were made partakers of the Holy Spirit. They tasted the good word of God. They tasted the powers of the age to come.

Arminian interpreters argue that this is the language of Christian experience. “Enlightened” is used again in 10:32 for the readers' conversion. “Partakers of the Holy Spirit” uses the same word the writer uses in 3:14 when he says, “we have become partakers of Christ.” Wesley understood these phrases as describing those who had known the light of God’s love, the sweetness of forgiveness, and the witness of the Spirit. If these descriptions do not describe believers, Arminians ask, what words could the writer have used that would?

Reformed interpreters agree that the language is strong, and most do not treat it as describing people who never had any contact with grace. Calvin acknowledged that the passage speaks of illumination and of a real taste of God’s goodness. He argued, however, that God can give a measure of these blessings to people who are not regenerate. The parable of the soils provides the pattern: the seed on rocky ground springs up quickly and receives the word with joy, but it has no root and withers. Reformed readers note that none of the descriptions in Hebrews 6 uses the vocabulary of justification, regeneration, or adoption. “Tasted” may suggest something less than full eating, though Arminians reply that Hebrews 2:9 says Jesus would “taste of death for everyone,” which was hardly partial.

“And then fell away”

Arminian interpreters read “and then fell away” as describing a real possibility for real believers. The participle is not conditional in form; Wesley read it as a plain statement of fact rather than a supposition. Those who were once renewed can turn away so completely that renewal to repentance becomes impossible. Classical Arminians insist that this is not about ordinary sin, backsliding, or even serious failure. It describes a deliberate, settled repudiation of Christ after having known him, a repudiation that amounts to siding with those who crucified him.

Reformed interpreters have offered several readings. The most common, following Calvin, holds that the passage describes people who had a temporary, non-saving experience of grace and then turned decisively against the gospel. Their apostasy reveals that they were never truly regenerate. A second Reformed reading, found in some modern scholars, holds that the warning describes a real believer but functions as a means God uses to keep believers from falling. On this view, the warning is true, since apostasy would indeed be fatal, and God ensures that the elect heed it. A third view, held by some, takes the passage as hypothetical, but most Reformed scholars today find that reading too weak, since it seems to drain the warning of its force.

Why “impossible”?

The writer says it is impossible to renew such people to repentance. Arminian readers have usually taken this at face value: final apostasy, as distinct from ordinary sin, is irreversible. Some Arminian interpreters suggest that the impossibility lasts as long as the person continues to crucify Christ afresh, since the present participles can be read that way. Others hold that the condition is permanent.

Reformed readers also take the impossibility seriously. Calvin argued that those who fall away in this sense have been given over to a hardened mind, and do not come to repentance again. He added a pastoral note: if someone returns after a grievous fall, that return shows they had not committed the kind of apostasy Hebrews describes. Both traditions therefore agree that the warning should not be used to drive penitent sinners to despair. Anyone who wants to repent is not in the condition the passage describes.

The farming image and the “better things”

Verses 7–8 compare the readers to land that receives frequent rain. If it produces a useful crop, it is blessed; if it produces thorns and thistles, it is near to being cursed and its end is burning. Arminian readers see the image as describing the same people who could produce either outcome. Reformed readers see two kinds of ground, distinguished by what grows, with the difference in fruit revealing the difference in soil.

Verse 9 changes the tone: “But, beloved, we are persuaded of better things for you, and things that accompany salvation, even though we speak like this.” Reformed interpreters find this decisive. The writer distinguishes the people described in verses 4–6 from his readers, and he speaks of “things that accompany salvation,” which suggests that the experiences of verses 4–5 do not necessarily accompany salvation. Arminian interpreters see verse 9 as an expression of confidence that the readers will heed the warning, not as proof that the warning could not apply to them. The writer is confident because of their love and service, which he mentions in verse 10, and he urges them to show diligence “to the end” (6:11).

God’s oath and assurance

The chapter ends with one of the strongest statements of assurance in the New Testament. God, “being determined to show more abundantly to the heirs of the promise the immutability of his counsel, interposed with an oath” (6:17), so that believers have a hope that is “an anchor of the soul, a hope both sure and steadfast” (6:19).

Reformed readers see this as the counterweight to the warning. The same chapter that warns of apostasy assures the heirs of the promise that God’s purpose is unchangeable. Those who belong to God are kept by his oath. Arminian readers also find great comfort here. They read the assurance as applying to those who continue to hold fast to the hope set before them, and they note that the writer elsewhere says, “we have become partakers of Christ, if we hold the beginning of our confidence firm to the end” (3:14).

How each tradition preaches the passage

Arminian preachers use Hebrews 6 to call believers to watchfulness and perseverance, and to warn that turning away from Christ is a real danger for anyone. They also stress that the passage describes deliberate apostasy, not weakness, so struggling Christians should not fear that they have committed it. The remedy for the danger is to keep drawing near to Christ, the great high priest.

Reformed preachers use Hebrews 6 to warn professing Christians not to rest in religious experience without fruit. They urge self-examination and diligence, and they also point anxious believers to God’s oath and to Christ’s priestly intercession. The warning is one of the means by which God keeps his people, so the right response is not to argue it away but to heed it.

Common ground

Both traditions agree that Hebrews 6 must not be softened. Both agree that it describes a deliberate and decisive turning away from Christ rather than ordinary sin. Both agree that the readers are meant to respond by pressing on to maturity. Both agree that anyone who desires to repent may come to Christ. And both find in the same chapter a powerful assurance grounded in God’s unchangeable promise.

The heart of the disagreement

The disagreement concerns whether the people described in verses 4–6 were genuinely regenerate. If they were, the passage teaches that a believer can finally fall away, as Arminians hold. If they were not, it teaches that a person can come close to saving faith and then turn away, as many Reformed readers hold. A third option holds that the warning describes believers but serves as the means by which God preserves them. Each reading tries to honor both the severity of the warning and the assurance that follows it. Readers who take the letter as a whole will find that both elements are essential to its message: hold fast, because the One who promised is faithful.
