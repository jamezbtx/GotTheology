Ephesians 1: Chosen in Christ Before the Foundation of the World

Corporate election, personal election, and foreknowledge: how both traditions read Paul's great blessing in Ephesians 1:3–14.

About 7 min read

Ephesians 1:3–14 is a single sentence in Greek, a long burst of praise to God for what he has done in Christ. Within it Paul says that God “chose us in him before the foundation of the world” (1:4), that he “predestined us for adoption as children through Jesus Christ” (1:5), and that believers were “foreordained according to the purpose of him who does all things after the counsel of his will” (1:11). These phrases have made the passage a central text in the debate over election. This article looks at how Arminian and Reformed readers understand it, and at what both traditions agree the passage is for.

Praise before doctrine

The passage opens, “Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, who has blessed us with every spiritual blessing in the heavenly places in Christ” (1:3). Whatever Paul says about choosing and predestining, he says it as worship. Both traditions take this seriously. Reformed preachers often insist that election should humble believers and move them to thanks, since nothing in them prompted God’s love. Arminian preachers insist just as strongly that election, rightly understood, shows the generosity of a God who wants his people to be holy and to share in his life. Neither tradition thinks the passage was written to settle a debate. Both think the debate has to answer to the passage.

“In him”

The phrase “in Christ” or “in him” runs through the whole sentence. God blesses us in Christ, chooses us in him, predestines us through him, gives us grace in the Beloved, and grants redemption in him. How this phrase relates to election is one of the key questions.

Many Arminian interpreters read the election in Ephesians 1 as corporate and Christ-centered. Christ is the chosen one, the Beloved, and the church is chosen in him. God determined before creation that there would be a people in Christ, holy and blameless, and individuals come to share in that election when they are joined to Christ by faith. On this reading, “he chose us in him” describes the destiny of the body of believers rather than a list of individuals selected before they existed. Arminians point to Israel as a parallel: Israel was chosen as a nation, and individual Israelites shared in that election through membership in the people, a membership they could forfeit.

Reformed interpreters agree that election is in Christ, but they read the phrase as naming the one through whom chosen individuals are saved, not as replacing personal election with a corporate category. The “us” of verse 4 is the same “us” who receive redemption and forgiveness in verse 7, and that is personal. Reformed readers also point out that Paul says God chose us in Christ “before the foundation of the world,” before any individual could believe. In their view God chose particular people and appointed Christ as their mediator, so that they would be saved in him.

Chosen to be holy

Paul says God chose us “that we would be holy and without defect before him in love” (1:4). Both traditions note that election here is aimed at holiness. It is not a license for indifference.

Arminian readers often stress that this purpose clause shows the moral character of election. God’s choice is directed toward people becoming holy, and those who refuse holiness cannot claim the comfort of election. Wesley consistently tied assurance to a life being renewed by grace.

Reformed readers agree and add that the purpose clause shows that holiness is the result of election, not its cause. God did not choose people because they were holy; he chose them so that they would become holy. This is one reason Reformed theologians reject election based on foreseen faith or foreseen good works: the passage presents holiness as the goal God’s choice secures.

Predestined for adoption

Verse 5 says God “predestined us for adoption as children through Jesus Christ to himself, according to the good pleasure of his desire.” The word “predestined” means to mark out or determine beforehand.

Arminian interpreters note that the object of predestination here is adoption, a destiny. God determined in advance that those in Christ would become his children. Many Arminians understand this as God’s predetermined plan for believers, not as his predetermination of who will believe. Others, following Arminius and Wesley, connect predestination with foreknowledge, as in Romans 8:29: God predestines those whom he foreknows will believe. “The good pleasure of his desire” affirms that the plan comes from God’s free initiative, but Arminians deny that this rules out the condition of faith that God himself established.

Reformed interpreters read “according to the good pleasure of his desire” as the decisive phrase. The ground of predestination is God’s will, not anything foreseen in the creature. The Westminster Confession teaches that God chose his people “out of his mere free grace and love, without any foresight of faith, or good works” as a condition moving him to choose. Reformed readers see this passage as one of the main sources for that teaching.

All things after the counsel of his will

Verse 11 widens the frame. Believers were “foreordained according to the purpose of him who does all things after the counsel of his will.” Reformed readers take this as a statement of God’s comprehensive sovereignty. God’s purpose covers all things, including the salvation of his people, and nothing happens outside his counsel.

Arminian readers affirm that God works all things according to his counsel, but they deny that this requires God to determine every human choice. God’s counsel includes his decision to create free creatures and to save those who believe. Arminians often argue that a sovereign God can choose to accomplish his purposes through genuinely free human responses, and that this is a greater display of wisdom than determining each response directly.

Hearing, believing, and being sealed

Verse 13 turns to how the Ephesians came to share in these blessings: “In him you also, having heard the word of the truth, the Good News of your salvation—in whom, having also believed, you were sealed with the promised Holy Spirit.”

Arminian readers see the order of hearing, believing, and sealing as important. Faith is the point at which a person enters into Christ and so into the elect body. The sealing of the Spirit follows faith and marks believers as God’s own.

Reformed readers agree that faith is the means by which believers receive the blessings of election, and that the Spirit seals those who believe. They see no conflict between this verse and unconditional election. God chose his people before the foundation of the world and then brought them to faith through the preaching of the gospel. Ephesians 2:8, a few verses later, says believers are saved by grace through faith, “and that not of yourselves; it is the gift of God,” which many Reformed readers apply to the whole of salvation, including faith. Arminian readers often note that in Greek the word “that” does not match the gender of “faith,” and that the gift is salvation by grace as a whole. Many Reformed scholars accept the grammatical point and argue that faith is still included in the whole gift.

The pledge of the inheritance

Paul ends the sentence by calling the Spirit “a pledge of our inheritance, to the redemption of God’s own possession, to the praise of his glory” (1:14). Reformed readers take this as a strong statement of assurance: the Spirit is God’s guarantee that those he has sealed will receive the full inheritance. Arminian readers also take great comfort from the pledge, and many say that believers who remain in Christ have every reason for confidence. They hold, however, that other passages warn of the possibility of falling away, so the pledge must be read with those warnings.

What both traditions affirm

Despite their disagreements, Arminian and Reformed readers agree on a great deal in Ephesians 1. Both affirm that God’s plan of salvation is eternal and not an afterthought. Both affirm that salvation is entirely by grace and centered in Christ. Both affirm that election aims at holiness and leads to worship. Both affirm that faith is necessary and that the gospel must be heard and believed. Both affirm that the Holy Spirit seals believers and gives them a foretaste of their inheritance.

Where they differ

The disagreement comes down to the relationship between God’s choice and human faith. Reformed readers hold that God chose particular individuals unconditionally and that faith is the result of that choice. Arminian readers hold either that God chose the church as a body in Christ, with individuals sharing in that election by faith, or that God chose individuals on the basis of foreknown faith. Both readings try to account for every phrase in the passage. Readers who want to judge between them do well to ask how each reading handles “before the foundation of the world,” “in him,” “according to the good pleasure of his desire,” and “having also believed.” The passage is meant to lead to praise, and both traditions agree that the best reading is the one that leaves believers most amazed at the grace of God.
