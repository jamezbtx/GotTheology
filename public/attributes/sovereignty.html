Attribute of God

Sovereignty

God rules; nothing is finally outside his hand—both camps confess a King, and they part over whether that rule includes an unconditional decree of who will believe.

Sovereignty is God’s kingly right and actual rule. Scripture blesses the God who works all things according to the counsel of his will, the Father who draws, the Lord of the wedding feast, the blessed and only Sovereign. Arminian and Reformed Christians both reject a God who cannot save and a world of pure chance. They disagree whether sovereignty means that God determines the outcome of every human choice in salvation, or that he governs in ways that leave a grace-enabled refusal possible.

  • Ephesians 1:3–6Predestined according to the purpose of his will.

  • Genesis 25:19–34The older shall serve the younger—God’s purpose in the twins.
  • Matthew 22:1–14The king’s wedding feast: many called, few chosen.
  • 2 Timothy 1:8–14He saved us and called us… not because of our works but because of his own purpose and grace.
  • 1 Timothy 6:11–16The blessed and only Sovereign, the King of kings.
  • Romans 9:14–29He has mercy on whom he wills; who are you, O man, to answer back to God?
  • Romans 8:18–30He works all things together for good for those called according to his purpose.
  • Daniel 2:17–30He changes times and seasons; he removes kings and sets up kings (2:21).
  • Daniel 4:1–18The Most High rules the kingdom of men and gives it to whom he will (4:17).
  • Daniel 4:28–37He does according to his will; none can stay his hand (4:35).
Arminian

Arminians do not preach an anxious God. The Remonstrance’s first article is an eternal, unchangeable purpose in Christ. Wesley’s “decree” is real: life and death set before the sons of men. Sovereignty here is the sovereignty of a holy love that governs, provides, and judges—without making the human yes a puppet-string.

Arminius ascribes all good to grace and locates the dispute in irresistibility. God can rule a world in which preventing grace makes faith possible and refusal still blameworthy. Jacob and Esau, the wedding guests, and “all that the Father gives me” are read without collapsing them into unconditional individual election unto faith.

Pastoral Arminian notes keep two refusals: chance, and a sovereignty that authors sin or makes evangelistic pleas play-acting. The King is not less King because he has chosen to be resisted by some.

Reformed

Calvin’s providence is exhaustive: no random motion in the creatures. Westminster adds the classic safeguards—God is not the author of sin; creaturely will is not violated; second causes remain. Sovereignty is not tyranny; it is wise, holy counsel that includes the means.

In salvation this becomes unconditional election and effectual calling. “He predestined… according to the purpose of his will” and “no one can come unless the Father draws” are heard as the King’s actual drawing, not mere wooing. Spurgeon’s dust-mote is rhetorical, but the doctrine is that the small and the great sit inside one counsel.

Pastoral Reformed notes use sovereignty for sleep: the world is not finally in the devil’s hands or ours. They also warn against fatalism—the confession that second causes are “established” means prayer, preaching, and choice matter as the ordained way. The fork is whether that counsel includes a resistible mode of saving grace.

Key contrast Both confess that God rules. Arminians typically describe a sovereignty that eternally purposes to save believers and governs without irresistible determination of the will; the Reformed typically describe a sovereignty that ordains whatsoever comes to pass—including who will be effectually drawn—without making God the author of sin.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the rule of God—so each camp speaks in its own voice.

Arminian Notable voices

“Yea, the decree is past; and so it was before the foundation of the world. But what decree? Even this: “I will set before the sons of men life and death, blessing and cursing.” And the soul that chooseth life shall live, as the soul that chooseth death shall die.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“God, by an eternal, unchangeable purpose in Jesus Christ his Son, before the foundation of the world, hath determined… to save in Christ… those who, through the grace of the Holy Ghost, shall believe on this his Son Jesus.”
The Remonstrance · Article I (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“There is no random power, or agency, or motion in the creatures, who are so governed by the secret counsel of God, that nothing happens but what he has knowingly and willingly decreed.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 1.16.3
“God from all eternity, did, by the most wise and holy counsel of His own will, freely, and unchangeably ordain whatsoever comes to pass: yet so, as thereby neither is God the author of sin, nor is violence offered to the will of the creatures; nor is the liberty or contingency of second causes taken away, but rather established.”
Westminster Confession of Faith · Chapter 3.1
“I believe that every particle of dust that dances in the sunbeam does not move an atom more or less than God wishes—that every particle of spray that dashes against the steamboat has its orbit, as well as the sun in the heavens.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “God’s Providence” (sermon)

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
